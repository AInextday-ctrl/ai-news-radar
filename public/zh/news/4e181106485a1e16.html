Bloomberg 权威实录

Five personnel at US Cyber Command died by suicide over a span of roughly five weeks last summer, and the investigators, lawmakers, and former operators who have been grappling with those deaths since say the cause is not simply stress — it is a structural design flaw in how America's digital warfare machine is staffed, paced, and supported.

The congressional response to the crisis has handed lawmakers a deadline: act through the fiscal year 2027 National Defense Authorization Act, targeted for a December vote, or let the most concrete funding fix on the table lapse.Between early June and early July 2026, at least five people working in or closely affiliated with Cyber Command took their own lives, according to internal military communications, public records, and sources cited by Bloomberg News, which first reported the cluster in August.

Army Gen. Joshua Rudd, who leads both Cyber Command and the National Security Agency in a dual-hat arrangement from their shared Fort Meade, Maryland headquarters, acknowledged three of the deaths in an all-hands email to personnel in June. Public records confirmed two more in the same period.The cluster arrived with the evidence already compiled and with a legislative mandate that had already been passed but not acted on.

A study commissioned under the FY2024 NDAA specifically examined the "occupational resiliency" of the Cyber Mission Force and found that resources available to cyber personnel were "inadequate and may jeopardize long-term personnel readiness." No implementation plan from that study has been delivered to Congress.What "Deployed-in-Place" Actually MeansThe phrase that appears most often in expert discussions of this crisis is "deployed-in-place," and it describes something the military's existing resilience infrastructure was not built to handle.

When conventional forces deploy — to Iraq, Afghanistan, or a carrier strike group in the Arabian Sea — they enter a defined cycle: forward deployment, followed by a mandated dwell period at home before the next rotation. Policies governing that cycle dictate minimum rest ratios and trigger the care resources that prevent the psychological accumulation the military calls burnout.

The FY2026 NDAA drone operator study mandate reflects the growing recognition that similar protections are not reaching personnel in continuous remote-warfare roles.Cyber operators at Fort Meade have no such cycle. They go to work in an office building in suburban Maryland, run offensive operations against Iran's command-and-control networks, conduct defensive hunts across Department of Defense infrastructure, support counter-narcotics missions along the southern border — and then drive home.

The next morning, they return and do it again. There is no geographic break that triggers existing rotation policies, no "dwell period" in any legal sense, no defined off-ramp from the operational environment that produces burnout.Andrew Schoka, a former Army officer who served in multiple roles at Cyber Command, put the structural failure plainly.

"Burning out operators is a direct result of leadership and staff that lack technical expertise in the cyber domain to properly plan, employ, and prioritize use of low-density work roles," he said. "There's always new priorities and new crises to deal with, and never enough qualified personnel to support every requirement."The pace has been accelerating.

Gen. Rudd told the House Armed Services Committee in April 2026 that the command conducted 8,000 missions in 2025 alone — a 25 percent increase over the prior year — and that the number was expected to rise further through the rest of 2026. Cyber Command participated in Operation Absolute Resolve, which resulted in the capture of Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro in January, and has been continuously engaged in Operation Epic Fury in support of US and Israeli military operations against Iran since late February.

On top of the active-conflict missions, the command has taken on expanded Southern border enforcement and counter-narcotics work.This Crisis Was Predicted — by the Drone Operator ResearchThe summer cluster at Cyber Command did not arrive without precedent or warning. Military psychologists and researchers who studied drone operator burnout across the Global War on Terrorism years documented the same mechanism — what some researchers now call "geographic whiplash" — that appears to be driving the cyber crisis.Drone pilots and sensor operators run lethal remote-warfare missions from ground control stations in Nevada, then clock out and drive to grocery stores.

Research published in the Journal of Mental Health and Clinical Psychology found that nearly half of RPA operators — approximately 46 to 48 percent — experienced psychological consequences affecting their personal or work life. Air Force studies documented elevated burnout, distress, and sleep disruption across RPA career fields specifically because of the absence of geographic distance that, for most combat veterans, serves as a psychological buffer between the violence of their work and the rhythms of their civilian lives.Psychologists describe the core injury not as PTSD in the traditional sense but as moral injury — the damage done to a person's values when they perpetrate acts, witness suffering, or fail to prevent harm in ways that violate their own ethical foundations.

The FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act, signed into law in December 2025, mandated a formal Pentagon study covering drone operator psychological health conditions — including PTSD, depression, anxiety, burnout, and moral injury. A Task and Purpose report described the study's scope as covering not just pilots but also the intelligence analysts and targeting personnel who work alongside them.Cyber operators face the same geographic whiplash as drone operators — with the added dimension of continuous, not episodic, operational engagement.

The FY2027 NDAA House version, passed 216 to 212 in July 2026, includes a Cyber Mission Force mental health expansion and a study of "remote-aircraft-operator psychological effects." The research framework exists. What has not yet been built is the care infrastructure that the research implies is necessary.The Clearance Bottleneck: Why Two Therapists Serve ThousandsThe most specific structural obstacle Rep.

Sarah Elfreth (D-MD), a House Armed Services Committee member whose district borders Fort Meade, identified is not funding — it is clearances. Every mental health provider who works with Cyber Command personnel must hold a security clearance, because those providers work in classified environments and hear classified information.

The pipeline for cleared therapists is thin, slow, and almost entirely divorced from the normal healthcare labor market. A Leidos clearance and mental health study found that significant levels of mistrust and misconception persist across the intelligence community workforce, compounding the structural shortage.As of May 2026 — before the suicide cluster was publicly reported — Cyber Command unveiled a partnership with Fort Meade's Kimbrough Ambulatory Care Center that embedded two cleared mental health providers directly in CYBERCOM spaces.

The Kimbrough embedded provider program was designed to eliminate the logistical barrier that had been suppressing demand: under the prior model, seeking care required a three-hour round trip to the main base clinic, a cost in time that, according to one of the new embedded providers, created active pressure for operators not to seek help.The maximum capacity of the new program is five embedded providers.

Cyber Command's Cyber Mission Force alone comprises roughly 6,000 personnel drawn from all military branches, plus a larger civilian and contractor workforce. Five cleared therapists for thousands of operators running continuous wartime missions is not a solution — it is evidence of the gap's scale.Rep.

Elfreth told The Record that "we have a dearth of providers and access" at the command.The FY2026 NDAA (P.L. 119-60), signed in December 2025, directed the Defense Department to ensure behavioral health specialists with security clearances are dispatched to Cyber Command and the Cyber Mission Force.

That mandate was in place before the deaths. The question Congress is now asking is whether the cleared-provider mandate was adequately resourced and implemented — and, if not, what the FY2027 NDAA can do to enforce it.What Congress Is Considering — and What It Cannot FundCongressional aides working on the FY2027 NDAA have been briefed on the cluster of deaths, and more briefings are expected.

The bill itself has already moved: the House passed its version in July, and the Senate Armed Services Committee has completed its markup. The two versions must now be reconciled in conference, with staff negotiations expected to continue during the campaign recess and a final bill targeted for a December floor vote.Rep.

Elfreth said she intends to push for NDAA language expanding access to cleared mental health providers. She is also attempting to redefine how the Pentagon classifies a suicide cluster to explicitly include civilian employees — a change that would extend the mandatory intervention protocols that currently cover uniformed personnel but do not automatically reach the civilian workforce at Fort Meade.

She described that effort as "a bit of a Hail Mary."A second legislative vehicle is the government funding bill. Congress passed a short-term continuing resolution extending funding through December 11, and lawmakers will likely need to pass another stopgap before year's end — potentially creating a narrow additional window.The most concrete funding item on the table is the $11 million "High Performance Team Training" line item that the House Appropriations Committee included in its FY2027 defense spending bill.

Cyber Command listed the program second on its own "Unfunded Priority List" transmitted to Congress. The document states that the training provides "cognitive, psychological, physiological, and spiritual sustainment for high-tempo cyber operations" and adds that without funding, "burnout and force degradation will continue."But the same congressional source who confirmed the political will to act was direct about the difficulty of the math.

"Policy-wise I do think there are some 'hooks' to do stuff," the source told The Record, speaking anonymously because they were not authorized to discuss the matter publicly. "The problem is, I don't think there's a hook to do anything funding-wise."A Study That Found Problems — and Produced No PlanThe cluster arrived with the evidence already compiled.

A study commissioned under the FY2024 NDAA specifically examined the "occupational resiliency" of the Cyber Mission Force and found that resources available to CMF personnel were "inadequate and may jeopardize long-term personnel readiness, posing unnecessary risk to the mission effectiveness of the CMF." The report recommended that senior command officials establish a framework for prevention and treatment programs.No implementation plan from that study has been delivered to Congress.

That gap, congressional sources told The Record, could become a legislative hook — demanding that implementation plan, with a deadline, through the FY2027 NDAA — without requiring new appropriations. It is, in effect, oversight pressure: prove you have a plan, or explain why you do not.Whether oversight language translates into the cleared providers, rest cycles, and structural changes that experts say the force actually needs is a separate question — and one the December deadline will begin, but not finish, answering.The Crisis Extends to the NSAThe summer deaths have reverberated beyond Cyber Command.

The NSA, which shares the Fort Meade campus and whose personnel frequently work alongside Cyber Command operators, has also experienced suicides in the same period. NSA Deputy Director Tim Kosiba was summoned to the White House to discuss the situation, though sources with direct knowledge of the meeting said the conversation focused primarily on Cyber Command rather than the spy agency.The senior Democrats on the Senate and House Intelligence committees, Sen.

Mark Warner (D-VA) and Rep. Jim Himes (D-CT), sent queries to NSA asking about the resources available to its personnel. As of The Record's September 17 reporting, neither had received a response.Warner stated that "these losses are a tragedy" and that intelligence community workforce health was his highest priority, adding he would work with Fort Meade to ensure personnel have needed support and resources.Cyber Command and the NSA both declined to comment to The Record.Is Mental Health Support Enough Without Structural Change?Schoka's analysis lands on a harder question than Congress appears to be asking.

Adding more cleared therapists and better access — necessary as those steps are — does not address what he identifies as the root cause: leadership without sufficient technical expertise in the cyber domain to make sound decisions about when to push operators hard, when to pull back, and how to rotate responsibility across a workforce that is chronically understaffed relative to the demands placed on it.Schoka told The Record directly that "adding more mental health and support resources is an important step, but it doesn't address the root cause of the problem."The parallel to the early Global War on Terror years is instructive.

When drone operator suicides drew scrutiny in the 2010s, the initial response was also primarily therapeutic — crisis counseling, peer support programs, mandatory check-ins. It took years of research and advocacy before the military began to examine whether the operational model itself needed to change: rest cycles, mission rotation, limits on continuous engagement time.

That structural reckoning has still not fully arrived for drone operators. For cyber operators, it has not yet begun.What the FY2027 NDAA will most likely produce is a better-resourced version of the existing framework — more cleared providers, more attention, more mandatory briefings to Gen.

Rudd. Whether any provision requires the command to define rest cycles, establish dwell-time ratios, or confront the "deployed-in-place" design at its core is, as of this writing, not among the items on the legislative table.Frequently Asked QuestionsWhy can't Cyber Command just hire more therapists from the civilian sector?Every mental health provider who serves Cyber Command personnel must hold a security clearance, because operators work in classified environments and discuss classified information in sessions.

The clearance process takes twelve months or more and requires the provider to meet national security eligibility standards that most healthcare professionals never pursue. The Kimbrough partnership embedded providers in CYBERCOM spaces in May 2026 represents the practical ceiling in the near term — the program can scale to a maximum of five providers.

For a workforce of thousands running continuous wartime operations, that is structurally insufficient regardless of budget.What is the "deployed-in-place" problem, and why does it matter for mental health?When conventional forces deploy to a forward operating base, DoD policy defines a minimum dwell period — time at home before the next rotation — that triggers rest-and-recovery protocols and mental health support resources.

Cyber operators at Fort Meade never formally "deploy" in this legal sense, so no rotation clock starts, no dwell ratio applies, and no mandatory rest period kicks in. They run continuous wartime operations — including offensive missions against Iran and Venezuela in 2026 — from the same building every day, without the geographic break that existing resilience frameworks depend on.

Research on drone operators, who face a similar "geographic whiplash," found that roughly 46 to 48 percent experience psychological consequences affecting their personal or work lives.What will the FY2027 NDAA actually do for Cyber Command personnel, and when?The House-passed FY2027 NDAA includes a Cyber Mission Force mental health expansion and a study of remote-aircraft-operator psychological effects.

The Senate version, which must be reconciled with the House bill in conference, includes broader mental health staffing and wait-time assessment provisions. A final bill is expected before the December 11 continuing resolution expiration and a December floor vote. The legislation is likely to authorize more cleared mental health providers for CYBERCOM and mandate implementation of the FY2024 NDAA occupational resiliency study's recommendations.

Whether Congress also addresses the structural cause — the absence of defined rest cycles for a permanently deployed-in-place force — is not yet settled.What can Cyber Command personnel do right now if they are struggling?Call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) — veterans and active service members can press 1 after dialing for dedicated military support.

Chat is also available at 988lifeline.org. The embedded mental health providers at the Kimbrough Ambulatory Care Center partnership are available in CYBERCOM spaces; supervisors should have current contact information. DoD policy explicitly states that seeking mental health treatment does not constitute grounds to revoke or deny a security clearance, and the embedded model was specifically designed to eliminate the three-hour round-trip to the main base clinic that had been discouraging operators from seeking care.If you or someone you know is struggling, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available by call or text at 988.
