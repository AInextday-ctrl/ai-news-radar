By most measures, it’s an awful time to take an AI data center business public. That isn’t stopping Josh Payne.

As founder and chief executive of Nscale, the 32-year-old Australian turned a small Bitcoin miner into an infrastructure provider at the center of the artificial intelligence boom in less than three years. Along the way he signed up blue-chip firms as customers, lured tech luminaries to Nscale’s board and attracted nearly $10 billion in equity funding and debt, despite being virtually unknown on Wall Street or in Silicon Valley.

Now Payne must convince public market investors that his London-based neocloud – a business that borrows heavily to build data centers, purchase chips and rent out computing power – is worth betting on at a tumultuous moment.

Politicians everywhere are growing more concerned about artificial intelligence’s risks. Investors are worried the technology won’t deliver on grand revenue promises. A planned initial public offering for Firmus Grid Ltd., an Australian developer of AI computing facilities, imploded earlier this month. A South Korean neocloud had a rocky trading debut. Most alarming perhaps, one of the largest firms in the sector, CoreWeave Inc., watched its credit risk soar, triggering a broader market selloff.

Elon Musk added to the concerns Sunday with comments that the debt markets are shut for many AI infrastructure companies. “That means the 200+ neoclouds are going to see guppy-level death and consolidation,” he wrote on X, the social media platform he owns. “Some will make it through, but not many.”

Payne is undaunted. Nscale, which filed its IPO paperwork in mid-September, is proceeding with the plans for a listing and targeting a $30 billion valuation in the face of some investor pushback, according to people familiar with the discussions. Bloomberg reported the startup is in active talks to sign Anthropic PBC, an existing customer, to another deal worth as much as $45 billion — even though Nscale had delivered less than 1% of the overall target capacity it had booked as of August.

“He’s a tremendous dealmaker,” said Steve Bellotti, a managing partner with Blue Sky Capital and early Nscale investor. The company declined to comment, citing the quiet period ahead of its listing.

Still, Payne brings an atypical background to the high-profile role, with no conventional corporate experience or college degree, and a history of false starts and dead ends. In its S-1 filing, Nscale pointed to Payne’s “experience and success in the data center industry.” But the CEO’s prior career mostly includes an unprofitable Bitcoin mining operation, a failed special purpose acquisition vehicle and investment firms with no identifiable portfolio or track record. In the past decade, Payne has co-founded at least six companies, and all, save Nscale, have effectively folded. In several cases, he brought on experienced hands, then swiftly moved onto the next venture.

With Nscale, Payne benefited from picking the right place in the tech industry at precisely the right time. Several bitcoin miners, including CoreWeave and Core Scientific Inc., had successfully pivoted from crypto to data centers as AI fever hit after ChatGPT’s debut. At least one of Payne’s backers made money from CoreWeave and figured pattern-matching with Nscale was a promising opportunity too.

Payne’s boosters describe him as an unusually gifted salesman and entrepreneur, skills that enabled him to sign up Anthropic and Microsoft Corp. as anchor customers, and convince Nvidia Corp. to join as a major shareholder. He also recruited several high-profile directors, including Sheryl Sandberg, who compared Payne to Mark Zuckerberg.

Those alliances are “testament to his vision and his ability to communicate,” said Barry Kupferberg, an Nscale investor and managing partner for Barkers Point Capital Advisors LLC. “A lot of investors think about the business as Josh.”

The soft-spoken executive has revealed little about his background, management style and personal life. Payne moved to London in 2023, but is not immersed in the city’s tight-knit scene of AI startups and scientists. After forming Nscale in 2024, he pitched the nascent company as a British AI champion, but quickly turned the company’s attention to the US after facing hurdles from high energy prices and red tape on computing sites in the UK.

As Nscale preps an IPO, data centers are getting more and more politically toxic. “It’s not very favorable right now,” said Dave Mazza, CEO of Roundhill Investments, which has an exchange-traded fund for neoclouds. Nscale has “a strong fundamental story,” he added. “It comes down to: How much am I going to be willing to pay for those positives?”

Payne previously told Time magazine he sees Nscale becoming a “$1 trillion hyperscaler.” Yet he doesn’t have the pedigree of the leaders of the trillion-dollar tech giants, or even his largest rivals – CoreWeave’s chief worked as a commodities trader; the CEO of neocloud Nebius Group NV spent years running Russia’s largest internet firm. At times, Payne comes off more as an evangelist than a technical savant. Some Nscale employees, while working to deal with hiccups at its data centers, recalled their CEO jumping on video calls to rally the troops, with messages of, “Let’s f— go.” They asked not to be identified discussing private meetings.

Last September, Nscale posted a video of Jensen Huang, tech’s rockstar CEO, gifting a signed bottle of Johnnie Walker whisky to Payne in London. “I’ve never seen a startup take off like that before,” Nvidia’s CEO told him. Payne replied with a “let’s go,” but added little else in response before fumbling a handshake.

“Is he the smartest guy in the room? No. Is he one of the most cunning and savvy? Absolutely,” Bellotti said of Payne.

While Nscale told prospective investors it’s signed $103 billion in contracts, the company has built a tiny fraction of the data center capacity needed to serve those customers. As of August, Nscale had delivered 55 megawatts out of a potential 10 gigawatts, according to its IPO filing. Bloomberg reported that Nscale’s sites have been plagued by serious delays, safety violations and operational challenges. The bulk of Nscale’s contracts rely on two customers, and one of them, Microsoft, recently withdrew from a project in the UK, months after OpenAI did something similar.

Payne wrote a memo to rally his staff last week in the wake of the AI turmoil. “Adversity is a constant on the path to building something truly great,” he said, according to a copy viewed by Bloomberg.

According to Nscale filings, Payne owns at least 5% of the company’s equity through a trust he controls. He stands to reap another 3.47% of its shares in awards with the IPO, including a grant worth around $350 million.

Payne took an unusual path to the tech spotlight. His first job was in a coal mine. After high school, he manned crane rigs for a mining operation near his hometown of Newcastle, north of Sydney, reading books on entrepreneurship and business during his downtime, he later told the New York Times.

He also got interested in crypto. He briefly sold equipment for Bitmain Technologies Ltd., the Chinese cryptocurrency mining firm. In his early twenties, he set up a firm for recruiting in the construction industry and another for investing in renewable energy, according to Nscale's filing. Yet Bitcoin remained a hobby. In his first ever post on LinkedIn, in 2018, he noted he was “urgently looking” for sellers for a large amount of the currency.

In 2019, Payne co-founded Envest Group as a brokerage and investment firm. It was incorporated in Malaysia as a tax-favorable region, according to his partner Bayley Blake. He said the pair looked at various projects, including a microchip venture, but Payne was chiefly interested in Bitcoin mining efforts. “He kept falling back to that,” Blake said.

Payne spent his time searching for support for a crypto exchange and other deals, but Blake recalled that his partner avoided in-person meetings, fearful that the family offices or investors would judge him as too junior. “He was always the youngest guy in the room,” Blake said. Their company dissolved once the pandemic hit. Payne quickly moved on. He started Arkon Energy, a company to build facilities for mining Bitcoin with a friend who had worked in oil and gas.

Kupferberg, a banking veteran, met Payne through a mutual contact while hunting for mining operations as crypto prices were soaring. After backing Arkon Energy, Kupferberg remembers “lots of nos” from other investors. Eventually, the startup secured a check from Kestrel0x1, a small fund backing web3 and blockchain projects. Noam Perski, a partner there, said he was drawn to Payne’s “deeply impressive” grasp of supply chains.

After scouting a potential site for mining equipment in Norway, Payne booked an immediate flight from Sydney to Oslo. That location didn’t pan out, but Payne found another property, in Glomfjord, which would become an early data center for Nscale.

Arkon Energy was not a success. Losses reached five times higher than revenue, and it defaulted on loans in late 2023. Those loans were repaid after Nscale’s 2025 financing to Sandton Capital Partners, which participated in the Nscale round. Rael Nurick, a co-founder at the investment firm who sits on Nscale's board, didn't respond to requests for comment. Kupferberg said Arkon Energy paid back his loans with interest.

Yet the business gave Payne a loyal business network and crypto mining real estate – sites in Glomfjord, Ohio and Texas – that he would convert to AI computing facilities as the enthusiasm for Bitcoin waned. Prices for the digital currency hit record lows in January of 2024.

Payne incorporated Nscale four months later. Arkon Energy investors moved shares to the new business and stayed close. Philippe Sachs, Perski’s childhood friend and a fellow Kestrel0x1 investor, joined Nscale in 2025 as chief business officer. He soon accompanied Payne on trips to court customers and financiers at the World Economic Forum in Davos. Perski also worked at Palantir Technologies Inc., where Payne recruited his first chief operating officer.

Despite his quick ascent in business circles, Payne has disclosed little about himself publicly, or even to associates. After some probing, a few shared he likes snowboarding and playing guitar. Kupferberg called him “a very normal guy” who people want to join for a beer.

Drinking partners might need to be willing to talk data centers. Before a recent birthday party, Perski had to be intentional about who he sat Payne next to since the Nscale CEO tends to keep conversation to his job. “He’s a private person,” said Perski. “The dude just works.”

That mystery extends to Payne’s career before Nscale. In its S-1, the company told investors that Payne advised businesses in renewable energy and tech, listing five firms where he worked before starting Arkon Energy. However, there are no discernable investments that Envest made in these sectors. Similarly, there are limited signs of operations or staff at Payne Capital, where Nscale said its CEO led “deal structure and negotiations.” Multiple investors who funded Payne at Arkon Energy and Nscale said they knew nothing about his prior work.

Blake, his early business partner, said neither had much experience before starting their brokerage. Payne is “just a super-fast learner,” he added. “He’s very determined.”

While he was running Arkon Energy, Payne was also co-founder and chief operating officer for Battery Future Acquisition Corp., a firm that listed on the NYSE, via a SPAC, in 2021, to acquire companies working on critical minerals for batteries. Greg Martyr, its CEO, said Payne pitched in his own funds and courted investors. “He's an absolute bloody terrier,” Martyr said. In its S-1, that firm touted Payne's five years of “investment banking and corporate advisory experience.” It delisted three years later.

The firm dissolved due to the “bad downdraft” in the SPAC market, according to Kupferberg. “Nothing particular to Josh,” he said.

By then, Payne was spending time on his next venture. “You might say he saw the writing on the wall and started drifting away,” Martyr recalled.

Soon after forming Nscale, Payne began recruiting seasoned hands. He hired from small startups in Europe, Microsoft, Amazon Web Services and data center company Iron Mountain Inc. Payne signed up Dell Technologies Inc. as an investor and partner. And he added Norwegian energy firm Aker ASA as a major shareholder after sending multiple cold messages to its CEO, Øyvind Eriksen, who later joined the board.

Aker declined to comment on Nscale and Payne.

Payne also overhauled most of Nscale’s entire board. Sandberg told Bloomberg TV that she initially declined Payne’s offer to join the board but was persuaded after he wrote a lengthy letter to his management team, which she likened to Facebook’s founder. In addition to Sandberg, he brought on former Meta Platforms Inc. executive Nick Clegg and veterans of Comcast, Yahoo, Snowflake and OpenAI – marquee figures that are rare for a two-year old startup.

Some investors cited Payne’s unorthodox background career as a reason that he’s fearless about breaking any norms in his recruiting. “He’s not going to lose face if Jensen [Huang] doesn’t answer,” said Perski. “This why not thing – it’s always there.”

Greg Hersch, founder of wealth management firm Florence Capital, said he backed Nscale, in part, because of a “very impressive team of experts” Payne had assembled.

Hersch, like other investors, saw a chance for a repeat hit. He had previously funded CoreWeave, which went public in early 2025 and saw its shares surge about 87% in its first year. But the company had little footprint outside of the US. Initially, Nscale positioned itself as a CoreWeave rival that could expand from Europe, a fertile ground.

In meetings with Payne, Hersch said he “was seeing and hearing the same thing” as CoreWeave’s leaders. “It felt like I’ve seen this movie before,” he added.

He said this in late September, before CoreWeave’s credit collapse and the broader neocloud troubles. In a later email, Hersch said that he maintains his positive outlook on Nscale, “as long as access to capital is available.”

In his memo, Payne reassured staff that Nscale has “insatiable” demand and “a robust capital structure” behind the company. “The numbers speak for themselves,” he wrote in the note.
