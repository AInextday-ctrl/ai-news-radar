高频交易巨头跨界布局AI算力

✍️ 记者 / 供稿: Bloomberg News

Jane Street等高频交易公司正投入AI初创企业及自建数据中心，利用算力优势强化市场博弈

  • 【核心事件】高频交易商（如Jane Street）在AI基础设施与金融市场：投资AI芯片与初创企业，并自建数据中心
  • 【起因与机理】在微秒级交易中抢占算力先机，建立竞争壁垒
  • 【关键数据与实测】At a $30 billion valuation, the company is priced against a future infrastructure position that must still be built.。

📰 深度报道原文实录

Bloomberg 权威实录

When Bloomberg reported on September 3 that Denver-based AI infrastructure company Crusoe had closed a $3 billion Series F at a $30 billion post-money valuation, the headline figure was striking but not entirely new — TechTimes had previously reported those talks in July. What was new, and what most coverage buried past the funding announcement, was the buyer attached to Crusoe's largest customer contract: Jane Street Group, the quantitative trading firm that just committed approximately $13 billion in cloud spending to Crusoe over five years.

That same week, Jane Street also led a FluidStack $1.5 billion equity round in rival AI infrastructure startup FluidStack. Combined with the Jane Street $6 billion cloud commitment and $1 billion equity stake Jane Street took in CoreWeave in April, a firm that has never sold an AI product to anyone now holds more contracted AI compute than most frontier model developers will ever consume — and has become the AI infrastructure sector's most consequential non-hyperscaler backer.Jane Street Rewrites the AI Infrastructure Demand PictureFor most of the AI infrastructure boom, the story of demand has been a story of model developers and hyperscalers: OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft fighting for scarce GPU capacity to train progressively larger models.

Jane Street's spending pattern in 2026 scrambles that picture entirely.The trading firm's AI infrastructure commitments now add up to roughly $21.5 billion: $6 billion in cloud capacity from CoreWeave, $1 billion in CoreWeave equity at $109 per share, approximately $13 billion in Crusoe cloud capacity over five years, and the $1.5 billion FluidStack equity investment it led this week.

That total, confirmed across TechCrunch's Series F reporting, CoreWeave's official press release, and Crunchbase's weekly funding roundup, exceeds the GPU infrastructure commitments of most AI laboratories — companies that exist entirely to build AI — because Jane Street has reasons to need compute that are structurally different from a model developer's.Quantitative trading is among the most computationally intensive enterprises on earth.

Firms like Jane Street use machine learning to generate trading signals, simulate market scenarios, optimize execution algorithms, and stress-test portfolio models in real time, simultaneously, at a scale that compounds the appetite for compute with each new generation of models.

The key distinction from a standard AI lab is competitive cadence: a frontier model laboratory trains a new model every few months; a quantitative trading firm refines and re-trains models daily or hourly, with each marginal improvement in prediction accuracy translating directly into captured profit.

Faster iteration means more compute consumed, and dedicated GPU clusters mean that iteration is never throttled by shared cloud queues or spot-market pricing.CoreWeave's official press release announcing the April deal was explicit about this dynamic: CoreWeave was providing Jane Street with access to next-generation NVIDIA Vera Rubin chips — at the time among the first commercially available — along with dedicated connectivity and custom storage configurations.

In quantitative trading, access to hardware before competitors do is not a cost-optimization decision. It is a structural competitive advantage.Crusoe's Series F: From Talks to Closed, With Named InvestorsThe round that closed is structurally identical to what Bloomberg's earlier reporting had described: $3 billion, approximately $30 billion post-money valuation, pre-IPO framing.

The new detail is confirmation of its leadership: Atreides Management and Valor Equity Partners co-led the financing, with Mubadala Capital — the alternative asset management arm of Abu Dhabi's $302 billion sovereign wealth fund Mubadala Investment Company — also participating, per TechCrunch's Series F reporting.

The "pre-IPO" designation carries its own signal: such structures typically imply a public listing within 12 to 24 months.Atreides Management is a Boston-based investment firm run by Gavin Baker, known for concentrated bets on technology companies at the growth stage. It has co-invested with Valor Equity Partners through the dedicated Valor Atreides AI Fund, a structure that pooled capital specifically for AI infrastructure and computing companies.

Valor Equity Partners, led by Antonio Gracias, had already backed Crusoe in its Series E round in October 2025; the firm's re-investment at a tripled valuation signals conviction in the company's trajectory rather than a forced-hand continuation.Mubadala Capital's participation connects the round to a global pattern of sovereign capital accelerating into AI infrastructure.

Abu Dhabi's sovereign wealth ecosystem — which collectively manages approximately $1.7 trillion across ADIA, Mubadala, and ADQ — has positioned AI infrastructure as a core strategic allocation, not merely a financial bet. Mubadala had participated in Crusoe's Series E as well, making this its second consecutive commitment to the same company.The $30 billion post-money valuation implies a pre-money figure of roughly $27 billion, representing a nearly threefold increase from the $10 billion valuation established in October 2025, per TechCrunch's Series F reporting.

Total equity raised by Crusoe now exceeds $7 billion according to Crunchbase, positioning it as the most heavily capitalized private AI infrastructure company not yet publicly traded.From Flared Gas to AI Factories: How Crusoe Built the Plumbing Finance Now Depends OnCrusoe's founding story — capturing natural gas flared and wasted at remote oil wells, converting it to cheap electricity, running compute on that power — is by now familiar to anyone following the neocloud sector.

What is less widely understood is why the company's current architecture makes it structurally better positioned than most of its neocloud rivals to capture the kind of large, single-tenant, dedicated-compute contracts that Jane Street's $13 billion agreement represents.Standard GPU cloud operators — the category often called neoclouds — acquire or lease GPU hardware, rack it at colocation facilities, and sell compute time at rates that can run 40 to 85 percent below hyperscaler pricing for equivalent silicon.

That model has significant appeal to buyers but narrow economics for operators: prior TechTimes Crusoe analysis cited McKinsey's estimate that bare-metal-as-a-service businesses post gross profit margins of only 14 to 16 percent after labor, power, and depreciation, lower than many non-technology retailers.

Each new chip generation cuts rental prices for older hardware by roughly half within five years, and revenue often concentrates in one or two customers.Crusoe's model is structurally different. The company owns or co-develops the land, the power generation assets, and the data center buildings at each campus, then layers GPU hardware and cloud software on top.

It manufactures prefabricated power and data center modules at its own facilities in Colorado, Oklahoma, and Louisiana — what it calls its Spark Factory — and ships them ready for field installation, as detailed in Crusoe's June 2026 contracted capacity announcement. Rather than waiting for sequential power-then-construction negotiations, the company co-develops both tracks simultaneously, compressing the time from site selection to energized capacity.At its flagship 1.2-gigawatt campus on the Lancium Clean Campus in Abilene, Texas, built for Oracle and serving OpenAI's Stargate project, the physical campus went from groundbreaking to the first buildings energized in under one year, as prior TechTimes Crusoe coverage documented.

The power architecture there illustrates the cost logic: the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality permitted Crusoe to operate ten simple-cycle turbines providing approximately 360 megawatts of behind-the-meter generation — electricity produced on-site and consumed on-site, authorized exclusively for the data center, bypassing the multi-year interconnection queue that confronts any grid-connected competitor.

Because energy represents roughly 60 percent of AI data center operating expenses, structural access to cheaper dedicated power is not a pricing advantage at the margin — it is a business model that can be sustained where others cannot.The campus design itself reflects the density requirements of modern AI hardware.

A single rack of NVIDIA H100 SXM5 GPUs draws approximately 56 kilowatts at full load; the newer GB200 NVL72 racks push well above that. Legacy data center racks drew two to four kilowatts. That factor-of-thirty-five-to-seventy density increase requires a completely different approach to power distribution, cooling, and building layout.

CEO Chase Lochmiller has described the design as a printed circuit board at campus scale: GPU compute wings radiating outward from a central core housing network and storage infrastructure. All GPUs at a campus must communicate as a cohesive unit, enabling the distributed gradient synchronization that large model training requires across thousands of nodes simultaneously.In March 2026, Crusoe broke ground on a second adjacent 900-megawatt campus in Abilene for Microsoft, per Crusoe's own announcement, bringing the total planned Abilene footprint to approximately 2.1 gigawatts.

As of June 2026, the company had disclosed 4.9 gigawatts of contracted capacity spanning five data center campuses across Texas, Missouri, and other US sites, against a total development pipeline exceeding 40 gigawatts.Finance Rewrites the AI Infrastructure EquationThe pattern that Jane Street's spending has established over the past five months is not yet widely recognized as a pattern, because each transaction has been reported bilaterally.

Seen in sequence, it is something different: a major financial institution systematically positioning itself as a structural anchor of AI infrastructure finance, on both the demand side and the equity side.In April, Jane Street's CoreWeave commitment was reported as the firm securing next-generation chip access for its quantitative models — a sensible operational decision.

The $1 billion equity stake alongside the cloud commitment was noted but not foregrounded. This week, Jane Street led FluidStack's $1.5 billion equity round while simultaneously serving as the demand anchor behind Crusoe's $3 billion financing, per Crunchbase's weekly funding roundup.

One firm now holds cloud commitments to two of the three most well-capitalized independent AI infrastructure operators, plus an equity stake in a third, plus its own equity position in a fourth.This is not a coincidence of timing. It is the behavior of an institution that has concluded that dedicated AI compute — not access to models, not access to inference APIs, but raw GPU capacity under long-term bilateral agreements — is a strategic resource comparable to capital itself in a quantitative trading context.

The corollary is what the pattern reveals about the market: if a trading firm with no AI product to sell is locking in infrastructure at this scale and in these structures, the constraint on the AI buildout is no longer uncertain demand. It is physical supply — power, land, chips, and construction timelines.Synergy Research Group's neocloud market forecast put full-year 2025 neocloud revenues at more than $25 billion, up 223 percent year-over-year in the final quarter alone, and projected the category approaching $400 billion by 2031 at a 58 percent compound annual growth rate.

That growth trajectory assumes the supply of physical infrastructure keeps pace with demand. McKinsey's separate estimate of 156 gigawatts of AI-related data center capacity needed globally by 2030 — against Crusoe's 4.9 gigawatts contracted today — makes the scale of the remaining buildout concrete, as prior TechTimes analysis noted.

When financial firms with the underwriting sophistication of Jane Street start treating take-or-pay GPU contracts as assets worth more than $20 billion in aggregate, they are not speculating on AI demand. They are stating a view about physical supply scarcity that the capital markets are only beginning to price.What a $30 Billion Valuation Is Actually PricingWhether Crusoe is valued as a neocloud or as something architecturally distinct from one determines how the $30 billion figure should be assessed — and the answer matters for anyone investing in the sector.A pure-play GPU rental business is valued on compute contract economics: utilization rates, GPU pricing trends across hardware generations, depreciation schedules, customer concentration, and financing costs.

Those multiples compress as new chip generations make older hardware cheaper, as hyperscalers expand internal capacity, and as new entrants increase supply. A vertically integrated power-and-compute developer, by contrast, is valued on long-term contracted cash flows from physical assets — land, power generation infrastructure, purpose-built data center buildings — that cannot be replicated quickly because the binding constraints are electrical equipment lead times, power permit approval timelines, and gigawatt-scale construction capacity, not capital alone.Crusoe's argument is that its full-stack ownership positions it differently from both categories.

The Jane Street deal supports a specific version of that argument: the $13 billion contract, pledged as loan collateral before its public disclosure per Bloomberg's reporting, provided the demonstrated contracted cash flow that made the Series F terms achievable. Investors are not writing $3 billion checks into a speculative buildout — they are writing them into a company that came to the table with a binding $13 billion customer commitment.The meaningful caveats remain.

The $13 billion Jane Street figure is approximate, sourced to people familiar with the arrangement rather than confirmed by either company. Contract value and delivered profitability diverge significantly: actual returns depend on utilization rates, energy costs, financing charges, depreciation schedules, maintenance, and the degree to which infrastructure serves multiple workloads over its useful life.

The Wyoming episode — where Bloomberg reported in June 2026 that Crusoe was pressured off a 1.8-gigawatt campus in Cheyenne after Google raised concerns about costs and timetable — demonstrated that contracted pipeline and operating capacity are different things, and that hyperscaler customers retain significant leverage even after initial agreements are signed.

At a $30 billion valuation, the company is being assessed against a future infrastructure position that must be built, permitted, powered, and filled — not against current revenue.Still, the signal that the September 3 announcements send is difficult to dismiss. Prior TechTimes reporting noted Crusoe was worth $2.8 billion eighteen months ago.

It is worth $30 billion today. Its largest customer is not an AI laboratory or a cloud platform — it is a trading firm that concluded GPU clusters are as strategically important as trading algorithms and balance sheet. And the investors writing billion-dollar checks into it now include sovereign capital from Abu Dhabi alongside the most prominent names in technology venture.Technical Architecture: Why Crusoe's Infrastructure Matches What Jane Street NeedsJane Street's decision to commit $13 billion to Crusoe rather than expand its CoreWeave agreement is not incidentally related to Crusoe's technical architecture — it is probably explained by it.

CoreWeave's model, like most neoclouds, is built around multi-tenant GPU rental: capacity is available on demand, shared across customer workloads, billed by the GPU-hour. For most AI development teams, that flexibility is a feature. For a quantitative trading firm running proprietary models on proprietary data, it is a liability.Crusoe's cloud platform for the Jane Street deal provides what CoreWeave's official press release described for that transaction as well: clusters of advanced GPUs with dedicated connectivity, custom storage configurations, and infrastructure isolated at the network level.

The distinction matters for financial firms: proprietary trading models cannot share physical infrastructure with other customers without introducing security and data-separation risk that compliance functions will not accept. Single-tenant, dedicated clusters — the kind that Crusoe's campus model produces by design — eliminate that risk at the infrastructure layer.Prior TechTimes Crusoe coverage documented Crusoe's GPU fleet spanning NVIDIA GB200 NVL72, B200, H200, H100, and A100, as well as AMD MI300X and MI355X chips.

The intelligent routing protocols and dynamic traffic shaping built into the cloud networking layer are designed specifically for the distributed training pattern where thousands of GPU nodes must synchronize gradient updates simultaneously — a workload that quantitative model training increasingly resembles as models grow larger and training runs more complex.The economics of that architecture are what a take-or-pay contract enables.

Jane Street commits to minimum spend whether or not all capacity is consumed; Crusoe receives a stable contracted cash flow against which it can borrow to finance the infrastructure build; and both parties benefit from the alignment that a five-year term provides — Jane Street gets priority access and rate certainty, Crusoe gets the balance sheet capacity to keep building.Frequently Asked QuestionsWhy is Jane Street, a trading firm, spending more on AI compute than most AI labs?Quantitative trading firms like Jane Street use machine learning to generate trading signals, optimize execution, and model market risk in real time.

Each improvement in model quality translates directly into trading performance, so access to faster hardware enables faster model iteration — and in financial markets, speed of iteration is a direct competitive advantage. Jane Street also needs single-tenant, dedicated GPU clusters rather than shared cloud capacity, because proprietary trading models cannot safely run on shared infrastructure with other customers.

That demand profile — large-scale, dedicated, long-term — is exactly what Crusoe's architecture is built to provide, and it explains why a firm with no AI product to sell has committed more to AI compute than most companies that exist solely to build AI.What does Jane Street's combined $20B-plus commitment tell us about where AI progress is actually constrained?When a trading firm with no public AI product systematically locks in more than $20 billion in AI infrastructure across three operators — as compute buyer, equity investor, and equity round lead — it is making a specific empirical claim about scarcity.

It is not speculating on demand for AI, which is already visible. It is betting that physical supply — power generation capacity, permitted land, long-lead electrical equipment, GPU hardware — will remain the binding constraint on the AI buildout for the duration of these agreements.

That is a different and more specific claim than "AI will be important." It means that whoever controls dedicated physical infrastructure at scale controls access to the AI compute stack, and Jane Street has decided that position is worth more than $20 billion.How does Crusoe's vertically integrated model protect it from the GPU depreciation risk that threatens other neoclouds?Standard neocloud operators monetize GPU capacity directly — they rent GPU-hours, and when a new chip generation makes older hardware cheaper, their rental revenue declines while their depreciation schedule continues.

Crusoe's exposure to that dynamic is second-order rather than direct. Because Crusoe owns the land, the power generation assets, and the purpose-built buildings, it is monetizing powered infrastructure under long-duration leases rather than monetizing GPU hardware specifically. If the hardware cycle compresses faster than expected, Crusoe's tenant still needs the physical facility and its power supply.

The shell can be re-let with different hardware, insulating the equity position from GPU residual value risk in a way that a pure-play GPU rental business cannot replicate.What are the remaining risks at a $30 billion valuation?Three structural risks matter most. First, the $13 billion Jane Street figure is approximate and unconfirmed by either party — it is sourced to people familiar with the arrangement, not a public filing.

Second, contracted value and delivered profitability diverge significantly: actual returns depend on utilization, energy costs, financing charges, and depreciation, none of which are publicly disclosed by Crusoe. Third, the Wyoming episode demonstrated that hyperscaler customers retain meaningful leverage even after agreements are signed — Crusoe was pushed off a 1.8-gigawatt Wyoming campus in June 2026 after Google raised concerns about costs and timetable, illustrating that pipeline and operating capacity are not the same thing.

At a $30 billion valuation, the company is priced against a future infrastructure position that must still be built.
