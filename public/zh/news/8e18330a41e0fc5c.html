美国计算机专业遇冷：AI冲击下生源锐减

✍️ 记者 / 供稿: Bloomberg News

美国国家学生交流中心数据显示，受AI替代初级编码岗位影响，本硕计算机专业入学人数分别暴跌超12%与10%

⚡ 核心要点提炼与关键事实

  • 【核心事件】美国国家学生交流中心及美国各大高校计算机系在美国高等教育招生市场：计算机科学专业本硕生源分别下滑12%与10%
  • 【起因与机理】AI自动化编程能力导致初级程序员就业市场看衰
  • 【关键数据与实测】美国国家学生交流中心发布最新秋季入学数据，显示计算机及信息科学专业的本科与研究生入学人数分别大幅下滑超过12%和10%。
  • 【各方立场与态度】传统编程专业市场价值定位出现动摇。

Bloomberg 权威实录

Enrollments in the major were once a sure bet. Then generative AI arrived. The students started noticing when the Discord, subreddit, and internal chat platform they used went quiet.

Between the spring of 2025 and the spring of 2026, Oregon State University lost nearly 1,000 computer-science majors, or 23 percent of its overall enrollment in the field. The drop-off was especially steep in its online programs, which lost 30 percent of their students. Computer science is the university’s most popular major, and the “melting down” of that enrollment was so significant that during a board meeting in March, Jayathi Y.

Murthy, Oregon State’s president, told trustees it was responsible for a $19-million budget shortfall. “I go to a lot of TA tutoring,” said Teodoro Partida, a case manager at a nonprofit in Chicago who last year started a bachelor’s program in computer science through Oregon State’s Ecampus.

“I was seeing less of my peers at those as more time went by.” Oregon State, of course, is part of a national trend. For two decades, students from elementary school on up were told that the future was in learning to code. Enrollment in computer science rose accordingly.

Then, in late 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT. At first, Chat stunned the public with its naturalistic conversations, but soon it became clear that another of its strengths was writing code. If agentic AI has thrown all of higher ed into an existential crisis — What’s the purpose of college, if AI can digest books/write essays/tutor students? — it has done so especially for computer-science departments.

In the last year, at four-year institutions alone, 53,000 students fled the field, as The Chronicle previously reported. “Why are people still enrolling in this program?” a Reddit user asked in the OSUOnlineCS subreddit, a public forum where current, former, and prospective students swap tips, gossip, and job-hunting horror stories.

“With the job market collapsing, incoming recession, AI, offshoring, and hundreds of thousands of CS grads and bootcampers being pumped into the market every year, why pursue a cs degree?” That was two years ago. Though some experts argue the market is better than anecdotes suggest, more recent posts have offered advice in the struggle to find work and described having to apply to as many as 700 jobs before landing one.

Sometimes it’s hard to know, as a student or a university, where the job market will head next. Some peculiarities of Oregon State meant that it was hit especially hard. Indeed, about one out of every 50 students at four-year colleges nationwide who left computer science in the last year had been enrolled there.

Other colleges’ programs haven’t been spared, however, particularly those that don’t limit the number of students who can join the major — in other words, those programs that try to make the dream of a secure, well-paying tech job more accessible. That promise is now harder to make.

The official history of remote learning at Oregon State University begins, charmingly, with “correspondence courses” that the university, then called the Oregon Agricultural College, offered by mail in the 1910s. It features a photo of a horse-drawn mail coach. By the late 1990s, the university offered bachelor’s degrees in agricultural and environmental sciences that students could finish partly online, “as well as by videotape.” Ecampus, short for Extended Campus, started in 2002.

The mission was to give “adult learners access to an Oregon State education no matter where they live.” The dot-com bubble had just burst, which would lead to a yearslong decline in computer-science majors, but culturally, the internet was thriving. Just a little more than half of American households had an internet connection.

Ecampus let them get OSU degrees entirely online. In 2012, when the tech industry had recovered, Ecampus worked with OSU’s School of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science (EECS) to create a fully online, post-baccalaureate program. Students who already had bachelor’s degrees in other fields could get a B.S. in computer science in as little as one year.

The university called the program “the first one in the country of its kind,” and it quickly hit rankers’ “top online computer-science degrees” lists, including U.S. News & World Report’s. Within a year, the computer-science post-bac became Ecampus’s biggest program.

In December 2013, the then-head of EECS deposited $20,000 into every tenure-track faculty member’s account, proceeds from the tuition that their program had raised. “Happy Friday the 13th!” read the email announcing the deposit. The university began seeing Ecampus as its golden goose, the program that could keep growing enrollment, unconstrained by time or place or supply of Oregonians of the age who would traditionally wander the walks of its 570-acre campus in Corvallis.

“Graph 2” in the university’s “FY27 Operating Budget,” which was published in March 2026, is illustrative. In an orange, dotted line, the graph shows the trend in Ecampus tuition revenue, pre-2026, sloping upward at a rate of more than $10 million a year. A black, solid line shows the recently adjusted expectation.

After 2025, revenues were projected to flatten. Was it a problem that the university had budgeted under the assumption that Ecampus would keep growing? Jessica DuPont, interim vice provost overseeing Ecampus, argued that administrators hadn't been entirely blindsided.

They had been tracking the computer-science enrollment decline, which began in 2024. “What was a bit more of a surprise was just how much,” she said. Selina Heppell, head of the department of fisheries, wildlife, and conservation sciences, who has been at Oregon State since 2001, had a blunter assessment: No one saw a logical reason for Ecampus’s growth to stop.

Now, she said: “Everybody is feeling the pinch, for sure.” Over all, Oregon State’s enrollment is actually rising. Headcount is 25 percent higher now than it was 10 years ago. It’s the state’s largest university, and it’s not in the same situation as, say, Southern Oregon University, which required a $15-million bailout from lawmakers earlier this year.

But the flip side of raking in revenue from Ecampus’s computer-science programs — whose students pay a higher tuition rate than Ecampus students in some other fields — is that the enrollment bust had an outsize effect on the budget. Oregon State’s expenses are growing faster than its revenue.

Personnel and operating costs are rising. The federal government’s payments for research overhead plummeted this year, and aren’t expected to recover. (Graph 3, tracking overhead reimbursements, is even more disheartening than Graph 2.) The university will have an operating budget shortfall of $33.7 million this year.

It implemented universitywide cuts of a little more than 5 percent last fiscal year and this one, though individual units may have seen larger or smaller trims. It’s up to the units to decide how they want to meet those tightened budgets. The School of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science has considered especially stringent measures.

It has already cut three staff members, 16 graduate teaching-assistant positions, and 10 Ecampus instructors, including senior instructors who had been with Oregon State University for a decade or more. At a faculty retreat in September, Ben Lee, the school’s interim head, presented a budget-cut plan that included a 5-percent furlough and more layoffs: of TAs, instructors, and staff members, plus two tenure-track faculty members, according to a copy of the presentation that The Chronicle obtained.

"These reductions were never approved or implemented," Rob Odom, the university's spokesperson, wrote in an email. The school's enrollment has "stabilized" since the spring, he wrote. Oregon State recently touted its fall entering class, including a 28-percent surge in enrollment in electrical and computer engineering, a separate major in EECS that focuses on hardware, rather than software.

Electrical and computer engineering is smaller than computer science, but still one of the university's biggest majors. The numbers come from a count done on September 23, just two days after the EECS retreat. Glencora Borradaile, a computer-science professor who was at the retreat, said that Lee had told faculty members that he had proposed his budget plan to the dean of the College of Engineering.

It was news to Borradaile that the layoffs definitely weren't happening. "No one has communicated anything to EECS faculty writ large since the retreat," said Borradaile, who is the college's faculty-union representative. "Having them dangling there as a possibility is stressing faculty out." The union alleges that the university had already violated the union contract in how it carried out its Ecampus layoffs.

Roy Haggerty, the provost, said he couldn't comment on personnel matters. Lee didn't respond to messages asking to learn more. Meanwhile, the university’s top leaders have struck a more positive tone.

The university needs to balance the budget this year, but expects to be able to start hiring again in 2029, said Haggerty, in an interview in his office. Strategic Resource Renewal, the official name for Oregon State’s plan to shore up its financial future, depends on finding new sources of revenue and on savings through actions like reducing external spending and managing buildings better, not through cutting positions, he said.

Whether part of Strategic Resource Renewal or not, units throughout Oregon State have laid off employees to help meet their smaller budgets in the last two years. Nontenured faculty members in the botany department, tenured faculty members in a community-health program, and library staff members are among those who have lost their jobs.

Ecampus has created more than a dozen new degree programs over the last few years, including six bachelor’s degrees in engineering disciplines, DuPont said, sitting at the same long conference table as Haggerty. Some are doing quite well, like mechanical, civil, and architectural engineering.

The university had been trying to diversify Ecampus’s offerings, DuPont said: “You can become overly dependent on a couple of programs that are really successful, and then if something happens, then what?” Faculty members at other colleges across the country didn’t mention $20,000 deposits, but they recalled Peak Computer Science.

Bobby Schnabel, a computer-science professor at the University of Colorado at Boulder, remembered the hiring, especially of teaching faculty, but also for tenure-track positions. Christopher M. Jermaine, chair of the computer-science department at Rice University, remembered scrambling to add sections of COMP 140, Rice’s equivalent of “Computer Science 101,” to meet student demand.

He remembered that, unlike chairs in other academic units, he never had to be “overly concerned about where to find funding.” If the department had a problem covering a class, for example: “Rather than me having to strong-arm someone into teaching it, we could look across campus and find someone who was willing, and pay for them.” Jermaine became chair of his department in 2021, right when there was a national spike in student interest in computer science.

“I don’t pretend to know why, exactly,” he said, although he pointed to major tech companies going on hiring sprees in the depths of the pandemic. Those companies have since shrunk, contributing to the current contraction of academic computer science. Between the spring of 2025 and the spring of 2026, the number of undergraduate computer-science majors at four-year colleges fell by more than 8 percent, according to the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center.

In no other case did The Chronicle find that the drop set off the kind of deep cuts that Oregon State University has seen. But computer-science departments everywhere are dealing with new discomforts. Southern New Hampshire University, the online-learning juggernaut, reported a 7-percent decline in new computer-science enrollments between 2024 and 2025.

A spokesperson said she couldn't access more recent numbers, or make anyone available for an interview. The CU Boulder main campus lost 11 percent of its computer science-related majors between the spring of 2025 and 2026, according to its public dashboard. The department is offering fewer teaching assistantships to graduate students, although it hasn’t had to do anything else to balance the budget, Schnabel said: “Because of all the good years, there’s somewhat of a cushion that allows us to not have to react urgently.” The department is considering adding an AI degree, in hopes of retaining some students, Schnabel said.

At Oakland Community College, in Michigan, the number of students majoring in computer-information systems fell by 12 percent between the 2024-25 and 2025-26 academic years. Student credit hours in the department are up, however, suggesting that students continue to be interested in the courses, or think that they could be a useful addition to their major fields of study.

“We’re not panicking,” said Eric Charnesky, a professor there. “We’re noting it. I think we’ve always been somewhat cyclical.” At Rice, Jermaine said that the department hasn’t yet seen a decline in declared majors, although he thought it could, in the future.

“At this time, there’s a certain unease,” he said. Schnabel pointed to another important budget pressure on computer-science departments at research universities. The National Science Foundation, the main government funder of computer-science research, has been disbursing grants more slowly than usual, and the future of federal funding for science seems uncertain.

As is often true in higher education, the most prestigious and selective programs may be insulated from the winds buffeting their more accessible peers. The University of Washington’s computer-science program has long been highly ranked, and bolstered by its association with Microsoft’s co-founders, who have contributed to named buildings on campus.

The university restricts the number of undergraduates who can join the major, to 630 per year, out of a student body of about 36,000 at the Seattle campus. About 2,000 fewer students applied to the major in 2026 than in 2025 — but applications still numbered more than 5,000, according to a graph provided by Dan Grossman, interim director of the Paul G.

Allen School of Computer Science and Engineering. Asked whether faculty members at the school were planning for a drop in enrollment, Grossman wrote in an email that they hadn’t felt the need: “What we do need to do — and are doing — is to make sure potential students know that computing remains an exciting field with plentiful jobs and opportunities.” His colleagues across the sector, at institutions at every level, echoed his message: Despite the national narrative and the experiences of some job seekers, positions still exist.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, for one, expects the market for “computer and information research scientists” to grow much faster than average for the next decade. The future will need people who know how to think about computing.

It’s just that computer-science faculty have never had to sell their field before. Now they do. You might not think, upon visiting Oregon State University’s Kelley Engineering Center, that it was suffering for money.

The biggest Huang presence on campus, however, is next door. Under the din of heavy equipment, the Jen-Hsun Huang and Lori Mills Huang Collaborative Innovation Complex was still under construction. Jen-Hsun, who also spells his name “Jensen,” is the chief executive and a founder of Nvidia, the company whose chips OpenAI and Anthropic use to run their AI models.

Over the last year, as Oregon State University stared down its own personal enrollment cliff, its most famous alumnus hobnobbed with President Trump and helmed the world’s most valuable company, whose $5-trillion valuation is based on the very technology that’s gutting Oregon State’s enrollments.

Jen-Hsun and his wife were classmates in engineering at Oregon State. They donated $50 million to their alma mater in 2022 for the Collaborative Innovation Complex. The building will house an AI supercomputer, powered by Nvidia chips.

In an interview with local reporters at the time, Huang said that he hoped the machine would help advance fields including climate and materials science. The latter could push forward the semiconductor industry, which depends on innovative materials that can perform computations faster and more cheaply.

In more recent interviews, he’s been bullish about what AI will mean for jobs and higher education. His predictions contrast with those from engineers who work on the software side of AI, who can be apocalyptic. In an AI-dominated future, engineers will still exist, Huang said on The Ezra Klein Show in September: “The purpose of the software engineer is ‘engineer.’ There was engineering before software.

There will be engineering after software programming." He also predicted that there will be a “wave of amazing engineers” in two years’ time, as those students who entered college after the advent of widely available, agentic AI graduate. Vicente Sanchez Bautista and Lily Vu might count as some of those students.

Both are sophomores majoring in electrical and computer engineering. Sitting at a bench in the Kelley Engineering Center, they talked about doing well in classes like math, physics, and various programming languages at their public high school in Wilsonville, about an hour north of Corvallis.

“Before coming to college, I thought about becoming a CS major,” Vu said. Then she saw what AI could do and decided on electrical and computer engineering instead, reasoning that it would be more resilient to AI disruptions because it’s focused on the physical components of computers.

“AI can’t do the physical stuff,” she said. “Yet,” Sanchez Bautista said.
