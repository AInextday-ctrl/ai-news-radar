Colossus Full Report

“Don’t move, senior citizen coming down.” Jensen Huang put his hands on Elon Musk’s shoulders and hopped off the dais, rejoining Michael Dell, Satya Nadella, Lisa Su, Sergey Brin, and Musk on the ground floor of the Donald J. Trump Institute of Peace. The President had just arrived on site. The six of them would soon join him on stage to receive national medals of science or technology, but for now they were locked down in an adjoining auditorium with time to kill.

“Okay, functional safety,” Huang soon said to the other five, apropos of nothing. “How does it work? An AI has a goal, how does it achieve it?” “By any means necessary,” Musk replied, laughing.

Several layers of the Secret Service had temporarily separated these executives—who between them own or control companies worth around $18 trillion—from their security details and staff, their families and guests. Michael Kratsios, Trump’s chief scientific advisor and the man responsible for assembling them, had been called away by an aide to see his boss. After he left, the gang of six were left all but alone.

There were two men from the White House video team on the dais, to whom each of the medalists was speaking in turn. While Huang talked to them, the conversation on the floor turned to compute. It was, to the benefit of Nvidia’s CEO, in short supply. “[Greg] Brockman at dinner was hounding me for compute,” Musk said, referring to OpenAI’s president. “He was asking me for compute,” Sergey Brin said. Musk marveled at his sense of the man’s chutzpah. “Dude, you stole my charity! You can’t be a huge dick then ask for favors, hello?”

Brockman had famously kept a diary during the 2010s, which became relevant in court when Musk began his unsuccessful lawsuit against Sam Altman, OpenAI’s CEO, in 2024. “How many grown men do you know that keep a diary?” Brin said, throwing a log on the pyre. “If I did I’d burn it.”

The current widespread lack of compute was nothing compared to the lack of memory chips. “I’ve never in my life seen a supply chain price shock like it,” Musk told the group, in disbelief at Micron’s 87 percent gross margin this past quarter, which has more than doubled in two years. (The company had booked $85 billion in net profit in the past 12 months.) Only six American companies had made more; half of them were run by men in the room. “They’re not really putting the money back,” Nadella said of Micron’s capex plans, sharing Musk’s skepticism of its desire to expand capacity fast.

Stripped of their retinues and the gaze that follows them in any room, they were—once Huang rejoined them—just six people, standing in a circle. As in any circle, a dynamic soon became clear. Huang took the lead, but Musk, holding a small wine glass filled with Diet Coke and ice, was irrepressible and dominant. Su was quiet. She had initially stood off to the side, before Nadella peeled off from the main group to join her. Dell, whose eponymous company had at the time increased in value more than fivefold since February, seemed content to let others speak. Brin and Nadella served as conversational foils for Huang and Musk.

“NetJets sucks for international travel,” Huang said, moving on to lighter matters. Musk turned to Dell. “He probably owns a fleet [of planes],” he said, smiling. “Just one,” replied Dell, tall, broad, and unhurried. “You can only be on one.” He credited Musk for creating Starlink. “I do Zoom meetings all the time.” “Starlink’s been great for us,” said Brin. Both men have owned a Gulfstream 650ER, which seats up to 19 people.

Huang chooses not to own a jet, despite being trapped on a recent Delta flight without working Wi-Fi. “I sat quietly,” he said, which was hard to imagine; few men possess more compressed energy. “I read about 7,000 emails… Nothing drives me nuttier than not getting internet access. I’ve got work to do!” “Best way to sandbox an AI,” said Musk. “Put it on a Delta flight: No escape is possible.”

They had all initially gathered with their entourages in another room down the hall. Musk brought his mother and three of his children; Huang brought his wife, adult children, and his parents; Su brought her husband; Brin brought his girlfriend, GiGi, a wellness influencer beloved by Trump; Dell and Nadella came alone.

Musk, the only guest not to be screened by security, had bounded into that room last: wide-eyed, bigger than everyone else, already amused, not entirely in control of his movements. He had looked around with a teenager’s excitement at the 20 or so people present—in a room containing the U.S. secretary of energy, the administrator of NASA, and three of the 10 richest men in the world after himself—and had, within a minute, pulled out his phone to show a gaggle of them some test footage of the Tesla Roadster in flight. “This is meant to be top secret but whatever,” he said, smiling.

Musk had been too absorbed by the footage to acknowledge Kratsios at first. (They know each other well.) Huang, capable of charming anyone, was more attentive, greeting Kratsios (“Director, nice to see you”) and making sure to introduce him to his parents as his father clasped Musk’s hand, beaming. “He is our nation’s chief scientist,” Huang told his parents of Kratsios, generously. He marveled at his youth. “39? I was at my peak at 39.”

The medalists had then moved to this room to make way for POTUS and the phalanx that always surrounds him. The ground floor was cleared and refilled while the gang of six passed the time. An aide arrived in the doorway. The President was here.

He was perhaps eight meters away, but quite unreachable. He then greeted the medalists one by one behind the stage. He shook hands, embraced them, left no shoulder untapped. When it came to Brin, Trump gave him a hug of sorts, gripping Brin by his arms as he motioned from side to side. “We love you,” Trump’s lips read, as he pulled back.

Seven members of the Secret Service Assault Team, bearing automatic rifles and covered in tactical gear, filed into the auditorium vacated by the medalists as the action moved to the stage, where the President was flanked by more classically dressed agents.

Trump spoke first, running through the achievements of each winner. He chose to start with and dwell longest on Musk, with whom his bond seemed stronger than ever. SpaceX had, that morning, safely returned four astronauts to Earth after eight months on board the International Space Station. “Could you imagine if things didn’t work out so well?” Trump said, veering off script. “I wonder if he’d be here… but it always works out well for Elon, right?”

Less than 18 months earlier, Musk had taken to X to expose Trump’s presence in the Epstein files. (“Time to drop the really big bomb,” he wrote.) But something deeper than necessity had reconciled them. “We’re proud of you, very proud of you. National treasure,” Trump said on stage.

Kratsios took to the lectern in front of a futuristic arching backdrop bearing the word SCIENCE, and proceeded to show a highly developed understanding of the President’s character. “Mr. President, before we get to the actual medaling, we have prepared a short video to celebrate the lifetime achievements of your uncle, a 1983 national medal of science recipient, John G. Trump.”

Trump had already begun his remarks by invoking his uncle, an MIT professor for almost 40 years, whose intellect he never tires of mentioning. Kratsios had reached out to Brett Ratner, who has become Trumpworld’s in-house director since making Melania earlier this year, to help make the video. “Some scientists transform medicine. Some help win wars. John G. Trump did both,” it began. Kratsios sprung another surprise once it concluded, bringing out John G. Trump’s medal in a frame. He presented it to Trump, who gladly paraded it to the audience. The medalists duly made their way to the stage.

“This is it, this is the whole ballgame,” Trump said to the recipients as he finished his remarks, enjoying the magnitude of the moment. “It is about time you were recognized. You are about to go down in the annals of history.”

I caught up with Kratsios after the event. “The President’s excited,” he said, excited himself. Trump had felt the meaning of it all, Kratsios suggested, because of his uncle. “He loved it. He said, ‘Get me five more [names]. Let’s do another one.’”

President Trump applauds National Medal of Technology and Innovation recipients Satya Nadella, Michael Dell, Lisa Su, Elon Musk, Jensen Huang, and Sergey Brin during the “A New Golden Age” summit in Washington, D.C., October 8, 2026.

Michael Kratsios has worked for Trump every day in office since January 20, 2017. He was little known in the first Trump term, but he has now become one of the few White House aides entrusted with making presidential caprice a reality. He has spent six years turning the traditionally irrelevant office he runs—the Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP)—into a consequential engine room of government. OSTP has historically been maligned for producing little more than congressionally mandated reports on, say, algae bloom. But Kratsios is using it to set tech policy across much of the U.S. government. He sits between the two most powerful constituencies in America—POTUS and Silicon Valley—and now effectively serves as the White House coordinator for critical emerging technologies, including in science, space, nuclear, and quantum. He is one of a handful of players steering AI policy for Trump, insofar as anyone has latitude to act on an issue the President has commandeered.

Kratsios had watched Trump’s victory speech on the night of the 2016 election from the sunken trading floor at Thiel Capital in San Francisco, a day after turning 30. He had worked for Peter Thiel for six years by then, and knew he was witnessing a miraculous opportunity materialize in front of him. His boss, the first man in the Valley to back Trump, was about to be named to the transition’s executive committee. As an 18-year-old in 2005, Kratsios had worked as an intern in Lindsey Graham’s office during the summer of John Roberts’ Supreme Court confirmation. He briefly began his career in investment banking before landing with Thiel, but he had always wanted to return to D.C.

He flew with Thiel and a handful of others to Trump Tower on the Monday after the election. They were given desks on the 14th floor, where he began to think through what a tech agenda could look like for the President, whose campaign had focused on more emotive issues like immigration, trade, and war. He helped organize the first meeting between Trump and the tech leaders of the day. (“You’ll call my people, you’ll call me, it doesn’t make any difference. We have no formal chain of command around here,” Trump told them then, before anyone realized how true that was.) When Thiel flew back to San Francisco, Kratsios stayed, and began to research the many offices and agencies of the federal government.

This underutilized office in the White House, he thought, could become the most important office for science and technology in America. He couldn’t understand why no one else grasped its potential relevance. Every previous director had been a PhD scientist, focused on providing largely ceremonial scientific counsel that often went unheeded. Congress formally created the position in 1976 after Nixon, tired of being lectured by the scientific community over foreign policy, had dissolved an earlier iteration of the office. Congress’ aim was to ensure presidents received non-partisan advice, but its director was also in charge of an organ of government, and few scientists were naturally suited to the work of a bureaucrat.

OSTP had, in Kratsios’ mind, long since turned inward. Its aims under Obama, he felt, over-indexed on climate science and fixing government websites—noble causes, but Kratsios traced OSTP’s lineage back to a much grander past: the wartime office run by a former MIT scientist, Vannevar Bush, that inaugurated the Manhattan Project.

In its modern form, OSTP lacked formal authority over the agencies that spent $200 billion of America’s R&D dollars every year: the then-Department of Defense; the Department of Energy (DOE), which ran America’s 17 national labs; the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and National Science Foundation (NSF), the twin pillars of basic science research; and NASA. But it was a wing of the White House: it could command the federal bureaucracy in the president’s voice, so long as the president and chief of staff wanted it to.

There was a chance Trump would shut it down. The Heritage Foundation wanted him to, and Obama had loved it, which might have been cause enough for the President-elect. But the Trump 45 White House had more important fights. OSTP was simply neglected instead, allowing Kratsios to get himself appointed as America’s deputy chief technology officer, housed inside the office. It was a role requiring no Senate confirmation, allowing him to start work immediately, and there was no CTO or OSTP director. The office was his.

On January 21, 2017, Kratsios made his way through the Washington rain to the Eisenhower Executive Office Building (EEOB), the neighboring building that houses most White House staff. He had never been inside. A conference room full of civil servants were waiting for him on the fourth floor in fear of their jobs, at the end of a long checkered stone hallway. Kratsios, who is wiry, alert, and unlikely to berate anyone, moved into the room to introduce himself.

He was a marathon runner, and a four-time winner of the Model UN competition at his South Carolina public school. As a 10-year-old boy on holiday in Japan in 1997 with his first and second-generation Greek immigrant parents, he had insisted on being taken to visit the Nozomi 500, the fastest train in the world. He was, to his great regret, too young to catch a flight on Concorde before it was decommissioned when he was 16. Now he wanted to bring it back, and turn OSTP into something more like an Office for the American Frontier.

Trump was in the process of sending vandals into key government departments, as he would do again eight years later. Kratsios wasn’t one of them, but the civil servants in the conference room didn’t know that. “Hey, I’m Michael,” Kratsios said in his diffident way. I’m going to be working here. He met the two primary lifers in the office, and got to work.

The President doesn’t leave much wall space to chance inside the West Wing. Every available inch of white paint running down the surprisingly low-ceilinged corridors of the White House has been covered by portraits of the man in action, whether disembarking Marine One, greeting a UFC fighter at the edge of the Octagon assembled in his honor this summer on the South Lawn, or striding out of an AI-generated wall of fire in his familiar get-up of box-cut suit, red tie, long black overcoat, and scowl fit for a mug shot.

A marine stood guard at the entrance at noon on September 15, 2026, which meant the President was inside on the day that I was shown around by Kratsios. It was three weeks before the science summit-slash-medal ceremony. After a hushed tour, I stepped outside onto the gilded “Presidential Walk of Fame” installed by Trump along the building’s west colonnade, with its narrative history of each presidency, and stood beside the portrait of an autopen that depicts Joe Biden. Kratsios asked me if I wanted a picture.

Trump would show off the pen to an unusually amused Xi Jinping nine days later. (“Sleepy Joe Biden was, by far, the worst President in American History…,” Trump writes of the only person to beat him in a presidential election.) To the left, chairs had been laid out for a press conference on the Rose Garden patio, paved over by Trump last summer. High heels no longer get caught in the grass, I was told. I looked out at the newly built helicopter pad behind the patio, and at the President’s half-built East Wing ballroom beyond that.
