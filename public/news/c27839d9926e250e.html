Wall Street Journal Full Report

China's push to strengthen its AI capabilities involved two separate efforts allegedly linked to Alice Huang and her husband, Kang Wei, according to a Wall Street Journal investigation. While Kang expanded a network of data centres backed by the Chinese government, Alice reportedly built a close, romantic relationship with Nvidia's then-CEO Jensen Huang. The report claims she also helped create a channel for accessing Nvidia's advanced computing chips, several of which Washington has sought to prevent from reaching China. Together, their activities allegedly supported China's ambition to gain an edge over the US in artificial intelligence.

Kang Wei's business interests in China grew into a major data-centre operation, with projects worth more than $9 billion proposed between 2021 and 2024, a review of Chinese public records by the Wall Street Journal found. These ventures had financial backing from government-linked investors and were promoted as part of China's plan to build more computing infrastructure. The findings offer a closer look at Kang's previously unreported involvement in China's AI expansion, at a time when the US is trying to limit China's access to advanced computing technology.

Alice Huang’s Husband Built a $9 Billion Data-Center Empire Backed by the Chinese State While Alice Huang secured access to Nvidia’s advanced AI chips, her husband was building a sprawling network of government-backed computing infrastructure across China. A new Wall Street Journal investigation by Lingling Wei reveals another dimension of the Nvidia–Megaspeed controversy: the extensive business network built by Alice Huang’s husband, Kang Wei, and its connections to China’s state-backed AI ambitions. According to Chinese securities filings and government announcements reviewed by the Journal, proposed investments tied to Kang’s data-center network exceeded $9 billion between 2021 and 2024, with backing from Chinese government entities. At the center of this network is Century Longteng, a company whose ownership structure reportedly includes a 10% stake held by a Shanghai government entity, 27% associated with Kang’s investment network, and 63% held by Shenzhen-listed Century Huatong. In 2022, Century Longteng signed a cooperation agreement with Huawei, the Chinese technology giant subject to U.S. export restrictions, covering cloud data centers, energy infrastructure, and computing capacity. Kang’s network subsequently pursued several massive projects: Wuhu, Anhui: A proposed $4.5 billion development featuring eight data-center buildings and approximately 24,000 server racks, with local government support for power and infrastructure. Liangshan, Sichuan: Another proposed data-center development valued at approximately $5 billion, launched in 2024. Shenzhen: Additional data-center involvement documented in corporate filings. These projects align with Beijing’s national East Data, West Computing initiative, designed to expand and redistribute computing capacity across China. Meanwhile, Alice Huang’s Singapore-based company, Megaspeed, became an Nvidia Cloud Partner in 2023, gaining preferential access to advanced AI chips. Alibaba was identified as a major customer. The Wall Street Journal previously reported that at least some Nvidia chips acquired by Megaspeed were shipped to China. Even more concerning, Kang and Alice reportedly pitched data-center projects in Shanghai and Southeast Asia to prospective investors while claiming they could bring Nvidia chips into China. This raises a much bigger question than Jensen Huang’s personal relationship with Alice Huang. How did access to America’s most advanced AI hardware intersect with a multibillion-dollar computing infrastructure network backed by the Chinese state? Importantly, the Journal acknowledges that the available public records do not establish that Beijing directed Megaspeed’s chip purchases or that those chips ultimately reached Kang’s data centers. Those connections remain unproven and require further investigation. But the broader national security implications are impossible to ignore. While Washington was trying to restrict China’s access to advanced AI computing power, one couple was operating on both sides of the technological divide: One had a privileged procurement channel into Nvidia’s chip ecosystem and a personal relationship with its then-CEO. The other was developing billions of dollars’ worth of computing infrastructure with Chinese government backing. How did this arrangement develop, who knew about it, and what safeguards were in place to prevent American technology from strengthening China’s military and strategic AI capabilities? This is no longer just a story about a CEO’s romantic scandal. It is a story about export controls, corporate accountability, and the potential intersection of American technological leadership with China’s state-backed AI ambitions. Kang's entry into the sector began in 2021 through Century Longteng, a Shanghai-based company backed by both private investors and a local government body. He later joined its leadership and helped steer its expansion into data centres. One of its early major partnerships was with Huawei in 2022, covering cloud computing, energy systems and data-centre infrastructure. The company then announced plans for a massive computing park in Wuhu, with an estimated investment of $4.5 billion. The development was designed to include eight buildings and space for roughly 24,000 server racks. A separate project worth around $5 billion was announced in Sichuan in 2024.

As Kang pursued data-centre projects in China, his wife Alice Huang was working on a separate business front involving Nvidia chips. Her company, Megaspeed, reportedly told Nvidia in November 2023 that Alibaba would be a major customer. It subsequently received cloud partner status, which offered it priority access to Nvidia processors. The WSJ has reported that some of the chips obtained by Megaspeed were later sent to China. However, there is no public evidence establishing that the Chinese government directed these purchases or that the processors were used in Kang's data centres. Alice was also reportedly in a romantic relationship with Nvidia's then-CEO Jensen Huang, whom Kang had known since their time in the video game industry.

The couple's business activities have since come under scrutiny. Singapore began investigating Megaspeed and several individuals in February 2025 over suspected false declarations related to server purchases. While authorities said there was not enough evidence to establish domestic criminal offences, Alice received a formal warning and was barred from entering Singapore. The investigation involving Megaspeed and six other people is still ongoing. Separately, Kang was detained in Shanghai earlier this year in connection with a stock-market manipulation inquiry, according to the WSJ, but has since been released. Megaspeed has defended its operations as legitimate and lawful and said Alice no longer works with the company.
