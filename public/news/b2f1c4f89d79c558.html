Bloomberg Full Report

Big Tech Mounts Last-Ditch Push to Rewrite Canada’s Data Access Bill Bloomberg · Photographer: Betty Laura Zapata/Bloomberg Laura Osman Mon, 16 November 2026 at 3:00 am GMT-8 5 min read (Bloomberg) -- Prime Minister Mark Carney's government is facing fierce pushback from Big Tech firms over security legislation that would require companies to retain and hand over user data to law enforcement.

Lobbyists and executives from Meta Platforms Inc., Apple Inc. and Alphabet Inc. are mounting a last-ditch blitz in Ottawa to rewrite the bill before it passes. On Monday, leaders from Meta, Signal Messenger LLC and Tailscale Inc. will take their fight to a key Senate committee hearing that will shape the legislation's final form.

Bill C-22, so-called "lawful access" legislation, is designed to allow Canada's spy agency and police services to compel electronic service providers to preserve and turn over certain metadata. The government insists the measures simply align Canada with its Five Eyes and Group of Seven allies.

Tech firms, privacy advocates and US lawmakers counter that the bill goes far beyond frameworks adopted by Canada's peers, warning it would threaten platform security and user privacy. Meta has argued the law would effectively conscript its platforms as "an arm of the government's surveillance apparatus."

"There's a real possibility that both the security and the privacy of Canadian users of these systems is really undermined," Rachel Curran, Meta's head of public policy in Canada, said in an interview.

For Carney's government, the battle risks chilling tech investment, escalating tensions with the US and driving privacy-focused platforms out of the Canadian market. Signal has threatened to pull out of Canada if the bill passes in its current form. Proton AG, a Swiss tech firm, has said it would rethink its expansion plans in the country.

The bill's critics object to a rule that would let the government force electronic service providers to retain certain user metadata for up to six months so it can be disclosed to law enforcement officials, if needed.

The proposed law could also compel some firms to unlock encrypted data — but only if they built the encryption and possess the information necessary to unlock it. Earlier this year, lawmakers amended the bill to make clear that companies also cannot be forced to introduce a "systemic vulnerability" into their software.

Those guardrails were meant to ease fears from firms like Signal and Meta, which owns WhatsApp, that the bill targeted end-to-end encryption. That form of encryption ensures only the sender and receiver can read the messages — meaning the company doesn't have the ability to decrypt them.

Even so, privacy advocates and tech leaders argue that the bill still gives the government sweeping power to order firms to build "technical capabilities" to intercept communications and hand over data.

Tech companies have long warned they cannot create "backdoor" access only for law enforcement without weakening security for all users. Curran said an order that would make Meta's systems more vulnerable is a "red line" for the company, and likely its industry peers as well.

"It probably puts Canada in a position where there's going to eventually be a bit of a standoff between service providers and the government, and it's not good for Canadians," she said.

The UK and Apple faced a similar dispute last year when the government ordered the company to circumvent encryption that the company uses to secure user data stored on its cloud services. Apple challenged the order and removed its advanced data protection feature for British users. The case is still before the courts.

"In the hypothetical future, if we faced similar orders, we would have to consider those options," Curran said.

Public Safety Minister Gary Anandasangaree has said the bill has already been amended to alleviate the concerns raised by Apple in the UK about the potential impact on encryption. He has accused some critics, including Big Tech companies, of spreading misinformation about the bill.

"Bill C-22 does not authorize indiscriminate surveillance and does not permit or compel companies to introduce systemic vulnerabilities or so-called 'backdoors.' Any claims suggesting otherwise are incorrect," Simon Lafortune, the minister's spokesperson, said in a statement.

Apple said in a statement it appreciates the Canadian government's efforts to improve the bill, but the current version would still undermine the company's ability to offer privacy and security features, including encryption, "even as AI and other evolving technologies provide hackers with new tools to exploit cybersecurity weaknesses."

US lawmakers have also pushed back. In May, Republican House Representatives Jim Jordan and Brian Mast warned in a letter to Anandasangaree that the bill would "create significant cross-border risks to the security and data privacy of Americans."

The Canadian Senate committee is the bill's last legislative hurdle before potential passage into law. While the appointed chamber rarely rejects bills outright, it frequently secures amendments — leaving tech giants and privacy advocates one last venue to lobby for changes.
