Wired AI Full Report

Becoming a programmer was, for me, a compromise. Midway through my junior year of college, it became clear that, as an international student from Korea, I likely wouldn’t be able to stay long after graduation unless I changed my major from philosophy to a STEM field that made me eligible for a visa extension. The obvious choice was computer science, which was already my minor.

The switch came with a sense of shame, which in retrospect had a whiff of melodrama. I suppose I was afflicted by what Ian McEwan once called “modern forms of snobbery” in describing a character who, after her bookish college education, came to consider “a man with a degree in chemistry incomplete as a human being.” Chemistry, computer science—same diff. It’s safe to say I did not have many friends in the CS department.

But I don’t think my squeamishness was entirely misplaced. In the post-Facebook-IPO ferment of the 2010s, this country’s computer science departments were newly flush with a certain breed of striver who in another era would’ve gone to Wall Street. My theory is that many in America who acquired a hostility toward technology acquired it by way of a hostility toward these upstarts. That is to say, they aren’t so much anti-technology as anti-technologist.

In any event, I half-assed my way through a good many CS assignments, which meant I was never in danger of going to grad school for AI. But by virtue of a robust public math education in Korea, my entry into the subject was, mercifully, not too bumpy. If anything, theoretical computer science and distributed systems seemed like a continuation of philosophy.

But I could not dispel the sense that programming was a frivolous waste of energy. Every line of code I had to read, every line of code I had to write, was a theft of the time I could’ve spent reading something I cared about. Perhaps it didn’t help that my first two languages were Java (hideous and tedious) and C (admirable but unforgiving). To this day, Java code is like a poisoned madeleine that brings back unwelcome memories.

After college, my visa dictated that I take a job directly tied to my major, and I found a tech company willing to sponsor my green card. The issue was, it could be any number of years until I got the green card—four, it’d turn out—so I figured I might as well find a way to make programming pleasurable.

The first couple of months on the job were joyless. I worked at a health care startup, which I thought gave me a serviceable sense of moral cover against the ongoing techlash and a way to indemnify myself later as a writer. But I liked my coworkers, some of whom also harbored artistic ambitions, and after work we’d regularly adjourn to literary events at the McNally Jackson, a block from our office.

Then, one day, I discovered a language called Haskell. I was tasked with rewriting a code base using a library that borrowed concepts from it. My vague impression of Haskell had been that it was a difficult language involving a branch of mathematics called category theory. There was something seductive about the intimidating aura around it, and when I tried my hand at writing basic programs, the language was indeed challenging, but appealingly so.

I started lurking in Haskell forums where researchers in the field of programming languages—“PL,” as it’s known—flaunted their lavish command of the language. PL researchers might as well be the poets of computer science: a small community of attentive monomaniacs who engaged in a largely unprofitable enterprise—a rarity in computer science—with all the attendant beef and infighting. The unprofitability meant that, unlike AI, the field had an incorruptible dignity to it.

Soon, to my surprise, I found myself programming outside work. After months of tinkering with Haskell, I discovered a book titled Writing a Compiler in Go. Turns out you could even invent a language yourself. I made a few toy languages, useless in practice but invaluable for demystifying how programming languages work.

There was also an oblique way coding could help writing. In Draft No. 4, John McPhee, the journalist’s journalist, says that he still uses an ancient text editor called Kedit, recommended to him in 1984 by a programmer at Princeton. (“I have never used a word processor,” he writes.) When I switched to a text editor myself, I could see why he’d stuck with it. With open source code, I could add silly but useful features, such as highlighting “TK”—a common shorthand in journalism for placeholder text—in a conspicuous color so that it was easy to locate later.

If nothing else, I came to appreciate that programming was a reliable way to enter that elusive mode called flow, a state in which we’re somehow forgetful and alert at once.

In the industry, there’s a general impression that fussing over programming languages is juvenile. If a job candidate expresses enthusiasm for languages, it could very well be a red flag. Being a “polyglot” is no virtue if you can read a menu in five languages but can’t write decent prose in one. And per the popular and influential essay “Choose Boring Technology,” using fancy, novel languages is at odds with serious, robust engineering—akin to how avant-garde poetics are unfit for drafting legal contracts.

Unfortunately for my employers, I had precisely zero fucks to give about serious engineering. I did, however, give a great many fucks about having a pleasurable time at my day job until I received the green card. I took on projects if they’d give me an opportunity to use a new language, which is a dumb reason, and for the same reason even volunteered to change teams, usually a bad career decision. At the obligatory company hackathon weeks, I hacked together domain-specific languages—those specialized for a narrow purpose. I made a meager public contribution by submitting a couple of low-hanging patches to the official TypeScript compiler. I picked up a few gold badges on Stack Overflow. The years went by and, what do you know, I became a pretty decent programmer.

Then everything changed, as it did for everyone else, in November 2022. When ChatGPT arrived, it was a wild card whose impact on the industry was far from obvious. Using neural networks to write code wasn’t new. Early readers of Andrej Karpathy’s blog will remember the 2015 post featuring plausible-looking C code generated by an LSTM—a precursor of the transformer—which nonetheless amounted to little more than a parlor trick.

I remember tasking GPT-4 with rewriting a simple JavaScript program in different languages. It performed like Google Translate, instantly and passably. I likened ChatGPT to the monkey’s paw from the eponymous horror story, the accursed object that grants your wishes but not without casualties. In dutiful pursuit of the given objective—and only that objective—LLMs produced a locally correct solution while mangling essential code around it.

Then, in early 2025, AI command-line tools were released. At first, what I saw wasn’t so much a leap in quality as a greater eagerness to volunteer help. Then they improved—at this point I should quote Hemingway’s line about how one goes bankrupt—gradually, then suddenly. Within a year, they became massively, belittlingly better at my job.

Some engineers still labor a great deal to remain unimpressed, quibbling about how “it’s good at X but not yet at Y.” Please. The flaws are there, sure, and guardrails are essential. But a better heuristic for their usefulness would be: If you had to choose, for the rest of your life, between pair-programming with a competent colleague and these tools, which would you want? Any programmer worth their salt must acknowledge that they won’t be as useful to their colleague as these tools are. Even if the colleague were Linus Torvalds, I would choose the LLMs, since they won’t give me hell for asking dumb questions.

For someone who feels no allegiance to tech, the usefulness of these coding tools triggers contradictory emotions. I find ChatGPT’s indecorous entry into the literary world intolerable, and I remember feeling a creeping nihilism about the writing profession when it was released. I know firsthand what a reliance on AI can do. As I’ve come to rely more on coding tools to hand me solutions, my ability to code has gone through a swift and decisive decline. Depend on it to write sentences and it will, like a neurodegenerative disease, blunt the finer senses that writers must protect the way pianists protect their hands.

Earlier this year, it was reported that more than 90 percent of code at companies like Anthropic and Coinbase is written with AI. As most people are prompting rather than hand-coding, much of modern software engineering has become machine-operated, not unlike Mitch McConnell these days.

While this may not be the end of programming, it’ll be the end of programmers. To keep calling those in this profession “programmers” would be incorrect, since the entity that does the programming is a platoon of agents, with humans at desks prompting something out of them. A more flattering term might be meta-programming, but it’d be more accurate to say that programmers have become operators of sorts.

An operator is someone a degree removed from the thing they manipulate. (Unlike someone chipping at rock with a pickaxe, an operator of mining equipment never touches it.) Perhaps one way to describe how the nature of the profession has changed is that it’s as if cooks had stopped making food themselves and taken to writing recipes instead. If you’ve spent any time around AI Twitter, you might be forgiven for thinking that software engineering is now about looking impressed by markdown files.

This isn’t necessarily something to be defeatist about, and I’m not pessimistic about the quality of engineering. When coding agents were first released, I wasn’t sure whether this was just another natural progression, since the story of programming is a story of ever higher levels of abstraction. From bytes to assembly to higher-level languages like Python, we’ve built tools to domesticate the complexity. And with AI, we’ve finally handed off tasks that are constitutionally unfit for the human brain.

But as programmers cede control over the final unit of manipulation, programming no longer qualifies as a craft. Hence my disagreement with Simon Willison, otherwise a voice of reason among witless clout chasers in AI discourse, who wrote, “Quitting programming as a career right now because of LLMs would be like quitting carpentry as a career thanks to the invention of the table saw.” With all due respect, this is a flawed analogy, as carpenters working with a table saw are still craftspeople.

This means there won’t be great individual programmers in the way that someone like Brian W. Kernighan was: the programmer laureate who cowrote what is possibly the greatest book on programming, The C Programming Language, by which we measure programming languages to this day. (It is the book that popularized the “Hello, World” convention.)

Another loss is that programming no longer induces the flow state—or it induces a shallow sort of flow at best. There’s still a certain “hookedness” to the activity, but whereas tackling a gnarly bug once meant begging every individual neuron in my brain to do its part, the work has become suspiciously effortless. That hookedness may be closer to what the cultural anthropologist Natasha Dow Schüll called, in her 2012 book Addiction by Design, the “machine zone,” the trancelike state of the Las Vegas gambler.

Even though I’ve stopped coding myself, I find I’ve been producing more code than ever before. The features I’d built for my open source editor in the past were unserious enough that I could bang out a quick program on a weekend afternoon or two. But with Claude Code, I’ve become addicted to making what has essentially become a heavyweight word processor, a juiced-up Scrivener. (My changelog tells me I’ve built 239 features and 17 extensions, mapped to 141 keyboard shortcuts.)

One Sunday this summer, I was disconcerted to discover that I’d spent 10 hours in Claude Code. How do I know? I used Claude Code to build a time-tracker for Claude Code usage. I resolved to cut back. I spent eight hours the next day. This compelled me to write a program, using Claude Code, to block myself from Claude Code except for two days a week, which I of course tried to undo with Claude Code, only to be valiantly defended by Claude Code.

With this borrowed competence has come a total loss of interest in learning new languages. A couple of years ago, an iOS meditation timer app I’d used for years started nudging me with ads, and I resolved to one day learn Swift, the language Apple created for iPhone and Mac apps, and build a simple version myself. But I now know that’s never going to happen, because I’ve already built it knowing zilch about Swift syntax, along with 12 more iOS and Mac apps: The machine helped me fork the long-unmaintained SelfControl into a more granular internet blocker; by reverse-engineering a clipboard manager—I was never sure it wasn’t leaking my input—it built a new one with an auditable security profile.

I suspect, going forward, that I’ll still mess around with code, but no longer as a professional. Recently, it became clear that I needed to quit my day job as a programmer to finish a book I’d set out to write. I once told a writer friend that I’d try to be a mole in this industry as long as I was in it. I did get to see some things while I stuck it out and, along the way, coaxed that green card from my employer.

I’m not too sorry to leave this profession at this moment of nuttiness. But I’ll admit that, after all those languages I enlisted to make my detour more tolerable, I came to appreciate the pleasures of polyglot programming. As humans cede control to the machine, it’ll be a joy lost to us all.
