📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

MIT Tech Review Full Report

Avi Davidson lost his left hand as a teenager. Since then, he’s cycled through a few prosthetics. But his latest is different—it’s wired up to nerves in the upper part of his forearm and allows him to feel pressure. For the first time in years, he can use his left limb to handle delicate objects like grapes, eggs, and a loved one’s hand.

Davidson, who is taking part in a clinical trial run by the University of Utah, is the ninth person to have this experimental device implanted in his arm. But he’s the first to take it home and use it independently.

The hand is part of the bionic LUKE arm, named after Luke Skywalker of Star Wars , who was fitted with a lifelike prosthetic arm after his own was light-sabered off . The arm was developed in the 2000s , initially trialed in 2014 , and approved by the FDA that same year. The trial that Davidson is enrolled in isn’t so focused on the arm itself but on a new way to connect it to a person’s nervous system. The connection allows users to control movements and receive feedback about how much pressure they’re applying.

Researchers hope that Davidson’s experiences will help them answer some long-standing questions about the technology, including how useful it really is in day-to-day life. They want to know when the device is helpful and where it might fail. They also want to learn how it might affect Davidson’s well-being as well as his brain and nervous system.

In 2009, when he was 16, Davidson climbed a telephone pole to get the best shot of a sunset. “I climbed about 35 feet up,” he recalls. But he lost his footing as he tried to descend. He grabbed for a wire, which ended up electrocuting him, before he fell to the ground.

The accident resulted in second- and third-degree burns over 37% of his body, says Davidson, “and I broke my back in approximately 50 separate locations.” He also broke eight ribs, and his spinal cord was severed. He’s pretty sure he broke his left wrist, too. But around 10 days later, his left hand—his dominant hand, and the one he’d grabbed the wire with—had started to develop gangrene. It was amputated.

Davidson was ultimately in a coma for around a month. “Once I woke up, they explained to me: You’re paralyzed, you’re never going to walk again, and also, we took your left hand,” he recalls. “A bit of an inconvenience at 16, to say the least.”

Davidson, now 33 and a trauma therapist, went on to have physical therapy and adjusted to life in his changed body. He’s tried a range of prosthetic hands. The first one, which he received a year and a half after his injury, was a basic hook that allowed him to pick things up. It wasn’t fancy, but it gave him “a psychological feeling of returning to being whole,” he says.

The hands he’s tried since have been more advanced—some have allowed him to control individual fingers with muscle movements, for example. Some have been covered by his medical insurance, which allows for replacements around every five years, he says. Others he’s had to buy himself. He bought his third prosthetic hand, for example, from eBay. “It’s funny to think I bought a secondhand second hand,” he says.

A chance encounter with a scientist at a supermarket introduced Davidson to the research team at the University of Utah, which specialized in researching devices that plug into the body. Since 2013, the team had been running a trial of a system that could connect with the nerves of a residual limb, allowing an amputee to control a bionic one. Davidson turned out to be an ideal candidate, says Jake George, director of the university’s neurorobotics lab .

Davidson, who was keen to take part, first had to undergo what George describes as “pretty extensive” physical and psychological evaluations. Not only did he have to be well enough for surgery, but the team also scanned his brain to make sure that he still had a mental representation of his left hand—suggesting he’d be able to move a bionic one. They also ran tests to check that the nerves in his arm were working.

A therapist evaluated Davidson’s motivations for taking part in the trial. “Our patients aren’t being compensated, they don’t get to keep the device, and they go through two surgeries—the surgery to get it in and the surgery to take it out,” says George. “Avi’s doing this because he wants to help others.”

In late January of this year, Davidson flew from Florida to Utah to undergo his first surgery. It was a fairly complicated procedure involving engineers as well as a full surgical team. “We [researchers] stand in the back of the room and basically answer the question when the surgeon asks, ‘Where do I put the damn thing?’” says George. The surgeon exposed three nerves in Davidson’s arm and implanted an electrode array containing 100 needles in each one.

Nerves are floppy, so pushing electrodes into them doesn’t do much, says George. Instead, the team connected the electrodes to the nerves using what he describes as a “compressed-air gun.” “You shoot the grid of needles into the nerve at a very, very high velocity,” he says.

The team also inserted 24 electrodes into the muscles around the nerves. Wires for each of the nerve arrays and for pairs of muscle electrodes were left protruding from Davidson’s skin. After the surgery, he was offered some time to recover. “By day two, I’m like ‘No, let’s get me on the machines,’” he says.

That next step involved training a computer system to essentially translate the electrical activity in Davidson’s nerves into signals for bionic hand and wrist movements.

To get it up and running, the team had to train the device in two directions. The first involved converting nerve activity into hand, wrist, and finger movements. The researchers did this by listening in on the signals collected from the hundreds of electrical connection points in Davidson’s arm while he thought about various movements. That part was easy, says George: “It takes a matter of about five minutes.”

The second direction—linking the electrodes to sensations felt by the prosthetic—is much more time-consuming. Team members would stimulate each of the electrode connection points in turn, asking Davidson if he felt anything. They’d then turn up the signal at each of those points to see the effect. “It’s like a three-day process,” says George.

Davidson’s wires have since been connected and simplified, says George, who describes the process as “more or less like plugging in an HDMI cord or a USB-C cord.” When he wants to use his prosthetic, Davidson must first put it on, connect it to the wires, and then initiate it using a small computer.

He’s also had to undergo extensive physical therapy to learn how to control the hand. After all, it’s nothing like the prostheses he’s used in the past. “It’s almost like learning a different language with this kind of technology,” says Leanne Seckinger, an occupational therapist at the University of Utah Health System, who works with Davidson.

At first, she and Davidson worked on learning to hold and stabilize objects. They focused on Davidson’s personal goals—he wanted to be able to cook at home, so the pair worked on strategies to hold and manipulate utensils, prepare and chop ingredients, and carry plates and bowls. “He was able to crack an egg,” says Seckinger. “He made an omelet.”

Davidson has been using the hand at home since July 10, and he has noticed improvements. At first it took him a few minutes to write his name; now he can manage it in seconds. “The things I’m able to do with it now are tremendous, comparatively to where I was at initially,” he says.

Today, he says, he wears the hand around six days a week. He changes the batteries once or twice during the day and takes it off overnight. It’s heavy, weighing in at around five pounds, he says.

The hand can be useful, but it has also proved surprising. Sometimes, Davidson feels pain in it. This could be what’s known as “phantom pain”—a symptom experienced by some people who have lost a limb.

Curiously, he also says he experiences wrist strain. Davidson says he’s been getting a stiffness in his prosthetic wrist similar to what he experiences when he’s been typing for a long time with his flesh-and-blood right hand. He’s also realized that he can relieve the tension by pulling the bionic fingers back to stretch out his prosthetic wrist. “It’s weird,” he says.

The phenomenon “makes no sense physiologically,” says Chris Duncan, a rehabilitation doctor and chief medical officer at BIOS, a neurotechnology company that is supporting the trial. Stretching a typical hand pulls on tendons that extend down the forearm. There aren’t any tendons in a bionic arm—Davidson’s “stretches” are just moving actuators, says Duncan. He doesn’t know why it’s helpful but suspects it might have something to do with how the hand is represented in his mind. “What Avi’s experiencing is kind of like uncharted territory, honestly,” he says.

The research team is currently planning a bigger study and hopes to get the two-way sensation device approved by the FDA so that it can be covered by health insurance. “They need to understand that this is a useful new technological paradigm that will bring value to patients,” says Duncan. Team members also hope the technology will allow users to operate other devices, such as remote controls and wheelchairs.

Davidson will have his implants and the hand for a couple more months at least. But the trial was designed to run for “at least” one year, and that year will be up by the end of January 2027. “I’m not quite sure what happens after that,” he says.

George hopes the trial could be extended, but that will depend on funding. “From our perspective, we’d like to go as long as we can,” he says. “We don’t want to leave Avi without support.”

Davidson isn’t too worried about what comes next, though. “I went into this knowing that this was not something that I get to keep,” he says. “I am not going to benefit from this … I wanted to be a part of this in the context of being able to help push the technology forward.”
