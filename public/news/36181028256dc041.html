📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

The San Francisco Standard Full Report

A venture capitalist’s glossy quarterly wants to be The New Yorker for tech billionaires.

Many media veterans doubt the profiles accomplish their subjects’ public relations goals. “It’s all well and good for the tech industry media bubble to have its own in-flight magazine, but audiences aren’t stupid,” Nilay Patel, editor-in-chief of The Verge, said in an email. “Writing a massive profile of Mark Zuckerberg in which … his staggering unpopularity with everyday Americans is treated as an unfortunate byproduct of his ambition just isn’t going to convince anyone of anything.

“If Mark actually wanted to be understood as he says he does, he’d sit down for a truly adversarial journalistic interview and try to make his case,” Patel added. “But I don’t think he can take the risk.”

Colossus is the brainchild of Patrick O’Shaughnessy, a Connecticut-raised heir to an oil fortune, who went to work for his father James’ firm, O’Shaughnessy Asset Management, in 2007. Patrick took over the company in 2018 and sold it four years later to Franklin Templeton. In 2016, he launched a podcast called “Invest Like the Best,” borrowing the title of a book his father published in 1994.

His guests on the podcast have included Berkshire Hathaway Vice Chairman Charlie Munger and OpenAI CEO Sam Altman. In 2020, building on the success of “Invest Like the Best,” which O’Shaughnessy has said gets north of 100,000 downloads per episode, he launched a podcast network called Colossus, which now has shows like “Business Breakdowns” and “Founders.” The same year, he spun up a venture capital firm, Positive Sum.

“It’s actually great that venture capitalists are now writing all of the founder hagiography, because it means that when, inevitably, one of these people goes to prison, the blowback will not affect mainstream journalists,” said Casey Newton, founder of Platformer and cohost of the new NPR podcast, “Machine Gods.” “It will just affect the venture class, and they can take it.”

But Colossus is the first in this wave of VC-sponsored media to make a serious effort to ape the writing and photography of glossy magazines. “This kind of wet kiss has always existed, but it was never done this artfully,” said Samtani. “To be super clear, it is not journalism. It is positioned, oftentimes, as journalism. They hire journalists, but it is not journalism. Think of it as high-grade, tier-one content marketing.”

The upside is less for the magazine itself, Samtani explained, than for O’Shaughnessy’s venture firm. “Their economics are not media company economics. This is at least partly a clout exercise and an access game, and it’s a very smart one,” he said. “Media is an incredible way to create connections and develop your own influence.”

Notably, O’Shaughnessy failed to absorb the first paragraph of Remnick’s introduction, which establishes the form’s ethos. He wrote that The New Yorker’s founding editor, Harold Ross, “prized intimacy and wit over biographical completeness or, God forbid, unabashed hero worship. Ross told his writers and editors that, above all, he wanted to get away from what he was reading in the other magazines — all the ‘Horatio Alger’ stuff.”

The typical Colossus profile follows the classic Alger trajectory: scrappy kid overcomes adversity to achieve success. But Colossus adds a twist befitting our nepo-baby era. Its subjects tend to be born not in rags but with relative riches. Their great accomplishment is to accumulate even more money than their well-off parents.

To fit Kushner’s story to the Alger narrative, Stern spans three generations to his beloved grandmother Rae, who survived the Holocaust. He glosses over the less savory parts of the Kushner family history — among them, his father Charles hiring a prostitute to entrap his brother-in-law.

With Zuckerberg, the Colossus editor focuses on Mark’s father’s quaint dentist office and both parents’ authentic New York accents, spelling out their pronunciation. He skips mentioning Phillips Exeter, the elite prep school that polished Zuckerberg’s Latin and nurtured his sense of imperial destiny.

The secondary sources are routinely impressive. The Kushner profile includes comments from Altman, Demis Hassabis, Jony Ive, John Collison, and the former dean of Harvard Business School. But it’s rare to find independent perspectives; almost everyone quoted by name has a financial relationship with the subject. And few of Colossus’ sources ever criticize or question the motivations of the subject.

In the magazine’s pages, we’re invited to understand and relate to these wounded colossi, vulnerable hulks persevering on the path to greatness through a nearly insurmountable accumulation of wounds. (Think of St. Sebastian, beatific with all the arrows in him.) Stern praises Kushner’s “mostly vindicated admirers” and castigates his “increasingly sheepish critics.” Zuckerberg’s detractors suffer from “Zuckerberg Derangement Syndrome,” persecuting “the most persistent scapegoat of the 21st century.”

When journalists talk about Stern and his Colossus colleagues, you sometimes hear traces of pity. They know what the marketplace for magazine journalism looks like today — there are few paying customers for profiles that actually delve into the darker corners of celebrity businesspeople’s psyches. “I’m sure that there is some desire by the writers writing these pieces to go further than the actual piece goes,” said Samtani.

In interviews, Stern occasionally expresses mild revulsion toward technology investors. “I had always found the personality types kind of annoying,” he told Meservey. “As a class of people, these people feel like they’re full of shit.” Yet such sentiments are rarely reflected in his work in the magazine.

Other practitioners suspect Colossus writers are simply wasting their time. Zuckerberg and oligarchs of his ilk, Newton said, make for bad interviews because “most important information about them is already public. You’ve seen the products. You’ve seen what they do to people. You’ve seen the fact that the company will often try to evade making [its products] safer or reckon[ing] with its role in society.”

Newton questions the value of trying to understand or analyze their interior lives. “Against that backdrop, what kind of kid was Mark Zuckerberg? It’s like, who fucking cares?” he said. “I’m sorry. It doesn’t matter.”
