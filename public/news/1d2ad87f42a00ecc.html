HomeView Teardown: A Surprising Step Backward

✍️ By Carsten Frauenheim, Elizabeth Chamberlain, and Shahram Mokhtari

iFixit's teardown of the Apple HomeView reveals a heavy reliance on structural adhesives for its display, speakers, and logic board, marking a regressive step in modular design. Despite recent improvements in repairability across iPad and HomePod lines, HomeView’s integration forces high-risk disassembly that frequently leads to component failure.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] iFixit; Apple Inc. (Laboratory teardown): High-strength adhesive usage prevents non-destructive access to display and logic board
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Prioritization of tight integration and structural rigidity over serviceability in the new smart home product architecture
  • [Key Fact Detail] HomeView uses industrial-grade adhesives, regressing on recent repairability progress.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] Shift in design strategy highlights potential conflict between tight hardware integration and right-to-repair standards.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

iFixit News Full Report

Apple’s first iPad-stuck-to-a-HomePod smarthome hub landed on our teardown table this week, and we figured we knew what we were getting into. We’ve taken apart plenty of iPads and HomePods. And lately, Apple has actually been getting better at making them repairable.

The original HomePod took a hacksaw to open, but by the second generation, we could get inside without destroying anything. Even the notoriously glue-heavy iPad has made progress: easier battery access, replaceable USB-C ports, and finally, official repair manuals. We were starting to think Apple had learned a few lessons.

Then we met HomeView. A 7-inch iPad display up top. A HomePod mini down below. A few screws holding the two together.

But that was an oversimplification. By the time we got it apart, we had ripped a connector, snapped a speaker in two, and put more force on the poor logic board than any of us were comfortable with. The base showed some promising signs of HomePod DNA, but the display was a different story entirely.

So what happened? How did a company that’s been making real repairability improvements produce a device that’s so dead set against being fixed?

We Hoped It’d Be Easy

Before picking up a tool, we put the HomeView through our Lumafield Neptune CT scanner. At first glance, things looked pretty encouraging.

There’s a small circuit board near the top, flanked by antennas on either side, with what appear to be large speakers mounted toward the bottom.

The base is more densely packed, which made sense to us, since we expected to find HomePod mini guts in there and not much more besides maybe added weight.

A central pivot connects the display and the base, which look like two distinct assemblies. Surely we’d be able to separate them if one half needed replacing?

There was one familiar warning sign. Just like in an iPad, getting to anything behind the display meant going through the fragile front glass first.

Still, we’d dealt with that plenty of times before. What we hadn’t bargained for was just how determined Apple was to keep everything else from coming apart.

Glued Tighter Than an iPad?

We’ve opened up enough iPads to know the basics: heat up the display, soften the adhesive, and carefully lift the screen.

The display fit our heat mat perfectly, but the aluminum enclosure was very good at carrying that heat away. The extra thermal mass meant for quite a long wait. Even so, the adhesive held on harder than on any Apple product we’ve opened recently. Why are we having an even harder time getting into something that spends its life sitting on a kitchen counter?

Once we finally lifted the display, we were quick to confirm that most of the internal space is occupied by those two enormous speakers. More on those later.

But first, an oddity. With the screen out of the way, we can get to the bracket covering the display’s press connector. One side of that bracket is held down with a Phillips screw. The other side uses Torx.

Could Apple have made both screws Phillips, or both Torx? Absolutely. Whether you care about efficient manufacturing or DIY repair, two driver types for a single bracket is a poor choice. Apple did this for years on iPhones. We didn’t get it then, and we don’t get it now.

It wasn’t the last time, either. The coaxial connectors for the two antennas are secured with Phillips screws, and then the camera bracket needs a larger Torx bit, so we spent the whole teardown juggling bits for no good reason.

Unnecessary bit changes are annoying, sure, but we can live with them. It’s when we try to disconnect those big speakers that things start to go seriously wrong.

Speaker Connectors Made of Sand

With the display out of the way, the logic board looked reasonably accessible. All we had to do was disconnect everything that leads to it, beginning with the big left speaker.

There was some light adhesive around the edges of the connector. Nothing we haven’t seen before. We scraped it away and pried, expecting the glue to give. It didn’t. The leads ripped right out of the plastic housing, which stayed put as if it had been superglued to the board.

We chalked that up to operator error and came back to the right speaker’s connector later with more caution. This time, we scraped away the glue on top, even used a little heat, and it still wouldn’t budge. When that didn’t work, we took a knife to the seams of the connector. Even then, we couldn’t get it out without damage. These brittle plastic connectors are designed to go in, stay in, and not be removed.

Unfortunately, we’ve seen these types of connectors before. Just-as-brittle examples that come to mind are used for Steam Deck haptics and the M4 Mac mini’s power button.

This is a shame. Kudos, Apple, for supergluing down a known-bad cable connector design on a device that’s sure to … uhhh … sit on the counter and never move. Brilliant. Friends, this is low hanging fruit.

Keeping the Chip Cool (and the Logic Board Stuck)

With the cables ripped off, we figured the worst was behind us and the logic board would lift right out. But when we started lifting it up and met resistance, we scratched our heads.

Had we missed a screw? We popped off the top shield to check. Nope, just a ton of thermal paste. So we kept prying, putting a lot of force on a very thin board, and it did not feel right.

We found out why. Turns out there’s a second clipped-on shield underneath the board that carries heat into the back of the enclosure. That shield is then thoroughly glued in place.

Apple turned the entire aluminum chassis into a heat sink, which is a smart design. But that thermal bond also makes removing the logic board a remarkably risky operation. A design that’s straightforward to assemble at the factory becomes a headache for anyone trying to service it.

A Speaker Replacement Shouldn’t Break the Speaker

Remember those enormous speakers we found behind the display? Speakers are mechanical components, with moving parts that can wear out or fail. Given how much space Apple dedicated to them, we figured they should be easy, right? Right?

We poured so much isopropyl alcohol behind them that the excess dripped out through the speaker grilles and onto the teardown table. We tried a pick, then a spudger, then a metal pry tool that bent under the force it took to lift a corner. Then the speaker snapped in two.

Flip the broken half over and the problem is plain. Thick pads of adhesive cover the underside. They were soaked in alcohol and still wouldn’t let go.

This is a solved problem. Most laptop makers mount their speakers with screws and isolation grommets. Or even just isolation grommets. Even Apple’s own MacBook team uses a much lighter adhesive to secure its speakers. And those are devices that have to survive being jostled around in backpacks. Surely we can manage something less permanent for a kitchen countertop.

Microphones on each side came out without any surprises, thankfully. A third mic hides under a bracket at the top, and for whatever reason that bracket is both screwed in and glued down with a thin strip of adhesive. Such glue-happy designers!

With the display internals finally out of the way, we could turn our attention to what had looked like one of the most promising aspects of HomeView’s design: its separate speaker base. HomeView is available in a wall-mount version without the speaker base, which made us further hopeful that the base could be semi-modular.

But getting the two halves apart was harder than we anticipated. Multiple interconnect cables run from the display to the base. The plate covering the tunnel between the two is glued more aggressively than we expected (at this point, that’s on us). It does come away, though, along with a flex cable leading to the bottom edge that appears to house a temperature sensor.

Someone Else Designed The Base

After our experience with the display, the base was (mostly) a breath of fresh air.

The base is built a lot like a HomePod, so the bottom non-slip pad peels away easily. Four Torx screws release the bottom plate, and a taped-in press connector in the corner frees a flex cable leading to the USB-C port.

And would you look at that! A modular USB-C port. Some of those hard-earned HomePod repair lessons made it into the HomeView after all.

Don’t celebrate yet. Pulling out the woofer, we were left wondering how anyone connected its extremely short cable to the board underneath. We could barely reach the release with a pry tool, and we have no idea how we’d reconnect it during reassembly. There’s plenty of room in here for a longer, coiled cable.

Two passive speakers mount inside an admittedly beautiful metal frame. They use the air pressure generated by the woofer to produce deeper bass without additional powered speakers. They’re modular, too. They’d even be easy to replace, if not for that crazy short woofer cable. And the power supply board underneath pops free after a few screws and a couple of connectors. After everything we went through upstairs, removing a circuit board without fighting adhesive felt positively luxurious.

Now, if you were to start this entire disassembly at the base, you’d be met with yet another design decision headscratcher once you got down to the screws holding the pivot to the screen. These large Torx screws are torqued and threadlocked. They’re also at an inaccessible off-angle—you’d need a specifically-sized ratchet to get them out.

To make things worse, Apple fed the interconnect cables through the pivot before these screws were installed at the factory, too, meaning that you literally cannot separate the two halves of HomeView without either specialized tools or completely (and most likely destructively) disassembling the screen side. This is bad for regular service as well as the cool future mods folks will inevitably want to do to this thing.

Repair Was Not on the Whiteboard

The HomeView is an absolute bear to take apart, and what made it so rough was watching the gleeful optimism we had when we started get crushed over and over. Designing for repair is an intentional act, and here, Apple didn’t bother.

We kept running into choices that made repairs unreasonably and unnecessarily difficult. Different bit types on jobs that only needed one. Adhesive on connectors, brackets, speakers, and the logic board, in a product that will never be dropped out of a pocket. Screws that are tantalizingly close to being accessible with an ordinary screwdriver.

The base is better than the screen half, and it does resemble the inside of a HomePod. Even there, repair takes a back seat to short cables and tight quarters.

Many of these design choices make sense if you’re only thinking about how to put something together at the factory. Taking it apart and putting it back together years later requires some additional thought.

How a product treats its owner years down the road says a lot about how much the company cared when it was designed. Every feature in a product exists because someone fought for it in a meeting. Nobody at Apple fought for the day a HomeView breaks.

Apple can do better. Their other modern products, on sale at the exact same time as this, prove it. That’s what makes the HomeView such a disappointment.

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

The design shift underscores Apple's preference for structural integrity over serviceability in complex, multi-modular smart devices. This regression suggests that for highly integrated terminals, Apple continues to prioritize manufacturing-side yield and aesthetic rigidity, effectively creating a barrier for third-party repair markets and complicating product lifecycle management.
