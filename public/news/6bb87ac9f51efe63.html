📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

iFixit News Full Report

“Fancy new display” makes our screwdrivers nervous, and Apple’s latest MacBook has the fanciest display yet. The MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR is the first-ever touchscreen MacBook. It’s got a tandem OLED panel that hits 120 Hz and peaks at 2,000 nits, powered by your choice of an M5 Pro or M5 Max chip.

And somehow, with a touchscreen and a redesigned hinge, this thing weighs 1.38 kg (three pounds). That’s 170 grams (half a pound) lighter than the M6 14-inch Pro.

“Lighter” also strikes fear in the heart of our screwdrivers: in pursuit of lightness, designers often leave repairability on the cutting room floor.

The good news? The new lid is just as easy to replace as last year. And we didn’t experience the problems we saw with TouchID calibration last year. But it’s not all good news. The fans and USB-C ports are still buried under the logic board.

We were maybe most excited to dig into the most useless feature of this display: the glowing apple on the back.

On old MacBooks, light from the LCD backlight shone through an apple-shaped window in the lid. But the PROLED’s pixels make their own light. So, yes, Apple waited until the first MacBook Pro without a backlight to bring back the backlit logo.

For a display repair, the lid comes off as one assembly. The familiar antenna bar still sits in the way of the hinges, but the display cable bracketry is slightly streamlined and there’s no separate lid-angle sensor to deal with.

The PROLED and M6 have nearly indistinguishable three-screw hinges. The difference is easier to feel than to see: a gentle tap on the PROLED results in a bit less wobble. It probably comes down to minor spring tuning, but this change is definitely mainly hype. We also couldn’t find any similarities to the iPhone Duo’s hinge tech, as was briefly rumored.

There are few laptops on the market that make it possible to replace a display without replacing the whole upper lid. Framework Laptop, for instance, has a cool magnetic bezel with screws underneath that release the panel, no need to throw away a working camera or pay for a new one. Some models of Framework Laptop even enable a display upgrade (e.g., from 2.2K to 2.8K) this way.

But generally, we’re okay with “replace the whole lid to replace the display” as the path to display repairs, especially with the changes in this design that make whole-lid replacement easier.

Still, we were curious: Could we get the panel out in one piece? Short answer, no. It seems theoretically possible, but getting inside that lid cost us the panel.

We warmed it from the back and worked a thin pick into the corner. The rubber trim lifted. Then the glass delaminated, cracking the OLED and capacitive layers underneath.

That gave us a look at two light, rigid foam pads flanking the logo. Our best guess is they give the OLED a little backing when you press on the glass.

Under a taped patch in the middle we found four LEDs, all in a strip along one edge. The light hits a reflective layer and passes through a diffuser, turning four little points of light into one evenly glowing apple. The logo itself has a mirror-like coating that looks shiny with the lights off.

Cool, but this is an insane repair path for reaching the glowing logo. If yours ever goes dark, you might see if you can live with a regular Apple. We’d be very interested to see if anyone successfully pulls off an LED mod.

Now that we’ve got the display off, let’s take a closer look at how it works.

For comparison, look at the 2025 M5 MacBook Pro’s Liquid Retina XDR display under our Evident DSX2000 microscope and you’ll see red, green, and blue stripes. They filter light from a backlight. On the OLED, each little patch makes its own light. That lets the screen display a bright star on a pitch-black sky without a halo around it.

The PROLED looks totally different under the scope. Look how much room blue gets compared to red and green.

Blue has always been tricky for OLEDs: the materials that make deep blue efficiently tend to wear out faster. Give blue a bigger patch and you can get the same amount of light while putting less strain on each bit of material.

There are actually two light-emitting layers in this OLED, one behind the other. Together they can deliver the same brightness with less current needed for each, or push the display to a higher peak. That’s what makes this a “tandem” OLED.

Getting parts out is only half the repair. When you put new parts in, they’ve got to work. And Apple has a long history of throwing software blocks in the way of MacBook repairs.

We wanted to know whether a touchscreen that started life in one MacBook would still accurately read a finger when we gave it a logic board from another. Apple has lots of practice with this on iPhones and iPads, so our hopes were high. On the first boot, Repair Assistant recognized the battery, display, and Touch ID sensor. A couple of key presses and a reboot later, we had True Tone, full brightness control, and a working touchscreen. Touch control needed no separate calibration.

Last year, Repair Assistant couldn’t finish calibrating Touch ID on our M5 MacBook Pro. This time we enrolled a fingerprint with the donor board installed, then we did what a repair shop borrowing a board for diagnosis would do: put the original back. The touchscreen still worked. So did Touch ID.

That’s great news for repair. But we’ll be keeping our ears open. Let us know if you experience calibration issues with parts swaps, as we’ve seen in some old MacBooks.

Swapping parts also gave us a chance to put them on a scale. Apple added a touchscreen and brought back the glowing logo, kept roughly the same battery capacity, and somehow made the whole machine lighter. We wanted to see where it found the savings.

We put the parts from the 14-inch PROLED and the base M6 on the scale. Apple trimmed some aluminum out of the lower chassis: 456 grams on the M6, 364 on the PROLED, counting the bottom cover on both. The bottom covers alone account for 44 of those 92 grams. The bigger surprise is the lid. Even with a touchscreen and its own little light show for the Apple logo, the whole display assembly came in at 384 grams, down from 485.

And the battery? The PROLED’s 72.6 Wh pack weighs 276 grams, just 2 grams lighter than the M6’s.

It still comes out with pull tabs. Ours were brand new, about as cooperative as those strips are ever going to be, and it was still hard to get them all out in one piece. We’d love to see Apple bring some of their battery innovation to the MacBook Pro: How about a screwed-down battery like the Neo? Or electrically debondable adhesive like the iPhone?

The speakers, however, actually got heavier. The pair weighs 39 grams, up from 29 on the base M6.

There are still three speakers per module. Apple says the redesigned woofers deliver 50 percent deeper bass. The trackpad lost eight grams, coming from an overall smaller size.

The rest of the design features a lot of the same design decisions, both good and bad, that we’ve seen in older models.

Four pentalobe screws hold the bottom cover on. A suction cup or quick prying pops the front clips. Inside, the PROLED looks virtually identical to the last few models of MacBook Pros with Pro or Max chips. Two fans, with board, speakers, and trackpad all in the same spot.

MagSafe comes out after two screws and a bracket. The USB-C ports look like they should too. They’re on their own little boards; we can reach the screws. But the port has to tilt to clear the case, and the logic board is exactly where that corner needs to go. Apple was way ahead of the game with modular ports on laptops. Although this MacBook design annoyance quirk isn’t new, we’re surprised we keep seeing it. The HDMI port and the SDXC card reader are soldered directly to the board, so don’t break those.

The headphone jack has a false summit of its own: Apple tucked its second screw under the logic board. Why, Apple, why??

Replacing a rattling fan sends you on the same damn trip under the logic board. Removing the logic board isn’t too bad, but getting it back in is surgery. Apple’s own manual recommends taping back every single cable so they don’t get caught. Smart, but indicative of the need for a better process.

And, speaking of the logic board, storage is still soldered down. Memory is still Unified.

The 14-inch MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR earns a 5 out of 10 for repairability, one point up from the M5 MacBook Pro.
