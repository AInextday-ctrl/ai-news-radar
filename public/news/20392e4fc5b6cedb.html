Data centre construction stocks lose $4.2tn as boom in AI trade unravels

✍️ By Clara Murray

Global manufacturing, utility, and mining firms have collectively shed $4.2 trillion in market value since June, signaling a major reversal in capital flows. This shift reflects a market correction against previous over-investment in AI 'picks and shovels' and a widening disconnect between corporate AI expenditure commitments and realized return on capital.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Financial Times Full Report

Global manufacturing, utility and mining companies have shed $4.2tn in value since June as the AI “picks and shovels” trade swings into reverse.

Companies more frequently linked by media coverage to data centre construction have been hit hardest by the sell-off, an FT analysis found, as investors’ confidence in the AI boom wobbles.

The losses wiped out almost half of the $9.4tn in value that the same companies gained in the previous 12 months as investors piled into the manufacturers of everything from jet engines adapted to power hyperscale data centres to the advanced fibre optics needed to connect AI server racks.

Caterpillar, a leading supplier of gas generators to data centres, has seen its market capitalisation slashed by $214bn, or 44 per cent, since its shares hit an all-time high on June 30, according to FactSet data.

Nuclear power company Oklo plunged 49 per cent despite signing a contract with Meta in January. Comfort Systems, which makes HVAC systems, and Corning, a fibre optics manufacturer, both lost around half of their value over the same period.

Paul Kedrosky, a venture capitalist and fellow at the MIT Institute, said investors had been wrong to start treating old-school industrial firms like semiconductor stocks. “It had become endless in discussions with hedge funds and family offices, this idea that everything was now a growth stock — which is ludicrous, of course.”

The sell-off comes amid wider market turmoil for AI-related shares, as investors question whether escalating debt-financed capital expenditures at hyperscalers and mounting political opposition to data centres in the US could lead to a bust.

That has hit industrial groups whose fortunes are increasingly bound to the build-out. The sector has been the second-worst-performing in the S&P 500 since the summer, after technology.

“There has been a lot of institutional investment piling blindly into these trades that probably fuelled an overly enthusiastic valuation on some of these [companies],” said Brett Ryan, senior US economist at Deutsche Bank.

The stock market was “finally benchmarking to what credit was already telling you” about the growth in risky financing for data centre construction, he added.

However, others argue that the companies supplying the boom were on solid foundations, with demand still vastly outstripping supply. Spending on data centre construction — excluding IT equipment and chips — hit $96bn in the US in the 12 months to September, the latest government data showed.

“In terms of the fundamentals of revenues and the potential earnings for these companies, we are still looking at a very strong prospect,” said Alex Cordovil, an analyst at Dell’Oro Group.

Wider trends such as electrification and supply chain restructuring continued to support many industrial firms’ core businesses. “The build-out is not decelerating as of yet . . . backlogs are at record highs, meaning that until 2027 . . . revenue growth is already baked in,” he added.

Richard S Warzala, chief executive of Allient, an industrial controls maker, said demand for AI was not going to decline. “They can have a moratorium on new data centre installation, but those that are already started need to be completed,” he said in an earnings call on November 5.

Third-quarter results showed strong demand. Many gas turbine manufacturers said they were taking billions of dollars in orders in the first nine months of 2026. Caterpillar reported a record $78bn backlog and said its power generation sales grew 75 per cent year-on-year, driven by data centre demand.

Siemens’ backlog of orders for its electrical equipment from data centres grew 60 per cent in 2026 to €7bn, while Eaton, which also supplies electrics, reported orders had doubled year-on-year.

Kedrosky cautioned that backlogs and orders were trailing indicators reflecting expectations, with people “double and triple ordering to protect their position in the queue given perceived scarcity”.

The impact of the data centre moratoriums and restrictions widely backed by US voters in this month’s midterm elections is still unclear.

Citi researchers said in a note published after the elections that while they expected tighter regulations to bring more volatility to the data centre industry, widespread restrictions on construction remained unlikely.

Joseph Dominguez of Constellation Energy told investors that the market movement following the election had been an “overreaction” and Democrats “care very deeply” about protecting blue-collar jobs at data centres.

Others say any bans would just shift construction to more friendly jurisdictions.

“At the end of the day, if there is demand for these things, they will get built somewhere,” said Deutsche Bank’s Ryan.

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

The market correction signals a transition from indiscriminate AI infrastructure fervor to a period of financial discipline, highlighting that energy consumption and grid strain are now critical physical constraints. Investors are re-pricing industrial assets to account for the structural inflationary pressure AI compute demand places on utility cost structures.
