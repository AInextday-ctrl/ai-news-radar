📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

CNET Full Report

The most fascinating and frustrating device I've used in a long time pushes at the edges of where headsets are heading, and glasses are evolving.

Let me tell you about a strange, fascinating and rough tweener product called Xreal Aura.

There are VR headsets, and there are smart glasses. And there’s a new class of devices that’s falling right in between. What do we call these things? I have no idea. The category includes the Meta VR Glasses coming next year and now the Aura, a collaboration by Google and Xreal.

The Xreal Aura shoots for the moon, aiming to bridge glasses and VR, and to shrink everything down on the go. It’s often fascinating, and more often frustrating. At times, it almost feels like a VR headset. At other times it feels like a superpowered version of the tethered display glasses that Xreal and several others already make, that act like wearable monitors for your phone or laptop or almost anything else.

I think it’s actually an experimental bridge to a future where phones will play nicer with advanced smart glasses. Those future glasses could create floating displays and workspaces all around you, if you wish. Consider the Aura, in a way, Google’s early attempt to get there.

Emphasis on early. Right now, the Xreal Aura’s features, OS and especially inputs feel rough to use at times and sometimes hard to get into a natural flow with. But it shows what something of this size could be capable of with more software and input-flow tweaks. And when it does work, it feels wildly like a portable VR/AR headset in a way I’ve never really seen before.

The Aura aspires to be a shrunk-down version of last year’s Samsung Galaxy XR, an expensive mixed-reality headset similar to Apple’s Vision Pro. Like the Galaxy XR, the Aura runs Android XR, a variant of Android that floats apps all around. It even runs VR apps. At times it gets close. Other times, it’s not nearly close enough.

What’s wild about the Aura is that it folds down into a case that’s truly small enough to slide into my bag. But setting up and using the Aura often involves patience and experimentation. When it works, it’s a heck of a peek at a future that’s likely around the corner. But the hoops you have to jump through to make it happen sometimes make me want to hop out.

Android XR is Google’s OS for AR/VR/AI devices and wearables, and aims to connect glasses and headsets of all shapes and sizes to Google and Gemini services and apps. The Samsung Galaxy XR, released last year, was the first. Coming up next, in just a few weeks, are Google and Samsung’s Warby Parker- and Gentle Monster-designed AI smart glasses.

The Xreal Aura is more Galaxy XR than smart glasses, in concept: It’s an AR/VR headset that you can take on the go. The glasses connect with a USB-C cable to an included processor puck that runs Android XR. It can display Android apps in both 2D and immersive 3D.

The Aura supports hand tracking and can allow full spatial movement to place floating 3D objects or play immersive games. It can float apps all around you, and you can navigate apps at the same time. You can use hand tracking to open or tap those apps, or a trackpad that’s built into the top of the processor puck. You can also pair a Bluetooth keyboard, or game controller, or a mouse, like I’m doing.

The kit tucks into an included carry case that’s smaller than the one I carry my Nintendo Switch 2 in. It’s easy to pack away for travel. I love this part of the Aura so, so much.

It’s $1,279 for the version with 256GB of storage and 12GB RAM or $1,499 for 512GB/16GB, but it’s on sale until the end of the year for $1,099/$1,299. That onboard storage is all you get, though. There’s no expandable microSD card slot. I reviewed the 256GB version.

The display tech in the Aura is an enhanced version of what’s already on Xreal’s other tethered display glasses, and similar to what Viture and TCL RayNeo use. The twin micro OLED displays are impressively bright and crisp, projected via prisms to look like they’re floating in the room with you. The “birdbath” display tech means the lenses are chunky and odd-looking, but they’re also a lot bigger and better-looking than what’s possible on display-enabled wireless glasses like Meta’s Ray-Ban Displays.

They have a 70-degree field of view, something that beats out previous generations of AR glasses like Magic Leap or the upcoming Snap Specs. The fidelity, although capping at 1,920×1,200 pixels per eye, is also really crisp and great for movie watching or games (up to 120Hz refresh). It’s enough to feel like a big movie screen is projected across the room or to lay out a virtual computer monitor, but there are clear edges where the image is still cut off compared to VR headsets (which tend to have 90 degree or better fields of view).

When running VR apps, which this can shockingly do, the graphics look sharp but narrowly framed. It’s good enough to use VR in a pinch, though: I floated through 3D Immersive Google Maps, dragging and scrolling with my fingers in the air, or played the VR role-playing game Demeo, exploring a tabletop board game dungeon, or played back 360-degree videos on YouTube. But the limits of the viewing field become a lot more stark in these modes, making me wish I had a VR headset instead. Then again, I never travel with VR headsets, and I’d travel with this.

The display’s visuals seemed to tear a few times while playing YouTube videos. Tracking, using the built-in cameras, also sometimes drifted. Mostly, the displays stayed where I put them, but sometimes they’d shift slightly out of alignment.

The processor puck means the Xreal Aura can open up most Android and Chrome-based apps I can think of, but not all of them. The Qualcommm Snapdragon Reality Elite chip on the puck seems more than zippy enough to handle whatever multiple games and apps (even VR ones) I throw at it, but not all Android apps are available: Developers can choose not to opt in, as with Netflix and Disney Plus, but they can be loaded up in-browser.

Other apps aren’t necessarily optimized for the fluid way the Aura can open an app in any size. I watched a Jets game on Paramount Plus on the Aura, and it stayed in a fixed window size. I could move closer or further away, but it wouldn’t enlarge.

The whole Aura interface feels new-ish, even if you’ve used VR. The glasses enable hand tracking that’s surprisingly OK: Tapping and pinching works, and an extended pointer can reach at a distance and click and drag or adjust windows. When my hand passes in front of the Aura’s display, it even gets overlaid with a virtual hand mapped onto mine, like VR.

There’s no eye tracking, though, something Apple Vision Pro, Samsung Galaxy XR and Meta VR Glasses do. Eye tracking is helpful: It means you can lock into something and click it without lifting your hand. Here, I found I had to lift and aim my hand a lot, like an interactive remote. Hand tracking does work somewhat below the display’s field of view, but you often won’t know if you’re interacting until that hand’s lifted up higher. This is where the Aura’s smaller-than-VR viewing area becomes a problem.

It’s particularly an issue when playing some VR games. I tried Synth Riders, a popular fitness music movement game that’s also on Android XR. I could see what was further off in front of me, but the limited viewing area meant my hands were out of sight when in my peripheral vision, so I couldn’t reach for targets near me or off to the sides.

There’s a second way to control Aura: The processor puck itself has a touchpad across its whole top. Hold it and it’s a touch remote: I can drag my finger and touch-click. On a table next to a paired keyboard, it’s a trackpad.

That’s super clever, but the trackpad’s performance ranged from OK to poor depending on the apps I ran. There are some multitouch elements too, but it still felt less effective as a headset control than I wanted.
