📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Wired Full Report

The message from the Trump administration to one of the world’s leading semiconductor companies was clear: Give the US government a chunk of your company. Right now. Or else.

Do not make the “mistake of thinking that this is a negotiation,” Commerce Department lawyer David Shapiro told the chief financial officer of the Intel Corporation in August 2025, according to internal notes from a company call contained in a federal lawsuit. “The President is expecting to announce a deal on these terms on Friday.”

Shapiro acknowledged that “the Board could choose not to accept the offer,” according to a summary of his remarks prepared for Intel’s directors. But the Trump administration official “noted that rejection will have consequences that [Shapiro] cannot predict.”

Days later, the board gave the administration what it wanted. “It is my Great Honor to report that the United States of America now fully owns and controls 10% of INTEL,” Donald Trump posted to his social platform. “The United States paid nothing for these Shares, and the Shares are now valued at approximately $11 Billion Dollars. This is a great Deal for America.”

President Trump was similarly jubilant on Monday, announcing that he had “convinced” Nvidia’s board of directors to rehire chief executive Jensen Huang. The proclamation was a shock. But it wasn’t entirely without precedent.

Fifteen months earlier, the Trump administration had interfered with the leadership decisions at another major chipmaker, court documents allege. Then, as now, allegations swirled that the company’s CEO was too close to China. Then, as now, the Trump administration dealt itself into the company’s financial future.

It’s part of a broader shift within the government in general and the Commerce Department in particular, a former member of that department tells WIRED. Out are old metrics “like creating jobs or the US being more competitive or owning more of the market share,” this ex-Trump administration official says. When dealing with private companies, a single overriding concern is: “Are you going to give back to the government in cash?”

Documents unredacted as part of a shareholder’s lawsuit against Intel’s board show how far the administration has been willing to go to get what it wants. The lawsuit’s allegations were previously reported by WP Intelligence.

“The Board, operating under the shadow of the President’s threats,” the lawsuit alleges, “caved to the government’s extortion.” A second case, asking for internal records, was filed in April by the same shareholder, Richard Paisner, against Nvidia.

“It's just straight-up coercion,” says Scott Lincicome, a vice president at the libertarian Cato Institute. “The company starts making decisions not based on what's best for the company in the short or long term, what's best for shareholders, but what's best for politics and what the government is demanding.”

“That's not what you want from any company,” he adds. “When you're talking about the world's most innovative companies and these kinds of bleeding-edge technologies that have not only huge economic implications but national security ones, then that's a huge problem.”

Representatives for Intel declined to comment for this story. The White House, Commerce Department, and Nvidia did not respond to requests for comment.

The parallels between Nvidia’s government-assisted autogolpe and Intel’s alleged forced sale aren’t exact. In the Intel case, Trump all but accused the company’s boss of being compromised by Beijing; in the Nvidia situation, the president reportedly dismissed concerns from his own intelligence agencies about the executive’s connections to China. But both instances show a growing fusion of private enterprise and public office.

During the Biden administration, Intel won awards for up to $11 billion in government grants and contracts, much of it under the CHIPS Act, which was meant to help build semiconductor manufacturing and assembly plants in America and bolster domestic research.

At the time, there was some grumbling inside the Commerce Department, which administered the CHIPS and Science Act grants, about funding what was seen as a “failing incumbent” in the semiconductor space, says the former official. (Intel had, in recent years, fallen on hard times.) But by March 2025, Intel had received about $2.2 billion of the grants. That’s when President Trump told Congress that the CHIPS Act was “a horrible, horrible thing.” The rest of Intel’s money was put on hold, tied to hitting certain milestones, while the administration said it was renegotiating the deals.

The same month Lip-Bu Tan was hired as Intel’s new CEO. Soon after, his ties to China made him a Trump target, when Reuters reported that Tan had invested in “hundreds of Chinese tech firms,” some with connections to Beijing's military. Tan’s former firm, Cadence Design Systems, pleaded guilty to violating US sanctions by selling semiconductor designs to a restricted Chinese military university, and agreed to pay more than $140 million in fines. Tan was never personally charged, but the violations happened while he was the CEO.

Two weeks later, the president was watching Maria Bartiromo’s show on the Fox Business Network. It mentioned a letter a senator had sent Intel about the China ties. Trump posted, "The CEO of INTEL is highly CONFLICTED and must resign, immediately. There is no other solution to this problem."

Tan went to the Oval Office on August 11 to see if there was, in fact, another solution. He got 40 minutes with the president, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent.

Tan claimed his experience in China was a strength, not a detriment. According to the book Regime Change, Tan argued that there was no way he could’ve known about every one of the deals his many companies made; Trump, who had his own sprawling business empire, surely knew what that was like. Tan promised to give up his holdings in China, according to a letter Tan shared with Intel’s board, and put any proceeds into a blind trust.

“The primary goal was to eliminate any worries about Lip-Bu himself, his Cadence background and his investments with China,” according to a source with knowledge of the matter. But there were major financial concerns for Intel, too. “We had declining revenues and needed money.”

Neither Tan’s letter, excerpted in the complaint, nor Trump’s social media posts praising the CEO after the meeting mentioned anything about giving the government a chunk of the company. Yet Trump later insisted that a deal had been cooked up right then and there. “He came in, he saw me, we talked for a while. I liked him a lot, I thought he was very good,” the president told the Wall Street Journal. “I said, ‘You know what? I think the United States should be given 10% of Intel.’ And he said, ‘I would consider that.’”

“I should’ve asked for more. It was so easy,” Trump later recalled.

According to the court filing’s review of Intel’s internal documents, Shapiro—a former partner at the New York law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz—made a verbal offer on August 18. Ultimately, in exchange for the cut of Intel, the Trump administration would free up the $8.9 billion US government grants that it had already won. They wanted about a tenth of the company’s stock for free, in exchange for Washington keeping the commitment it had already made.

Then came the call in which Shapiro insisted there was no room for negotiation. “The board needs to consider,” he said, according to Intel’s summary of his remarks included in the court documents, the “benefits of having a friend in this administration and what the administration can do to help Intel (including with customers).”

Intel’s board met, and decided they hated the deal. “The economic terms that had been

proposed [by] the Administration were not, in their current form, attractive to the company,” minutes of the meeting, reprinted in the lawsuit, noted.

But they went ahead with it anyway. Paisner’s lawyers say in the court documents the board took the offer thrust forward by the administration. Intel’s attorneys disagree, arguing in their motion to dismiss that there was some back-and-forth deal-making, and it made the agreement more favorable to the company. The grants became “immediately available” while “strategically aligning the interests of Intel’s stockholders and the US Government.”

On August 25, Lutnick released a video with Tan, sitting side-by-side on cushioned chairs. “I really look forward to have the US government be my shareholder,” Tan turned to Lutnick and said. “And we can work together and make Intel great again.”

Inside the company, the video was “a surprise to everyone,” according to a source with direct knowledge of the matter. Some employees joked that it was reminiscent of a “hostage video.”

Not long after that, Nvidia—led by Tan’s friend Jensen Huang—made a $5 billion investment in Intel. “I love seeing their stock price go up, making the USA RICHER, AND RICHER,” Trump posted. “Who would not want to make deals like that?”

Deals, plural. On the day Tan was hauled to the Oval Office, Trump announced that his administration had been negotiating separately with Huang. The Nvidia leader was beyond eager to sell more of his semiconductors to China. “And I said, if I'm going to do that, I want you to pay us as a country something,’” Trump said. They settled on 15 percent of all sales of the H20 model chip. Months after that, they cut a second deal: a 25 percent bounty on sales of Nvidia’s even-more advanced H200 chips.

In their lawsuit against Nvidia, Paisner’s attorneys argue the surcharges amount to “unlawful taxes,” and that the board’s “decisions to enter into such agreements are the product of mismanagement and breaches of fiduciary duty.”

In court, NVIDIA’s lawyers insisted that the company “did not enter into any illegal ‘agreement’ with the government but instead engaged with the government and stated that it would comply with federal regulations.” Moreover, the attorneys say Paisner is trying to make a political point, rather than a legal one. “When asked about his purpose in bringing this action,” they write, “Paisner was candid: ‘It’s to make a public point of how NVIDIA conducts itself.’”

The attorneys wrote this in May, months before Nvidia’s and Huang’s recent drama. Last week, board chair Harvey Jones said on the company’s quarterly earnings call that it did “not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO,” citing his “disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company’s code of conduct, standards, and cultural norms.” (Not to mention mounting evidence that the company’s most sophisticated chips were making their way to China, in violation of US law.) But, according to The New York Times, after days of pressure from top administration officials, the company and the government essentially struck a deal Sunday night to reinstate Huang.

“Don’t think for a second that politics ever play a role in this company, not even a little bit,” Huang said during his first company-wide meeting after rejoining the company, WIRED reported. “We are the most apolitical company. I don’t love that we talk about politics here. Take it home. I love that we talk about work here.”
