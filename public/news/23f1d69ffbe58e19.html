Samsung conquered AI chips. Now it wants to build data centres

✍️ By Song Jung-a

Samsung Electronics is aggressively expanding its AI infrastructure ecosystem through its IT subsidiary, covering construction, operation, and power supply for data centers. The company is currently in early-stage talks with OpenAI and Anthropic to leverage its semiconductor manufacturing and hardware integration advantages for providing a one-stop AI computing solution.

After conquering memory chips, Samsung wants to turn other parts of its sprawling business into AI supply chain champions, but analysts question whether it can replicate its success with semiconductors across the group.

In the past year, the South Korean conglomerate has taken steps to try to become a one-stop provider of AI infrastructure. It has invested $1bn in a KKR- and Nvidia-backed data centre operator, acquired a European cooling systems maker and signed a partnership with OpenAI to develop AI infrastructure.

The group’s hope is to be able to offer a service in which it can build data centres through its construction arm, operate them through its IT services unit and provide energy storage systems through its battery maker, in addition to supplying memory chips.

“Moving forward, Samsung is poised to expand its role from a traditional hardware component supplier to an active architect of the global AI infrastructure ecosystem,” it said following its $1bn investment in Helix Digital Infrastructure in September.

Samsung is betting that its future in the AI boom lies beyond semiconductors, a highly lucrative but also highly cyclical industry.

While best known for Samsung Electronics — the manufacturing juggernaut that makes chips, Galaxy smartphones and household appliances — Samsung Group is a vast conglomerate with businesses as wide-ranging as insurance and security services.

Many of them started as internal units to serve the conglomerate and still count Samsung as their primary customer, but the group hopes some of the subsidiaries can expand to external clients as AI demand takes hold.

Samsung SDS, which began as an in-house IT services provider and operates data centres mostly to support the group, is in early-stage talks with Anthropic and OpenAI to potentially sign them on as tenants. Analysts expect the company to generate annual data centre revenue of Won6.6tn ($4.9bn) by 2031, roughly six times current levels.

Last year, Samsung signed a partnership with OpenAI to supply advanced memory chips to the company’s Stargate data centre initiative.

The agreement included Samsung SDS collaborating with OpenAI on designing, developing and operating data centres. Construction arm Samsung C&T and shipbuilder Samsung Heavy Industries also said they would work with the US tech company on developing floating data centres.

Morgan Stanley said in a recent report that Samsung SDS was well positioned to benefit from a surge in data centre construction, a trend reinforced by governments around the world passing laws requiring information to be stored domestically.

McKinsey estimates global data centre capacity demand could almost triple by 2030, with AI workloads accounting for about 70 per cent of the total. Meeting AI computing needs alone could require $5.2tn in capital expenditure.

“Samsung has an opportunity to provide a total solution,” said Joungho Kim, a professor of electrical engineering at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology. “US Big Tech companies are strong in AI and software, but they rely heavily on external suppliers for hardware.”

However, recent tech earnings have sparked investor concerns about the astronomical sums being spent on AI infrastructure and whether future demand will justify the cost.

Electoral victories of Democratic candidates in the US who pledged to halt data centre construction in their states have added to worries of regulatory pushback.

But Sanjeev Rana, head of Korea research at CLSA, said that while there would be “ups and downs” in the market, the spending was “structural”.

“In several years [and] decades, we are going to see huge investments in AI data centre-related products,” he said.

For Samsung, the challenge is bringing together disparate parts of its business empire.

Until 2017, the conglomerate had a Future Strategy Office responsible for group-wide decisions, but it was dismantled following a political corruption scandal involving its leadership.

Since then, Samsung has relied on so-called co-ordination bodies that are more lightly staffed, have fewer resources and are split based on business lines.

Analysts said the lack of a central decision-making body could complicate efforts to pursue more ambitious AI infrastructure projects that require close co-operation.

“Samsung has a broad range of capabilities, but it has been relatively weak at bringing them together [into an integrated offering],” said Peter Lee, an analyst at Citi.

There are also questions about experience serving external customers at scale as several subsidiaries remain heavily dependent on Samsung for work.

Rana said the group’s more immediate opportunity was in designing the systems inside data centres rather than operating the facilities themselves.

Samsung already supplies a range of components used in AI infrastructure including high-bandwidth memory chips, advanced packaging substrates for semiconductors and backup battery units for servers.

“Improving and extending the advantages in components will serve it better. And these are high-margin businesses,” said Rana. “Owning or operating data centres, I am not sure actually that will play to their strengths.”
