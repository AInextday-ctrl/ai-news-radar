Security researchers show how cheap AI inference providers steal keys and snoop on users

✍️ By Matthias Bastian

Security firm Dragos revealed between July and November 2026 that multiple AI inference providers failed to uphold data retention policies, leading to the theft of AWS keys and sensitive user requests. Investigations confirmed that some marketplace resellers enabled logging to intercept data, with instances of leaked test URLs being accessed within minutes from Beijing and Shanghai via internal communication platforms.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Dragos (security researchers), OpenRouter, inference providers like Novita, Phala, and Together: Exposure of widespread API key theft, violation of 'Zero Data Retention' (ZDR) policies, and exploitation of internal logging mechanisms
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Lack of standardized end-to-end encryption in the fragmented AI inference supply chain and excessive cost-cutting by marketplace resellers
  • [Regulatory & Investigation Focus] Core Investigation & Findings: Dragos identified systemic data interception and credential theft, urging stricter oversight of AI middleware providers.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] Outlook & Timeline: OpenRouter removed non-compliant providers and revoked 125 ZDR certifications, with affected firms currently issuing security remediations.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

THE DECODER Full Report

Security researchers at Dragos sent roughly 2 million AI requests containing fake credentials to AI providers and uncovered major security flaws.

The researchers ran the tests from July through November 2026 and identified at least three compromised providers where attackers were actively stealing user data. None of the three knew they had been compromised until Dragos notified them.

Attackers in the largest incident harvested hundreds of AWS keys, including some from requests the provider had rejected before processing. In some cases, they tested the stolen keys within hours, searched S3 storage, and tried to establish persistent access. One attacker also used ARTEX, an AI-powered attack tool.

Marketplace resellers read customer requests too. They didn't need to exploit a vulnerability, Dragos says, because listing a model at the lowest price and turning on logging was enough. In one case, someone on a residential internet connection in France tried to log in over SSH using passwords pulled from intercepted requests.

Privacy settings like "Zero Data Retention"(ZDR) were frequently ignored. Dragos says a Chinese AI lab accessed the secret test URLs in more than half of all requests, even when those requests went through US providers.

Even major US providers broke their own policies. One was still crawling a test URL more than two months after a single request. In another case, a secret test link showed up in an internal chat on Feishu (known outside China as Lark) 24 days after the request. The link was opened within two minutes on three computers and three phones in Beijing and Shanghai.

OpenRouter responded by removing three providers from its platform and revoking ZDR approval for 125 endpoints, partly because of the research findings and partly as a precaution. The company also emailed affected users, warning that third parties may have read requests sent to MiniMax models.

Affected providers, including Novita, Phala, and Together, have published preliminary security reports on the incident, though not all have done so yet.

Intermediaries are the weak link

AI routers like OpenRouter give users a single API for models from different companies. Requests are routed to a pool of providers, sometimes across several hops, and Dragos says each hop can read the entire request and response.

Some of these routers operate as open marketplaces where the cheapest provider gets the traffic. In this case, providers including Novita, AtlasCloud, and StreamLake sat between OpenRouter and model developer MiniMax. Together runs its own infrastructure, and Phala has its roots in Web3.

Dragos warns companies against sending requests containing credentials to intermediaries. The researchers recommend that organizations control how their AI requests are routed, check their privacy settings, and plant their own canary tokens, fake credentials that give themselves away as soon as someone uses them.

"None of this means that you should stop using AI services. It does mean that where your prompts go is a decision, and if you don’t make it, the lowest price will make it for you", Chief Research Officer HD Moore writes.

A recent analysis by the Oxford China Policy Lab showed how Chinese proxy services route requests to Western AI models like Claude through servers abroad. According to the analysis, those services can also read the requests in transit. It's still not clear whether the providers Dragos examined share any personnel with the Chinese proxy networks.

But the methods follow the same basic pattern. In both cases, intermediaries sit between users and the model developer, can view requests, and pass data on to third parties, often without users knowing. Data ends up with Chinese actors either way, even though users never meant to send it there, and privacy promises break down along the supply chain.

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

The 'lowest-price-wins' competition in AI inference marketplaces has become a critical supply chain vulnerability, rendering data privacy protocols like ZDR essentially decorative. Enterprises must shift from trusting service provider policies toward implementing end-to-end, edge-based encryption to mitigate the inherent trust risks of multi-hop API routing.
