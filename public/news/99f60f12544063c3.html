AI is straining America’s open records systems

✍️ By Rachel Courtland

AI-powered automation is triggering a surge in public records requests across the U.S., forcing local agencies to handle significantly increased volume and scope. This influx has strained the limited resources of town and state offices, leading some jurisdictions to consider legislative changes to limit transparency requirements, potentially hindering open government.

In Panton, Vermont, population 660, town clerk Kyle Rowe did it all. He crafted budgets, paid vendors, handled property tax disputes, and served as the town’s zoning supervisor and presiding elections officer. During morning office hours at the town hall—a wood-frame structure built as a church in 1858—he also fielded public records requests. Recently, he got rather busy.

Panton used to get a request every year or so from a company called SmartProcure for “all purchasing records,” covering everything from road salt to computers to toilet paper. Last year, those requests started coming quarterly. Then different companies began making more specific requests, like records of every purchase related to emergency road flares. These targeted inquiries started coming monthly or more. “I’m happy to answer public records requests from people,” says Rowe, who recently moved into a position at a nearby town. “But answering it for these purposes—it feels like I’m doing unpaid opposition research on the taxpayers’ time.”

Rowe’s experiences are being repeated across the US. While data brokers have mined public records for decades, AI has intensified investors’ interest. Over the last few years, venture-backed startups have raised upwards of $100 million to collect data from thousands of agencies and turn it into AI-powered “signals” that help businesses sell to governments.

At the same time, AI tools have made it easier for individuals to draft public records requests. Many places have seen double-digit annual increases in recent years. While more activity is good for democracy, AI-drafted requests tend to be longer, and broader in scope—and the offices answering them haven’t always grown in head count or budget.

As agencies struggle to tell whether they’re dealing with a citizen, a chatbot, or a sales pipeline, some states are passing legislation that makes it easier to deny requests, possibly making public offices less transparent.

The US Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) of 1966 established citizens’ right to know what their government is doing, and by now every state has written its own version into law. Greater transparency has led to cleaner drinking water, lower taxes, and better schools. But the system is far from perfect: Many requests linger for months, the process is often frustratingly bureaucratic, and rules and guidelines vary by state, county, and even town.

This is why businesses, with the time and resources to navigate this labyrinth, have often dominated FOIA since its early days. Margaret Kwoka, a law professor at Ohio State University and a recent member of the FOIA Advisory Committee, documented the trend in her 2016 study “ FOIA, Inc .”

Her analysis found that commercial requesters of all kinds—regulated companies, government contractors, information resellers—have accounted for 79% to 96% of public records requests at federal agencies like the Environmental Protection Agency and the Food and Drug Administration. What Kwoka once described as a cottage industry of small shops reselling public records has become a lucrative market for “much more sophisticated and integrated database products,” she says.

SmartProcure (which does business as GovSpend) pioneered the model 15 years ago, with a standardized approach for collecting government purchasing data. Its requests are “handled by real people with long-standing relationships,” says Trip Colden, GovSpend’s chief operating officer, and the company has preferential pricing for agencies.

The newer AI startups promise to make selling to government faster and fairer. Starbridge has raised $52 million, including a Series A led by Craft Ventures (the firm of David Sacks, the former White House AI and crypto czar), to build the equivalent of a “Bloomberg Terminal for public sector data,” a reference to the pricey data consoles that are a fixture on Wall Street trading desks. The company describes its mission as getting “the best technology into the hands of public leaders,” with public records as one input that helps match agencies with vendors. Pursuit, backed by investors including Bill Gurley and Jack Altman, has raised $25.5 million to help companies identify likely government buyers, drawing on board minutes, budgets, and what it calls “an industrial-scale FOIA operation.” CEO and cofounder Mike Vichich says Pursuit’s data will benefit the public, noting that many towns are seeing costs rise faster than revenues and “competition is what saves taxpayers money.”

While incumbents employ account teams to build a rapport with agencies, several clerks and county attorneys say newer startups often send many requests as they scramble to catch up on historical data. For example, PinPoint Analytics, which analyzes construction bid data, sent as many as 2,000 requests per day while building its dataset before settling closer to 500 today, says chief operating officer Mark Zurada. That mirrors the activity of SmartProcure and Pursuit, which send roughly 180,000 requests per year, according to their websites.

But the numbers can be much higher. NationGraph, which serves midsize vendors such as janitorial service providers, claims to send about 10,000 requests per day on behalf of clients; the company did not respond to requests for comment. The requests that companies send are typically written with templates, not AI. But AI helps standardize what comes back—spreadsheets, PDFs, mailed packets—and analyze it all to create branded sales “signals,” or benchmarks to help the business clients set their prices.

In small towns, this activity can be an annoyance; in cities and counties, which get many times the volume, handling it is becoming a full-time job. Chautauqua County, New York, hired a paralegal this summer after seeing requests double—an increase its county attorney blamed on people using ChatGPT to generate requests and on others seeking records to resell.

In Ann Arbor, Michigan, chief deputy clerk Sarah Alanis has seen the number of requests climb from 581 in 2021 to 937 last year—“our new normal is high,” she says. A growing share comes from requesters harvesting data at scale, sometimes asking for purchases from “vendors that don’t have much to do with one another,” or ones the city has never used. FOIA used to take a few hours of her week; now it’s often half her day , she told the Michigan Advance earlier this year.

It’s not just sales tech—AI tools have made it easy for anyone to fire off requests. Federal agencies received a record 1.7 million last year, which a New York Times report attributed partly to AI, as well as to mass filings by the Heritage Foundation and a rise in the number of immigrants seeking their own records for legal protection. David Cuillier, director of the Brechner Freedom of Information Project at the University of Florida, ran the US Department of Education’s July request log through an AI detector and concluded that at least 29% of requests had been written at least partly by AI, up from almost none before 2025. The AI-written requests were twice as long, and 83% asked for long lists of records.

In Pennsylvania, AI-driven activity is “breaking records every month,” says Liz Wagenseller, executive director of the Office of Open Records, which fields appeals for denied requests. Volumes had surged before—during the covid years, at the height of the 2020 election fight, or amid battles over school curricula. But Wagenseller says this wave isn’t driven by a hot-button issue, and appeals keep rising—from about 3,000 in 2021 and just under 4,000 in 2025 to a projected 6,000 this year.

While more activity signals stronger civic engagement, AI is straining an already fragile infrastructure with often poorly drafted requests. Wagenseller notes that when it comes to appealing a denial, chatbots “always give [people] encouragement to try,” even when it has no chance. Filings that used to take a paragraph now run pages. Some contain hallucinated legal citations, and one even included the user’s prompt to Claude.

On the other side of the records requests, resource-strapped offices are using tech to manage it all. One-fifth of federal agencies report using AI or machine learning to help search for records. The Washington Post reported that the Department of Homeland Security is using AI to speed redactions , which transparency advocates worry could lock in agencies’ long habit of withholding more than the law requires. Local governments are catching up, with many still migrating from email to centralized request portals. The software vendor CivicPlus says its NextRequest platform handles more than a million requests a year and uses captchas to screen out bots. That stops automated agents, says chief marketing officer Jane Snyder, but it “doesn’t determine whether a person used AI to help draft a request.”

As volumes rise, legislatures are responding. CivicPlus counts more than 20 state and federal measures in play this year, several of which give agencies new ways to say no. California’s, signed in September, declares that AI systems are not “members of the public,” though people may still use AI “at an ordinary human volume and frequency.” A stalled Pennsylvania bill would let agencies deny requests they “reasonably suspect” were generated by a script or AI. Wagenseller doesn’t think that’s the answer. The real choice, she says, is funding agencies to keep up or making the system less free, fast, and open—“and you’re giving up something for both.”

Arguably the most aggressive action yet has been in Indiana, where Allen County officials described a growing trend in “suspiciously non-human” requests for years of purchase orders, eating up to 20 hours a week of staff time, as reported by the Journal Gazette . A state law that passed without a single dissenting vote and took effect July 1 lets agencies charge out-of-state requesters, prioritize residents, and deny requests they suspect are aimed at data scraping or phishing.

Neighboring Adams County went further. In March, it banned commercial use of its records, including resale. The county still hands over the documents, though; a notice of the ban is attached, but there are few means to enforce it. Requests had jumped from about one a week to as many as 10, often in a single burst; since the state law took effect, they’ve settled at one to five. County attorney Clayton Lengerich says the new rules don’t affect “any individual requester’s ability to keep their local municipality accountable,” only requests that “someone’s going to throw into an AI generator for some sales analysis.”

Fielding FOIA requests is expensive, and someone has to pay. Congress tried to put the responsibility on business in 1986, amending FOIA to let agencies charge commercial requesters more, but the rules were so riddled with exceptions that this rarely happens. “It costs more for the agencies to collect the fees than it’s worth in fees, and so they just don’t,” says Kwoka, the Ohio State law professor. She estimates that agencies recoup less than 5% of their costs from commercial requesters; at the FDA in one year she studied, it was $328,000 against $33.6 million spent.

Kwoka has long argued for “affirmative disclosure,” or proactively posting the information that gets asked for most. She points to the EPA, which put property contamination records online after commercial buyers had requested them one address at a time for years. A handful of states have adopted portals, such as California’s Open FI$Cal , that regularly publish high-level spending data. But this doesn’t include the full contracts that sales-tech firms often request, which reveal expiration dates, termination clauses, and service commitments.

Startups like Pursuit and Starbridge say they’d welcome this approach, and they seem confident their business model could survive if the data became freely available. “The best request is one that no one has to file because the data is already out there,” says Vichich. But publishing isn’t free, and commercial requesters also aren’t raising their hands to fund the fix.

As states negotiate reforms, the biggest risk is that rules built to limit bulk AI requests or sales-tech crawlers will land on everyone else: out-of-state reporters, academic researchers, immigration lawyers. Meanwhile, the requests keep increasing. “It’s something we’re going to have to hold our nose and continue doing,” says Rowe, the small-town Vermont clerk. “The alternative sets a dangerous precedent for a person being able to hold government accountable.”

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

AI is transforming public records from administrative tools into low-cost commercial signal harvesting, destabilizing the foundations of FOIA. The regulatory focus will shift toward governing data access rates and intent, while the technical limitations of legacy government infrastructure emerge as a major bottleneck to transparency.
