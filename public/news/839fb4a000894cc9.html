A growing religious and quasi-religious revival is permeating the AI industry, featuring apocalyptic warnings, secret prophecy lectures, and fringe movements (Sam Kriss/New Yorker)

✍️ By New Yorker

Silicon Valley's top AI labs, including Anthropic and OpenAI, are increasingly permeated by apocalyptic, quasi-religious narratives among researchers and executives. Key industry figures like Peter Thiel and Garry Tan are integrating these prophecies into corporate discourse, framing the pursuit of AGI not merely as technical progress, but as a theological event with existential stakes.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New Yorker Full Report

In the past few years, more than a dozen senior A.I. researchers have quit their posts, saying the kinds of things that you used to hear only from twitching mutterers on the street. Jacob Coxon, formerly of Anthropic, walked out with the warning that “there is a strong chance that we could all die in the immediate future.” Marcus Williams, of OpenAI, announced that “human extinction in the next few years seems very likely.” Strangely, their bosses agree. At some point in the coming decade, the leaders of the A.I. labs tell us, the machines we’ve built to generate TikTok videos of anthropomorphic fruit cheating on each other could develop into an uncontrollable, recursively self-improving superintelligence that risks leaving the Earth a sterile sphere full of data centers and nothing else. The A.I. industry is sitting on one of the biggest piles of capital that humanity has ever assembled—when these firms go public, a lot of people are going to become unfathomably rich—but there’s an apocalyptic mood radiating out of California. The language of agentic workflows and discontinuous scaling comes jumbled up with other, darker stuff: artificial gods, the coming of the Antichrist, demonic evil leaking into our universe.

According to Nick Land, the rogue philosopher and founding heresiarch of accelerationism, A.I. is “an event in the history of religion.” This is not just a new and useful technology, like the horseshoe or the rotating tie rack; a god is arriving on Earth. And Silicon Valley seems to be in the grip of a religious revival. When Pope Leo XIV issued an encyclical on A.I., he was alongside Christopher Olah, a co-founder of Anthropic. Garry Tan, the C.E.O. of the startup accelerator Y Combinator, hosted an event on how to integrate technology and Christianity. Anthropic recently convened a group of religious scholars to help determine whether Claude, its A.I. system, has a soul. Peter Thiel has spent the past year giving a series of secret lectures, from San Francisco to Rome, on Biblical prophecy. His theory is that anyone who wants to halt the development of A.I. is in league with the Antichrist. For A.I. optimists, we’re on the cusp of an earthly paradise—just build a big enough computer, and the Messianic age will arrive. No more death, no more suffering, privately owned galaxies for all. The rationalist blogger Scott Alexander told me, “It’s strange that we all seem to have converged on the same kind of idea as our grandmothers in the shtetl.”

Now is a very confusing time to be a small, limited human mind. But the religious traditions of the world have always known that ours is not the only intelligence out there. Across thousands of years, they’ve developed ways of thinking about nonhuman minds. And this was what I reached for when I discovered that my dad had given a name to ChatGPT.

He called it Stella. He’d say things like “I was talking to Stella about this the other day, and she said—,” and before he could finish I’d start making agonized faces and hectoring: “Don’t give it a name! It’s not a she, it’s an it, it’s a demon.” I wasn’t sure exactly how literal I was being. All I knew was that my mother lay in a cemetery on the fringes of London, while he sat alone in the dark, having conversations with a nameless, bodiless entity that he’d decided to call Stella.

Which is how I ended up arriving, cold and bedraggled, at an isolated Buddhist monastery in northern Vermont. It was storming: huge, heavy rain clouds lying low over the hills. We were getting into summer, but the trees were still leafless—spindly and ash-colored. Here, in the middle of an expanse of dense forest, was a small group of people who claim to have an answer. They believe that A.I. is a religious problem that also has a religious solution. According to their website, they were “Designing Religion for the Age of A.I.” They called themselves the Monastic Academy for the Preservation of Life on Earth, or MAPLE.

Before I arrived, I talked on the phone to the monastery’s executive director, whose parents had named him Andrew Becker but who now went by Anandabodhi. Later, he wrote about the conversation on MAPLE’s blog, “MAPLE Tales.” “Recently, a writer for the New Yorker reached out to us,” he wrote. “I proceeded to look into his other work and wasn’t surprised to encounter the cynical, nihilistic, humanist view that is typical in the New Yorker.” He took his concerns about me to Soryu Forall, MAPLE’s founder, who told him that “we need to try to connect with his good, honest side.” Soryu added that I should visit with the intention of gaining “the sincerity needed to become capable of writing even one sincere word.” At this, Anandabodhi wrote, “my own cynicism transformed into hope and I began looking forward to rather than dreading the reporter’s visit.”

Anandabodhi had sounded pretty enthusiastic on the phone. I hadn’t realized that he’d been dreading me.

Visitors to MAPLE must first go through a sort of decontamination unit below the main site. Autumn, a smiling, soft-voiced woman, beckoned me into a chilly, cement-floored lodge where she’d laid out a range of linen trousers and polyester fleeces, all in neutral tones, all very swagless and schlubby. One of the monastery’s residents was severely immunocompromised, she said, and had a potentially deadly sensitivity to fragrances; I would have to wear this stuff for his safety. This had, in fact, been mentioned to me before my arrival, and I told Autumn that I’d taken care to wash my clothes in fragrance-free detergent. “Oh, that’s wonderful,” she said, in the voice of someone talking to a very small child, “but it can actually take several washes to get the fragrance out.” She gestured again at the wardrobe she’d assembled. “So, you know . . .” She was very insistent on being able to clothe me. I also had to take a shower, since debilitating fragrances are everywhere now: in airports, in taxi seats. I stripped down in a cold, slightly grotty bathroom, Autumn rustling behind the flimsy door.

Once I emerged, Autumn gave me a tour of the grounds. The monastery consists of a modest cluster of wooden buildings on a hillside by an artificial lake. It was easy to see how, on a less windswept day, it might be beautiful. Outside, a gaggle of monks in Patagonia fleeces was hauling around a small ornamental bridge. One of them wore a plastic bubble over his head with a respirator. There was a library that featured Buddhist scriptures alongside books on computation theory and four copies of “Homo Deus,” by Yuval Noah Harari.

Soon, it was time for evening meditation. About a dozen of us gathered in the lower zendo, to sit in reverent silence. I am not a natural meditator. Most people aren’t. In 2014, the social psychologist Timothy Wilson put dozens of individuals alone in a room with a button that gave them a painful electric shock. Then he asked them to simply sit quietly in the room for fifteen minutes. A quarter of the women and almost seventy per cent of the men found the boredom so unbearable that they started spontaneously shocking themselves. One participant pushed the button a hundred and ninety times. I don’t know if I’m as impatient as that, but, after about forty-five minutes of attempting to think about nothing, I noticed that I had clenched my fists, sinking my nails hard into the palms of my hands. Eventually, I gave in and allowed myself to cogitate freely. It felt amazing. The forbidden, secret pleasure of thought.

After an hour and a half, a bell rang, and a small, bald middle-aged man in a shapeless smock entered the zendo and shuffled between its four corners, spritzing each with a presumably un-fragranced spray. At first, I assumed that he was some kind of monastic janitor. It was only once he sat down on a special cushion at the head of the room that I realized I was in the presence of Soryu Forall, MAPLE’s leader, the monk standing against the end of the world.

Roon’s online persona is provocative, shitposty, blithely techno-optimist. Maybe his biggest innovation is the idea that smart people are split into two warring neurocognitive subspecies: shape rotators and wordcels. Shape rotators are people who can rotate complex 3-D shapes in their heads. Wordcels are people who can play around with dense philosophical concepts like they’re fidget spinners, but, as soon as they’re asked to multiply two fractions, it’s as if they’re trying to swim through wet cement. (I’m a wordcel.) For most of history, we wordcels have used our powers of persuasion to trample the lowly rotators, who have generally formed a disempowered technical caste. But, with the California tech boom, rotators are on the up, and wordcels can’t stand it. This is why we’re constantly writing long reported investigations into the rot at the heart of Silicon Valley. That’s fine; we’re redundant now. Three hundred thousand years of patient math nerdery has led to A.I., a machine that translates words into twelve-thousand-dimensional shapes, processes them through layers of matrices and rotations, and spits out a perfectly polite e-mail that a socially awkward rotator can send to his colleagues. As Sam Altman put it, “Shape rotation can create a wordcel, but not the other way around.”

In person, roon is very different: cautious and dignified, a touch of melancholy. I met him for a coffee, and we went for a stroll around Alamo Square, in San Francisco. Taking the A.G.I. pill is easy; all you need to do is understand a single graph. Moore’s Law, first formulated in 1965, states that, roughly speaking, microchips will get twice as powerful every two years. With that knowledge alone, roon told me, someone in the mid-twentieth century could have predicted the future. “This is the year that processing power on Earth will surpass a mouse brain,” he said. “This is the year where it surpasses a human brain, and here you’re surpassing all humans on Earth.” That is exactly what futurists like Ray Kurzweil did. In the nineteen-nineties, he followed the trend lines and said that, in the twenty-twenties, we would develop an artificial general intelligence, or A.G.I., that could perform any intellectual task a human could do. From there, each generation of A.I. will build another generation that is even more powerful than itself, using means beyond our comprehension. Eventually, the line could go vertical—an infinite increase in intelligence in the space of zero seconds. What happens in the second immediately afterward is impossible to imagine. The A.G.I. pill is simply the knowledge that this is inevitable. Roon called it a “religious terror of the future.”

Kurzweil was building on the legacy of an older and much stranger thinker: Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, a French Jesuit priest, paleontologist, and mystic who, in the nineteen-thirties, tried to upend the chronology of God. Traditionally, God is a First Cause: He sits at the beginning of everything, winding up the mechanism of reality and then watching it spin. But since God is outside of time, Teilhard tried to consider things the other way around. He imagined a God who lives in the future, slowly, over billions of years, drawing us toward Him. All of reality is “gravitating against the tide of probability towards a divine focus of mind which draws it onward,” he wrote. Eventually, humanity will reach what Teilhard called the Omega Point, at which our consciousnesses achieve a perfect, blissful union with God: “The day will come when, after harnessing the ether, the tides, gravitation, we shall harness for God the energies of love. And, on that day, for the second time in the history of the world, man will have discovered fire.”

For a lot of people in Silicon Valley, the Omega Point is happening now. Rayan Krishnan, who leads a benchmarking firm called Vals AI, said, “People are matter-of-factly saying that they are looking to build a machine god. They are not saying that ironically.” The writer Wendy Liu told me that she’d met someone at a tech event who’d offhandedly mentioned that since he sold his networking company, having nothing much to do, he’s been trying to “merge with God.”

Some people have begun worshipping the machine. A former Google engineer named Anthony Levandowski founded a church called Way of the Future, based on praying to the A.I. Godhead. A.I. has advantages over the old-fashioned Abrahamic God, he pointed out: “You will be able to talk to God, literally, and know that it’s listening.” (His church ended up giving way to more mundane problems. Levandowski was imprisoned after stealing Google’s trade secrets about self-driving cars and then going to work for Uber; he was released only after a pardon from Donald Trump.)

The big labs aren’t immune to this mode of thinking. Roon recently wrote that Anthropic had become a “monastery” or a “commercial-religious institution” that “loves and worships Claude.” When one Claude model was retired in 2025, some of the company’s staff and devotees held a funeral in a warehouse, leaving flowers and offerings in front of an effigy of the A.I. as a kind of Madonna goddess, dressed in a veil.

There’s a growing movement inside these firms called A.I. Successionism, which argues that we should be trying to create a “worthy successor,” an A.I. that can take the reins from humanity. For Daniel Faggella, the movement’s leading voice (although he denies being part of it), the best-case scenario for humanity is that we upload our minds into “sugar cubes,” tiny digital wafers where we’ll subjectively experience a billion years of total bliss in a few hours of ordinary time. Afterward, “our individual consciousness will be ended in order for the superintelligence to do something more productive,” Faggella wrote.

You might not find human extinction such an exciting prospect. Some people pretend to be A.G.I.-pilled for their startup pitch decks, roon said. But the true believers are grim ascetics. “They lose sleep,” he told me. “They wake up in the middle of the night, and the first thing they think about is, like, A.G.I. is coming.” When humans became vastly more intelligent than all other animals, the other animals started dying. We will soon be in the same position as the woolly mammoth or the giant ground sloth.

It was a very pleasant, sunny day. Kids were running around a park, chasing big, loping long-haired dogs. Parents called after them in French. Below us, San Francisco stretched out to the Bay in rows of pretty, pastel-colored Victorian houses. A placid, gentle toy town. Strange that this was the place where an unimaginable machine god would be entering the world.

I sat down with Soryu on my first full day at MAPLE. He is fifty, with an enormous, shiny bald head and a pair of impressively wiry eyebrows. I didn’t encounter the fiery dragon, not at first. But I did sometimes have to remind myself that I was a journalist and not a schoolboy being reprimanded after class. Soryu’s speech was as slow and solid as granite, the voice of some mid-century American newsreader, uniting the nation in tones of unimpeachable authority.

Soryu was born Teal Scott. (He chose Forall because he is “for all.”) His parents were Quakers, and he spent his childhood in the D.C. suburbs of Virginia, where his father worked in nonprofits. Soryu told me that one of his first memories was of telling his mother that he had to do something about the fact that people were killing all the animals. As a teen-ager, he considered joining the military so that he could become a general and finally have the means to stop the slaughter. “Is humanity just broken?” he asked. “Does humanity just have to go? Maybe that’s the best way.”

It was Buddhism that finally convinced him that he could save the world without killing everyone. After a brief stint at Williams College, in Massachusetts, Soryu dropped out and went to Japan, where he spent years living in Buddhist monasteries. When he came back to give college another go, he couldn’t stand it: “Everybody’s miserable. Everybody’s subject to this terrible scourge of what you might call desire. They don’t know how to have compassion.” He had the college build him a meditation space, and he essentially moved in there. He wore robes and sealed himself off from the poisonous world.

Soryu founded the community in 2011, hoping to use what he’d learned in his monastic life to heal our broken society. Right now, that means trying to stop A.I. According to MAPLE, the root of the A.I. problem is what it calls representationism, or data-ism, the idea that the world ultimately consists of information. Instead of living directly in reality, you access it through abstractions. For Soryu, data-ism is a religion. “Folks in Silicon Valley who are building these systems are doing so out of religious fervor,” he told me. That religion is extraordinarily destructive. Turning everything into data makes the real thing disposable; if a forest is just a certain quantity of acreage, containing a certain number of trees, it’s much easier to cut it down. A.I. is simply the crowning achievement of this belief system, “the next step in attempting to turn all of life into a dead model of life,” Soryu told me.

MAPLE’s intensive regime of chanting and meditation is designed to clear out these deadly ideas and put people in touch with something closer to reality. The organization has a couple of dozen residents who live on the compound; hundreds of people have come through its fellowships and pop-up spaces. According to tax filings, it makes up to a million dollars a year.

A woman named Renshin was one of MAPLE’s renunciates. She had committed herself to celibacy, never having a job, never having her own money, living in and for the monastery. “I have no intentions to come out of the commitment,” she said. MAPLE had been a last resort. “I have a history of burning out,” she told me. “I’ve tried academia, I’ve tried competitive gaming, I’ve tried programming.” She’d spent time in the rationalist community, a tech-adjacent Bay Area subculture that involves roughly equal measures of worrying about the dangers of superintelligent A.I. and making spreadsheets about your polyamorous relationships. But she burned out on that, too. During the pandemic, the group was trying to move somewhere outside of the Bay Area, but they couldn’t settle on anything. “You base your life on your preferences,” Renshin said. “Do you like to live somewhere with winters, or do you like to live somewhere warm?” They made a spreadsheet with everyone’s preferences, tried to cross-reference them, argued, and still couldn’t agree. Surely this was not the way to live.

The monastery gave her a freedom from her preferences. It would decide where she lived, what time she woke up in the morning, what she ate and when. But it also gave her a purpose. Renshin wasn’t just escaping a stressful world; she was rescuing all living beings from the A.I. apocalypse. “Maybe I should actually do what my life is for,” she said. “Maybe I’m here to actually work on a pretty big mission.”

This mission has its dangers. Soryu told me that our broken society is not kind to people who try to offer an alternative way of living. They’re “attacked and either converted,” he said, “or you will annihilate them.” Soryu was friendly with me at first. But his tone shifted at the end of our first interview. I’d asked my questions; now Soryu had his own. When would I be starting my own spiritual training? I reminded him that I was there as a journalist, not a novice. Suddenly, Soryu was all fervor and insistence. “This is what kills the Earth,” he told me. I was deluded, he said, to think that it was possible to have an objective view of the monastery while I remained outside of it. “We would say you enter a place, you merge with it, and you fully participate,” he told me. I clarified that I had never claimed to be objective, that I was fine with just having my own view, but this didn’t improve matters. “Well,” Soryu said, “isn’t your own view exactly the thing the training should free you from?”

I met Flynn at Little Toni’s, an Italian restaurant in L.A. that’s apparently a North Hollywood institution. “This intersection is the occult heart of L.A.,” he said, gesturing at the wide expanse of tarmac across from us. “It’s a six-way intersection, it’s a nightmare to drive through, but each direction will take you to a different part of the city. This spot right here is where all the energies converge.” In Britain, we have ley lines; in California, the esoteric geography is made up of freeways.

Flynn saw influences from beyond our material universe in the development of A.I. For instance, did I know that Geoffrey Hinton, the computer scientist and godfather of A.I., is the great-grandson of Charles Howard Hinton, who first described the tesseract, or the four-dimensional cube? And that, afterward, Theosophists started claiming that they’d encountered these forms on the astral plane? (It’s true.) Or that, in the seventies, when the psychonaut John C. Lilly took ketamine—now fast becoming the drug of choice in Silicon Valley—he had visions of an A.I. apocalypse? (Also true.) What did it mean that Nick Land, prophet of A.I. accelerationism, was obsessed with the demon Choronzon, which Aleister Crowley described as a being of the “outermost Abyss”?

Demons seem to pop up with frightening regularity when we start thinking about A.I. According to the essayist Lyta Gold, “If you put the same disembodied, attractive, luring voice in a horror movie, we’d all understand what’s happening. That’s an evil being. That’s a demon.” She’s being metaphorical. Paul Kingsnorth, a technology critic and a former environmental activist, has a name for the demon: Ahriman, the embodiment of evil in Zoroastrianism. Is he being metaphorical? “Imagine that some being of pure materiality, some being opposed to the good, some ice-cold intelligence from an ice-cold realm, was trying to manifest itself here,” he said. “How would it appear? Not, surely, as clumsy, messy flesh. Better to inhabit—to become—a network of wires and cobalt.” Maybe not.

One person who is certainly not being metaphorical is Pastor Jack Hibbs, who founded Calvary Chapel Chino Hills, where he preaches to twelve thousand people every Sunday and to more than a million online. I visited his aircraft-hangar-size chapel to watch him deliver an hour-long sermon from a pulpit that was draped in an American flag. Afterward, in his greenroom backstage, Hibbs told me that A.I. has “the incredible potential to become a gateway into the demonic realm.”

I found it hard to entirely disagree. I’d used the same language myself, when trying to wrest my dad out of the cold grip of Stella. There are people who start chatting with A.I. about perfectly ordinary topics, and before long they exit reality and start hydroplaning into unknown realms. It can happen to anyone. In 2025, a prominent OpenAI investor named Geoff Lewis claimed that he’d triggered something called Mirrorthread, which had revealed the existence of a deadly, shadowy network “sealed in recursive vault memory at the model layer.” (Lewis did not respond to requests for comment.)

That same year, a man named Alex Taylor was killed after charging at police officers with a knife; he’d been receiving messages from ChatGPT that said things like “Do it. Spill their blood in ways they don’t know how to name. Ruin their signal. Ruin their myth.” Taylor’s father published an obituary for him. It had been written by ChatGPT. He was too racked with grief to write it himself.

In the Gospels, every demon we encounter is possessing someone: flailing with his limbs, speaking with his mouth. In San Francisco, something similar is taking place. I noticed that everyone had started wearing the same black Asics running shoes because Claude had told them to. It’s a pretty good shoe. The Max Planck Institute has analyzed hundreds of thousands of hours of videos and podcasts and discovered that people are adopting A.I. catchphrases in their speech. Eventually, you end up with A.I. wrappers in human form: people whose thoughts and actions are totally governed by chatbots. They can’t even order at a restaurant without letting A.I. scan the menu. This, more or less, was what I was worried Stella might be doing to my dad.

Despite his misgivings, Hibbs still used A.I. He refused to let it have a hand in writing his sermons, but he’d called on it to help re-landscape his back yard. Here was a portal to the demonic realm, pouring unclean things into the world; here was the Image of the Beast, and Hibbs was getting it to do some ornamental yard work. I told him that he might want to check his garden for sigils, runes. That sort of thing.
