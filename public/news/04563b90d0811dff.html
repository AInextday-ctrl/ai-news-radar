Sources: technical staff at several Chinese humanoid robot makers have been denied US visas ahead of CES, hampering their efforts to showcase their hardware (Nikkei Asia)

✍️ By Nikkei Asia

Technical staff from Chinese humanoid robot companies, including Xpeng and Engine AI, have faced U.S. visa denials for the upcoming CES. This systemic rejection of engineering personnel, contrasted with easier access for marketing staff, indicates an intensified U.S. regulatory push to limit technology transfer and engagement in critical hardware sectors.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Nikkei Asia Full Report

HONG KONG -- Researchers and engineers at several Chinese humanoid robot makers, including Xpeng and Engine AI, have been denied U.S. visas, overshadowing their efforts to showcase the country's cutting-edge hardware at the upcoming Consumer Electronics Show (CES) in Las Vegas.

The move comes as Washington tightens restrictions on the inflow and outflow of high-tech products on national security grounds.

Employees with science and engineering backgrounds have had their applications for combined U.S. business and tourism visas, known as B-1/B-2 visas, rejected in recent weeks, according to multiple people who work at the affected startups, while most marketing staff obtained the visas.

At least one company had decided to withdraw from the exhibition because of "geopolitical complexities," after all 15 of its technical staff failed to secure visas, a person said. At another company, half of the staff who applied were rejected, another person said.

Chris Pereira, founder of consultancy iMpact, which helps Chinese companies expand overseas, said around 40% of his clients planning to attend the CES in early January had been denied U.S. visas. Most of those affected were executives and technical staff in the artificial intelligence and robotics sectors, he added.

Xpeng and Engine AI didn't respond to inquiries for comment.

While the problem is not new, the visa challenge facing Chinese companies has intensified.

Last year, dozens of engineers from several Chinese humanoid robotic companies were denied U.S. visas and missed the CES 2026, forcing the companies to cancel some planned robot demonstrations, people familiar with the matter told Nikkei Asia. Companies typically have as many employees as possible apply for visas to improve their chances of securing enough approvals.

AgiBot and another leading Chinese humanoid maker, Unitree, remain on the CES exhibitor roster. People close to the companies said both companies still plan to send staff from mainland China to the show.

The annual electronics fair, one of the world's largest, attracts thousands of exhibitors seeking to pitch their most advanced offerings.

The visa problems facing Chinese humanoid robot producers have highlighted accelerated decoupling between the U.S. and China in a critical industry that has emerged as the new frontier in the two countries' battle for technological supremacy.

Last month, U.S. President Donald Trump determined that imported robotics, machinery and their components "threaten to impair" national security, raising the prospect of tariffs and quotas if negotiations with trading partners fall short.

The decision follows a ban introduced by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) in July on import, marketing and sales of new foreign-made robots in the U.S., a move widely regarded as targeting China, which accounted for two-thirds of global humanoid robot production last year. The rule allows humanoid models and quadruped robots that had already received the FCC authorization to be imported.

Humanoid robots integrate an array of sensors, such as cameras and lidars, with AI-powered software that perceives and maps their surroundings.

"This could raise data security and surveillance concerns, while AI-enabled robots also have clear dual-use characteristics from a national security perspective," said Sheng Zhong, head of Chinese industrials research at Morgan Stanley.

Earlier this year, the Chinese Communist Party listed humanoid robots as one of the "strategic emerging industries" to be nurtured in its five-year economic plan for the first time.

"In China, humanoid and embodied intelligence became a clear national priority," Zhong said.

Mutual distrust between the U.S. and China could lead to a bipolar confrontation that disrupts companies in both countries, industry insiders said.

Beijing has tightened controls on the movement of its AI talent amid fears of technology leaks, said iMpact's Pereira, adding that some Chinese researchers have faced hurdles in obtaining local government approvals for overseas travel.

"Given the escalating tensions between China and the U.S., we'll see more Chinese companies choosing to attend trade shows in Europe and Singapore in the coming years," he said.

Meanwhile, despite the Trump administration's goals of increasing the local content rate of new advanced robots assembled in the U.S. to 65% in 2028 and 75% in 2029, the country's humanoid robot developers still rely on Chinese suppliers for key hardware.

Actuators, the "muscle-like" components that control a humanoid robot's movements, and reducers, which function as its "joints," are among the components most heavily exposed to Chinese supply chains. Leading players such as Tesla, Figure and Boston Dynamics source these components from Chinese partners, according to research by brokerage CLSA.

Taylor Ogan, chief executive of Shenzhen-based hedge fund Snow Bull Capital, said efforts to decouple supply chains were virtually impossible because some U.S. humanoid robot makers depended on China to such an extent that "they aren't really U.S. companies."

"They're obviously buying actuators from Hangzhou and Shenzhen companies. They're [also] buying batteries and controllers from Chinese companies," Ogan said.

"It's not because [Chinese components] are cheaper. With the humanoid robotic supply chain, there is just no alternative."

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

Weaponizing visa denials is a tactical evolution of the 'small yard, high fence' strategy applied to human capital. By undermining the international visibility of Chinese robotics, the U.S. is forcing a decoupling of global tech ecosystems, likely accelerating the bifurcation of the embodied AI market into distinct, non-integrated spheres.
