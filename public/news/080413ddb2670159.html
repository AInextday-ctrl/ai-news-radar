Apple Was Late to the Video Podcast Boom. Now It’s Trying to Catch Up.

✍️ By Sapna Maheshwari and Kalley Huang

Apple Vice President Oliver Schusser announced the addition of a video podcast section to the Apple TV app, currently in developer testing with plans to reach over 1 billion devices. This move aims to bridge Apple’s ecosystem gap in the podcast sector and counter market share loss to YouTube and Spotify by enhancing distribution efficiency for creators across the Apple hardware footprint.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Apple Inc., Oliver Schusser (VP of Services): Launching a video podcast section for developer testing and multi-device distribution
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] To regain competitive ground against YouTube and Spotify in the rapidly evolving podcast market
  • [Key Metrics] Video integration driving over 20% average audience growth for top-tier content.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] Expanding distribution across 1 billion+ Apple TV-enabled devices.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New York Times Full Report

Apple helped turbocharge the rise of podcasts two decades ago, but as video podcasts reshape the industry it’s fallen behind competitors like YouTube and Spotify.

Now it’s playing catch up. On Monday, Apple will add a video podcasts section to Apple TV, initially just for testing with developers. They will soon be free to all users even if they don’t pay for Apple’s streaming service and will appear on over one billion devices that have the Apple TV app, including Rokus and smart TVs. Apple also plans to increase the number of video podcasts on its regular Podcasts app, where it introduced video features nine months ago but has been working to bring some of the biggest shows on board.

“We want podcasts to be available wherever people want to enjoy them — the small screens, big screens,” said Oliver Schusser, a vice president at Apple who oversees services including podcasts, television and music. “It’s really about discovery and helping the creators to reach a new audience. We didn’t approach this in a way that would financially benefit us.”

Video podcasts have proliferated in the past few years as audio creators adapted their shows to reach consumers spending more time on video platforms like YouTube. The video format has proved attractive to celebrities, including Amy Poehler, Pete Davidson and Food Network’s Ina Garten. And the trend of “clipping” — creating short buzzy video clips from longer shows to post on social media platforms like TikTok and Instagram — has encouraged creators to add video to their podcasts.

The popularity of video podcasts, driven by YouTube and Spotify, and newer entrants like Hulu and Netflix, has pushed Apple down in the podcast platform rankings. Spotify passed Apple as the top platform among weekly podcast consumers in the United States at the end of 2022, according to survey data from Edison Research at SSRS. YouTube took over the top spot at the end of 2024. In Edison Research’s third-quarter survey, YouTube remained the most popular, followed by Spotify then Apple Podcasts. Netflix was No. 7.

Many in the industry had long awaited Apple’s move.

“It’s super smart that they’ve done it, but it’s after a lot of people in the space wanted them to make it available,” said Megan Lazovick, a vice president at Edison Research.

Apple’s addition of video to its regular app earlier this year has been encouraging, with overall audiences for top shows that added video growing by more than 20 percent on average. That includes shows like IMO with Michelle Obama and Craig Robinson and the comedy podcast Giggly Squad.

Sam Fragoso, the host of the show Talk Easy, had been “eager” for Apple to add video, saying it had doubled the audience of his show, which is also available on Spotify and YouTube.

“It’s so helpful that people can go on their phones, they see a clip on Instagram, TikTok or Twitter and open the Apple app and now watch it on video,” he said. “People are already on their iPhones and they’re used to watching videos on their phones so it was the next logical step.”

Apple’s history may help it catch up to competitors who were earlier to the video podcast boom.

“I love our YouTube audience but it feels not as intentional on that platform — Apple’s where people know where to find their favorite podcasts,” said Rachel Martin, the host of NPR’s Wild Card.

Her show’s audience has grown by more than 60 percent since Apple introduced video, which she called astonishing in an industry where “sometimes it can feel excruciating and really hard to get just another 100 subscribers.”

In 2005, podcasts were viewed as a way for Apple to sell more iPods, the popular audio player that connected to iTunes. (Tech journalists once compared it to a razor company trying to sell more products by distributing free razor blades.) Today, Apple still seems to view video podcasts as a sweetener for devices, including newer products like its iPhone Duo and the upcoming HomeView tablet.

Mr. Schusser said he envisions a future where people can listen to their favorite podcasts as they move through their day.

More than 35 percent of video podcast consumers actively switch between watching and listening to shows in a given month, Apple’s data shows. Some view it as a sign that video podcasts are the future.

“Everything is TV now, which is a little bit heartbreaking for a lifelong audio person to say,” Ms. Martin of NPR said. “I don’t think this is a flash in the pan.”

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

This initiative represents a defensive restructuring of platform access, aimed at capturing podcast audience migration toward video-first platforms like YouTube. The success of this move hinges on whether Apple can leverage hardware synergy within its walled garden to drive differentiated content consumption, especially as the industry pivots toward short-form video clipping and multimodal experiences.
