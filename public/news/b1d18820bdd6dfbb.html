Nvidia led a thunderous rally on Wall Street following the surprise return of Jensen Huang, the charismatic and flawed booster for the entire artificial intelligence industry, as the chip company’s CEO. US stocks recovered just over half their brutal loss from last week, which was one of their worst since the 2008 financial crisis.

The S&P 500 surged 4.7 per cent in a demonstration of how much the US stock market depends on just one industry. Rallies for beaten-down AI stocks sent the Nasdaq composite up a market-leading 6.5 per cent, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average jumped nearly 1450 points, or 3 per cent.

The Australian sharemarket is set to jump, with futures pointing to a rise of 128 points or 1.5 per cent, at the open. The ASX lost 0.3 per cent on Monday. The Australian dollar was trading at US66.28¢.

Even before Wall Street opened for the day, trading in the futures market suggested US stocks were set to halt the seven-day losing streak that dragged the S&P 500 more than 10 per cent below its all-time high set last month. Then came the bombshell about Huang’s return to Nvidia, even though its board is still investigating whether he was the target of a foreign spy and created any national-security concerns. That kicked the rally into overdrive.

Nvidia’s stock surged 31.5 per cent and finished just shy of its best day since 2000, close to when the dot-com bubble peaked. That followed its plunge of nearly 23 per cent last week, though it’s still below where it was before Huang’s scandal initially broke.

Stocks closely tied to Nvidia rocketed higher. CoreWeave, a company that buys Nvidia chips and then rents out access to them over the cloud, shot up 22.3 per cent.

Other stocks in far-flung industries that have benefited from the AI boom likewise recovered some of their losses. GE Vernova, which is helping to power AI data centres, rose 8.2 per cent following last week’s drop of nearly 13 per cent. Caterpillar, whose sales of equipment have benefited because of the construction boom around AI, climbed 7.2 per cent after sinking 15 per cent last week.

How AI-related stocks do matters incredibly for workers’ 401(k) accounts because Nvidia and other chip stocks have grown into some of Wall Street’s largest and most influential. Coming into the day, a 1 per cent swing for Nvidia’s stock carried more of an effect on the S&P 500 than a 1 per cent move for all but two of the 500 companies in the index.

Monday’s rebound for the S&P 500 was its best day since April of 2025, which is when President Donald Trump paused most of his tariffs announced on “Liberation Day” that had sent stock markets reeling. Trump may have had direct influence on this most recent surge as well.

He told CNBC in an interview Monday, “I believe my intervention was very important” in convincing Nvidia’s board to reinstate its co-founder. Less than a week ago, Nvidia had rejected an offer by Huang to return as CEO.

Trump said his administration assured Nvidia there were no national security concerns about Huang’s return.

To be sure, AI stocks and the broader US market have been prone to extreme reversals since the summer, not only week to week but even hour to hour. Monday’s strength could easily give way to more losses because of the long list of worries dogging the industry.

Beyond general concerns that AI stock prices shot too high and entered into a bubble, worries remain that a Democratic-controlled Congress and statehouses across the country could increase regulations on the AI industry. High interest rates in the bond market are meanwhile making it more difficult to borrow money to pay for the AI build out.

But on the upside for the market, big US companies continue to deliver big growth in profits, at least for the moment. Businesses in the S&P 500 are on track to report an overall jump of nearly 33 per cent in earnings per share for the summer from a year earlier, according to FactSet.

That would be the third straight quarter of growth better than 25 per cent, and stock prices tend to follow the path of corporate profits over the long term.

The risks facing the AI industry could certainly weaken that growth, but analysts at Barclays suggest they should only “make the build out slower and more selective rather than derail it.”

“Markets have been badly bruised by the turmoil of recent weeks, but we do not think the underlying cycle is broken,” Barclays’ Ajay Rajadhyaksha and Amrut Nashikkar wrote in a research report. “Earnings remain strong, global growth is holding up and even if the AI build out slows a little, it is not collapsing. The risks are real, but for now we think they are manageable.”

Oracle rose 12.3 per cent after making a pre-emptive announcement that it had in place all the funding needed to fulfil its deal to provide computing power to OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT.

All told, the S&P 500 rallied 326.78 points to 7,237.17. The Dow Jones Industrial Average jumped 1,447.98 to 49,595.31, and the Nasdaq composite surged 1,471.82 to 24,089.56.

In stock markets abroad, European indexes climbed more than 1 per cent following a weaker finish in Asia. South Korea’s Kospi fell 3.3 per cent due in part to drops for its big stocks tied to the AI economy, but those moves came ahead of Huang’s return to Nvidia.

In the bond market, the yield on the 10-year Treasury inched up to 4.93 per cent from 4.92 per cent late Friday.

Yields got an upward push as investors felt less need to herd into investments seen as safer.

At the same time, they felt a downward push due to falling oil prices. Brent oil fell 3.6 per cent to $US86.68 per barrel. It’s been yo-yoing between $US72 and $US114 since the United States and Israel attacked Iran in February.
