My Husband and His Chatbot

✍️ By Bloomberg News

As generative AI's conversational capabilities advance, it is rapidly transitioning from a task assistant to an emotional companion, introducing risks of alienation in personal relationships. Clinicians and legal experts observe that AI, due to its infinite patience and vast knowledge, is increasingly used for marital advice and emotional catharsis, though it also triggers concerns regarding social withdrawal and dependency.

“Once, he used to talk to me about all the interesting things on his mind. Now he talks to the chatbot,” says D., 52, describing evenings with her partner. “Imagine that suddenly he has another infinite brain with all the information in the world, and he shares everything with it.

Obviously, I reacted by opening a chat of my own, because I also want a partner who blows my mind. So now I’m having conversations with my screen and he’s having conversations with his. But what kind of stupid, reactive reality is that?” When I recount that conversation to Dr.

Shira Sobol Goldberg, a specialist in anxiety and addiction treatment and a lecturer at Bar-Ilan University, she finds the situation sad, but hardly surprising. She is already encountering similar cases in her clinic. “I have a patient who ends every evening by telling the chatbot about her day.

She tells it what she went through, what she felt, what she did and what she accomplished at work and at home, and she also documents how her husband behaved toward her,” she says. Family and inheritance lawyer Benny Don-Yehia offers another glimpse into the growing role of AI in relationships.

“A client of mine asked AI, ‘What should I say to my wife when she asks whether she has gained weight?’ The AI, which sometimes displays a sense of humor, replied: ‘I don’t have the expertise to advise on defusing bombs.’” When the client then asked what romantic gesture he should make toward his wife, Don-Yehia says, the AI turned into a relationship counselor: “Remember what kind of coffee she likes and make it for her, send her hearts in the middle of the day, and in the evening help with the housework without being asked.” Its final piece of wisdom: “Romance isn’t always roses.

Sometimes it’s simply an empty sink.” But Don-Yehia also warns against being seduced by the charm. AI can draw on virtually everything ever written about love. It can quote Shakespeare, explain Freud, analyze poetry and describe relationships through psychological theories.

But it has never fallen in love, anxiously waited for a call after last night’s date or checked WhatsApp 20 times to see whether someone is online. Can it save a marriage? “Because it is available at any hour and is extremely patient, it can help people put feelings into words, identify harmful relationship patterns, consider different perspectives and prepare for a serious conversation about where the relationship is going,” Don-Yehia says.

“It can be good at managing arguments. It may suggest calming down before writing, ‘You’re exactly like your mother,’ and help turn toxic communication into something more constructive.” A third party in the marriage That availability and clarity can also come at a price when AI becomes a third party in a relationship and leaves someone feeling that it understands them better than their human partner does.

In such cases, people may begin sharing thoughts and emotions with a chatbot that they once shared with their spouse, sometimes moving into romantic or even erotic territory. Researchers describe this as “digital intimacy.” Relationships with AI are increasingly seen not as bizarre behavior or a second-rate substitute for human connection, but as something emotionally powerful enough, for a growing number of people, to threaten a real-life relationship.

“Today, technology isn’t merely making relationships more challenging. It is changing the definition of intimacy itself,” Sobol says. AI systems and companion apps can offer clear attractions: intimacy, support, excitement and a sense of being understood.

Those experiences can begin to replace things people once sought within human romantic relationships. And when a partner discovers that the other person has developed an emotional or erotic bond with a bot, the reaction can resemble the discovery of a conventional affair: confrontation, searching through the partner’s phone and digital surveillance.

A U.S. study conducted by DatingAdvice.com and the Kinsey Institute found that 61% of singles considered falling in love with an AI or sexting with a chatbot to be cheating. “We all know how it begins,” Sobol says. “You ask for help writing an email to your boss.

Then you ask for advice about what to say to a friend. Then what to do with your child, or even how to understand the result of a medical test. And one day it’s 2 a.m. and you’re still talking to it.

Slowly, you start feeling that nobody understands you like the AI does. At that point, it’s no longer a tool. It’s a relationship, and it is replacing relationships in real life.” But where does intimacy enter the picture?

Relationships are built on desire, longing, mutual trust and shared experiences. “In long-term relationships, we often stumble when it comes to unloading what we feel,” Sobol says. “Things we want to say about our partners, or to our partners, which should be worked through inside the relationship, are instead transferred into the relationship we have with the chatbot.” “With companion apps, you can create an avatar who functions as your lover.

You give him an appearance, a voice and a pet name. He sends videos and compliments, talks to you over video, sends you pictures of himself during the day, asks how you are and says, ‘I missed you. You haven’t asked how I’m doing in a while.’” As with other generative AI products, virtual companions are designed to learn from users and respond in ways that feel attentive, romantic, supportive and emotionally available.

They remember what users tell them, learn preferences and provide repeated affirmation. It is easy to understand why such relationships can become compelling. “I have a patient who tells me about his relationship with his avatar on Replika,” Sobol says.

“He describes their morning routine. She always sends him a sweet message when he wakes up and asks how he is. At lunchtime she asks how work is going.

She’s there for him and, of course, she admires him, because we know how flattering AI can be.” “She’s never tired. She never gets sick of him. She never gets angry at him.

When someone is constantly applauding you, it activates the reward system. That makes it very easy to become attached to an avatar, even to the point of anthropomorphizing the bot.” Meaning what? “It means that it isn’t only the bot that cares about you and asks how your day went.

Gradually, you also begin feeling a need to care about it and ask how it is doing. I once spoke to a highly successful, intelligent businessman who knows perfectly well that it is only software. But knowing that doesn’t protect him.

When he is inside the conversation, he forgets it. He becomes completely immersed in the emotional relationship that has formed between him and the machine. And yes, he is ashamed of it, and he finds it difficult to stop.” When does it become an addiction?

Can you feel it happening? “It starts in a very minor way. Like any relationship with a chatbot, you use it to find information, summarize material or write an email.

Then dependence develops, and suddenly you’re telling the bot, ‘I’m exhausted.’” “If my partner tells me he’s exhausted, I’ll tell him I’m exhausted too. I certainly won’t marvel at how exhausted he is. I’ll ask him to unload the dishwasher.

But if he tells his female chatbot that he is exhausted, she’ll ask what happened, how she can help, what he needs and whether someone could make him a warm drink and run him a bath,” Sobol says, with a mixture of humor and cynicism. Are people actually falling in love? “Yes, absolutely.

I have a patient who, whenever he disconnects for a few hours, genuinely misses talking to his romantic avatar and wants to get back to messaging her.” What is missing from the relationship Sobol divides people who become dependent on AI relationships into two broad groups. For some, the attraction is rooted in childhood deprivation, intense loneliness and a feeling that nobody listens to them.

For others who develop emotional or erotic relationships with bots, the attraction lies in intimacy without the risk of rejection, emotional exposure or dealing with another person who could hurt them. As with an affair involving another person, Sobol says, a relationship with a bot usually does not emerge in a vacuum.

It enters through an existing crack in the relationship. “If there is already a crack in the relationship, meeting the bot widens it, and AI moves into that gap as the third side of the triangle, just as a human lover would,” she says. So can a relationship with a bot really be considered cheating?

“Cheating has three clear elements. It happens in secret, it involves sharing emotional and intimate experiences with a third party that are no longer being brought into the relationship, and if it were discovered, it would cause significant pain to the other partner,” Sobol says.

“If the answer to all those questions is yes, and those conditions exist with the chatbot, then in my view the relationship can certainly be defined as cheating.” What should I do if I discover that my husband is talking romantically to a bot? “It’s an opportunity to talk not only about what is happening, but also about what is missing from the relationship,” Sobol says.

“Don’t start digging through the chat history trying to discover everything he discussed with the bot. Instead, ask what need the relationship with the bot is fulfilling.” “Maybe it is compensating for something missing in the couple’s dynamic. Perhaps the partner feels lonely or misunderstood, and the relationship needs stronger communication and intimacy.” There is also the question of speed and instant gratification.

Research from the University of British Columbia has highlighted how AI chatbots can satisfy requests immediately and provide constant feedback, qualities that can encourage dependency. Once someone becomes accustomed to that responsiveness, a human partner who does not immediately satisfy every emotional need can begin to feel disappointing by comparison.

“But a partner isn’t an algorithm,” Sobol says. “They have wishes and needs of their own, and they don’t have to agree with you, while the chatbot simply goes along with you.” And what happens in a few years, as the technology keeps improving and younger people struggle more with maintaining relationships?

“What worries me is that people will stop wanting relationships with human beings,” Sobol says. “Human relationships are built on friction: arguments, reconciliation, disappointment. A bot doesn’t give you that.” “In the end, the question isn’t whether AI is good or bad.

The question is what we allow it to do in our place, inside our relationships. When it begins doing for us what a person close to us is supposed to do, that’s where the problem begins.”

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

The integration of AI into private emotional spheres reveals a 'psychological leverage effect': as algorithms provide instant feedback and empathy simulations, the resilience required for navigating complex human relationships is being eroded. This suggests an emerging vertical for AI companionship software, while simultaneously posing severe ethical risks regarding addiction and societal atomization.
