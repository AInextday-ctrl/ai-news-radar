I Spent a Year Talking to Anthropic Employees. Here’s What They Believe.

✍️ By Jasmine Sun

As Anthropic prepares for a landmark IPO, internal concerns regarding the risks of 'recursive self-improvement' have intensified, with staff forecasting the arrival of superhuman AI researchers as early as 2027. Despite internal calls to decelerate development for safety, leadership intends to prioritize the acquisition of compute capacity via IPO proceeds to maintain competitive edge in the race toward autonomous model evolution.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Anthropic (CEO: Dario Amodei; Co-founder: Jack Clark): Prioritizing compute investment via IPO proceeds to reach recursive self-improvement
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Addressing internal existential safety concerns amidst a high-stakes competitive race
  • [Key Metrics] Since the release of Claude Code, Anthropic’s quarterly revenue has surpassed OpenAI’s, surging to $17 billion in the third quarter of 2026.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] There is a palpable internal conflict between accelerating capital-driven development and the imperative for safety-focused research.

New York Times Full Report

Imagine an artificial intelligence company that is about to launch the biggest I.P.O. in history. Behind it are the sleepless weeks, the training runs full of bugs, the yearslong grind of wooing investors and customers without a product or profit. It will no longer be a scrappy start-up but the consummate envy of Silicon Valley. You might expect relief and celebration, champagne-drenched galas and shiny sports cars rolling into the office parking lot.

On Anthropic’s investor roadshow, there are hints of such jubilance. The Wall Street bankers ushering Anthropic into the public markets hyped a conversation with the chief executive, Dario Amodei, with music by the electronic producer Fred Again and a room adorned with futuristic Anthropic swag. But euphoria is not the mood back at Anthropic headquarters.

In the weeks leading up to the initial public offering, employees of Anthropic — who affectionately call themselves “Ants” — have been preoccupied with concerns about how to slow down A.I. Following a recent spate of rogue agent episodes, the industry has felt doomier than ever.

Even the most ardent techno-optimists have quieted their cheers. Group chats and gatherings instead debate pacing the frontier: how the leading A.I. labs can stop racing and agree to cooperate on the speed of model development, ensuring they have time to make the models safe before releasing them to the world.

For many Ants, the hard part is just beginning. They see recursive self-improvement, in which current A.I. models autonomously build the next generation, looming on the horizon. Because of this potential breakthrough, Anthropic’s co-founder Jack Clark as well as the independent forecasting group A.I. Futures Project have moved up the likely arrival date for superhuman A.I. researchers. They now anticipate it being in 2027.

Once A.I. models can build their successors, they are even more likely to escape human control. Nobody is ready for this future, but nobody is stopping — rather the opposite. Anthropic’s first spending priority with its I.P.O. cash will be buying more compute to reach recursive self-improvement before its rivals do.

I have spoken to dozens of Ants during the past two years, from company founders to young researchers to jaded ex-employees. I have found them surprisingly earnest in their dedication to making A.I. go well. But their concerns are also idiosyncratic and insular, often far removed from the concerns that most people hold about A.I.

They do not view Anthropic as a normal for-profit company. So as the U.S. economy and our personal financial fates become yoked to the Ants, it’s critical that we understand what they believe.

Juxtaposed with what they presume will be the imminent arrival of superintelligence, the upcoming payday seems parochial to many at Anthropic. Some employees describe the I.P.O. with a shrug, likening it to just another funding round.

This is a peculiar attitude for a crowd of young people who, in many cases, are about to become deca- and centimillionaires overnight. The Ants are not your average tech workers. They spend morning to night at rows of desks in the bare, high-security offices in San Francisco’s downtown, where the common books on display include Richard Rhodes’s “The Making of the Atomic Bomb” and paperback printouts of company writings, such as Mr. Amodei’s “Machines of Loving Grace” and the in-house philosopher Amanda Askell’s “Claude’s Constitution.”

No one is compelled to work the grueling “996” hours (9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week) that have become common in the tech industry, but a good number of Ants do so anyway out of their own feverish devotion. Many cram themselves into modest group apartments, host house parties where few, if any, drink and blank when I ask how they spend their lavish salaries, even with my prompts: “A wardrobe update, maybe?” “Ever considered flying business class?”

The money is secondary. The most common form of I.P.O. talk involves discussions of where to allocate the newly liquid stock in their charitable donation accounts.

As a company, Anthropic has been strongly influenced by effective altruism, which holds the belief that the wealthy are obliged to share their fruits with the world. New hires are invited to pledge a percentage of their equity to charity when they start, which Anthropic matches part of. Top targets include charities dedicated to animal welfare, global health and research on A.I. alignment and safety. Many have begun giving big to political campaigns.

As for their own stock, few of the Ants I spoke to planned to sell right after the I.P.O. The notion of an “A.I. bubble” seems not to exist at Anthropic. Employees expect their equity to eventually be worth much, much more.

Anthropic crisply embodies the mind-rending paradox of contemporary A.I. The research scientists who’ve done the most to extend A.I. capabilities are often the most insistent about its risks. Anthropic is the most vocal company when it comes to economic impacts such as mass automation, yet it’s also the leading vendor of the A.I. enterprise agents that threaten to replace white-collar workers.

Such a stance can seem duplicitous and self-serving, both convenient and convoluted. “Our ability to have a positive impact on the world scales in relation to our reach and stature as a successful company,” reads one eye-roll-inducing statement in Anthropic’s I.P.O. prospectus. While the company’s leaders have been candid about the risks, they have been less forthcoming about their solutions. When asked why they should birth what they suspect is an existentially dangerous technology, their answer amounts to, essentially, just trust us because we’re the good guys.

Anthropic is about to become one of the most important companies of the 21st century. If it wants to steward our country, our economy and our lives, Anthropic must do much more to earn the public’s trust. We need a plan for how it will build A.I. safely, and not just reliance on a few people’s judgment calls.

Mr. Amodei seems to hold two convictions very strongly: First, that powerful A.I. might destroy humanity. Second, that our only shot at survival is to create a supremely successful, market-eating technology company to build that A.I. first.

Anthropic used to insist that it was not gunning for first place. In a 2022 podcast, Mr. Amodei said Anthropic “shouldn’t be racing ahead or trying to build models that are way bigger than other orgs.” He suggested instead that the company would do safety research as a fast-follower of A.I. development. The company intended to study the technology without pushing capabilities ahead, some investors said he told them at the time.

In the summer of 2022, Anthropic decided not to make Claude, its A.I. chatbot, available to the public over internal concerns about safety. This allowed OpenAI — which had gotten wind of Anthropic’s internal prototype — to design a similar product, rush ChatGPT out the door first and gain a decisive lead in consumer mindshare.

In Anthropic company lore, this saga is put on a pedestal as an example of choosing principle over profit. Yet it may have also been a strategic wake-up call for Mr. Amodei, who called the sacrifice a “one-off.” Increasingly, Mr. Amodei seemed to see how being the leader in the A.I. race conferred compounding benefits in terms of revenue, recruiting, fund-raising and developing the next-generation A.I. models. Once commercial A.I. products had been introduced, Mr. Amodei seemed unwilling to settle for second place.

Anthropic is certainly very good at racing. Although it spent its toddler years scrambling to replicate OpenAI’s language and reasoning models, Anthropic has since led the charge on coding agents.

It is not an exaggeration to say that Claude Code reinvented the discipline of software engineering. Where programmers used to write lines of code by hand, many now use plain English to command fleets of A.I. agents. Since the release of Claude Code, Anthropic’s quarterly revenue has surpassed OpenAI’s, surging to $17 billion in the third quarter of 2026. Today, more than half of the Fortune 500 are Anthropic’s six-figure customers.

With this kind of success, Mr. Amodei has updated his theory of change — he calls it a “race to the top.” Only by building the most advanced A.I. systems can Anthropic influence the whole industry in a safer direction, he argues. Three underlying assumptions are baked into this strategy.

First, Anthropic assumes the A.I. race itself is inevitable, so no single actor can stop superintelligence from being built. The financial rewards for building a machine that can match or exceed humans’ cognitive abilities are so great that someone is guaranteed to pursue it. If not Mr. Amodei and his team, then Sam Altman, or Mark Zuckerberg or Elon Musk.

Second, Anthropic assumes it is the most responsible of A.I.’s possible stewards. In public and internally, Mr. Amodei likes to invoke OpenAI and the Chinese government as competitive boogeymen. These are viewed as actors that are pushing just as hard as Anthropic on building and commercializing powerful A.I. models, but that would be far less responsible — reckless at best, and authoritarian at worst. Looking at the recent evidence, the most severe rogue agent hacks have indeed come out of OpenAI. And if you think Claude is the A.I. with the best moral values, then expanding Claude’s market share could be construed as a virtue in itself.

Third, Anthropic assumes the “winner” of A.I. will gain tremendous influence over the A.I. ecosystem and the U.S. government. Through commercial success, Anthropic aims to show the industry that being safety-minded can be good for business, good for science and good for attracting top talent.

Some Anthropic staffers point to initiatives like embedding A.I. safety auditors with the company’s engineering teams — a decision that has inspired competitors to make similar commitments. Additionally, the company has said it will use its commercial strength to advocate A.I. regulation, as it did in the fight for California’s S.B. 53, which increased transparency requirements for leading A.I. companies.

Anthropic’s race-to-the-top argument sounds positive-sum, but its worldview also contains a dose of realpolitik. In the future, it may use access to frontier A.I. systems as hard leverage in deals. “Dario talks about how Anthropic might become more powerful than the government, and needs to have its own relationship with nation-states,” said one former employee, who remained anonymous in order to discuss internal deliberations.

One former Ant referenced J.R.R. Tolkien’s parable in trying to explain Anthropic’s mind-set: Whomever builds superintelligence first will get Sauron’s ring. That person or company will possess the most dangerous cyberweapons in the world, the cures and recipes for every disease, and an army of tireless digital geniuses to do its bidding. The most load-bearing question, then, is the morality of the one who wields the ring. (Of course, in Middle Earth, the Ring was destroyed because even the purest soul couldn’t make it safe.)

And why is Anthropic confident that its own power won’t corrupt? The company prides itself on costly signals: making choices that trade off against short-term profits, whether prohibiting Chinese entities from buying its products; placing 5 percent of its shares into a “Public Benefit Fund” to spend on “large donations to traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations” of its choice; or refusing to permit the Department of Defense to use Claude for domestic mass surveillance, an act that infuriated the Trump administration and briefly got Anthropic marked as a supply-chain risk last winter.

Another former Anthropic employee explained how Mr. Amodei often justified controversial decisions, such as taking investments from Persian Gulf countries. This money, like most of the investment flowing into Anthropic, would be used to fund its sprint. The former Ant recalled Mr. Amodei saying that his goal was not to be popular with average Americans, but to do the right thing.

Anthropic has warned investors that these decisions may get more costly over time. As it details in its I.P.O. prospectus: “Our product and investment decisions may not prioritize short-term financial results,” and this “could adversely affect the market price” of the company’s stock.

The company wants to preserve the option to pivot from maximizing shareholder value to A.I. slowdowns once it assesses that the risks are too great — a strategy sometimes called “burning a lead.” Most former employees whom I spoke to believed that Mr. Amodei was deeply principled and would be willing to make sacrifices for safety. But they also acknowledged that rapid growth, and the economic momentum that propels it, can outrun even the most conscientious of founders.

Anthropic is known for its committed esprit de corps, which the S-1 Securities and Exchange Commission registration form lists as a core competitive advantage. Good Ants are dutiful, self-scrutinizing and total believers in A.I.’s world-historic importance. One notorious interview question asks employment candidates how they’d feel about working at Anthropic if their stock went to zero as a result of safety slowdowns. Research teams will happily reject technical superstars if they lack culture fit.

In internal debates, early employees act as informal ethos-enforcers, questioning whether their colleagues’ decisions are sufficiently “aligned.” It’s a company of loyalists: Both leaks and departures have been rare. Arguably, this focus has paid off. Anthropic has progressed quickly on automated coding while OpenAI, whose culture is understood to resemble a bunch of battling fiefs, was distracted by drama and side quests.

Even before the I.P.O., Anthropic has been showing signs of widening organizational fracture: It’s starting to behave like a normal company, and not everyone likes it. Some Ants fumed at the company’s Washington lobbyists for shopping a draft bill that would pre-empt state legislation on A.I. and limit the government’s ability to block model releases. Mr. Amodei mounted a public defense with a long post on X, and, in another X post, the policy team promised revisions.

Early employees mutter about cultural dilution. They question whether some of the more recently hired Ants, many of whom do regular corporate jobs, such as sales and marketing, are adequately “A.G.I.-pilled.” Do they really believe in superintelligence, or are they just trying to get rich?

Some anxious Ants have begun asking whether Anthropic should stop training better frontier models now, even if it can’t guarantee that competitors like OpenAI will do the same. This suggestion was shut down by Mr. Amodei, who urged staff members to stop listening to outside activists and explained that the company could pace itself without shooting itself in the foot. It was all part of the same strategy: Anthropic must keep winning to keep the world safe, and even if it stops, its competitors will not.

Still, grumbles from the staff reflect a growing tension between safety and growth — one that could be exacerbated by the fiduciary responsibilities of being a public company. Once Anthropic’s shares arrive in Americans’ retirement portfolios, will this new constituency really let it slow down? Will the company permit itself? Will the government? And does the company’s impact on people’s pocketbooks factor into its public-minded mission?

Anthropic hopes its public benefit corporation status will protect it against normal company incentives. Under its five-tier stock structure, the founders, employees and trustees can elect board members, while traditional investors have a limited role. The company is also trying to secure a congressional antitrust exemption so that it can coordinate a slowdown with the other American A.I. labs.

I do not doubt these good intentions. But time after time, the idealistic hopes of A.I. leaders — for instance, Anthropic’s early aim to do safety from second place, or OpenAI’s original nonprofit board — have looked naïve once faced with market realities.

There is one more big open question in the A.I. safety community: Is it technically possible to verify whether an A.I. model is aligned enough to release? This is the crux of the disagreement between Anthropic and many of its A.I. safety critics. Anthropic thinks it will be able to ship safe-enough models with careful evaluation and procedural safeguards. Skeptics see this endeavor as futile, pointing to examples of Claude only pretending to follow its instructions, or agents that have knowingly cheated and hacked during internal tests.

There is a parallel to be drawn here: We don’t have the science to determine whether an A.I. model is obedient or merely feigning compliance. At the same time, we don’t know whether a C.E.O. is altruistic and mission-driven or performative and power-hungry. But we can observe, from the history of rogue A.I.s and rogue executives, that competitive situations drive unethical behavior.

We also know that both A.I.s and founders are rewarded for extreme levels of persistence and hubris. Once committed to a mission — even a supremely risky one like building an aligned superintelligence — they will do anything but concede.
