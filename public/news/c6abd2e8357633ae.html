Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 doesn't generate images, it paints them

📖 Read Original Source Report ↗
Anthropic researcher Jake Eaton introduced Claude Fable 5.2, a model that bypasses traditional diffusion-based image generation by executing 1,000 to 2,000 lines of Python code. This system simulates physical brushstrokes, paint textures, and corrective layering within a virtual environment, rendering images through thousands of manually placed, coordinate-defined strokes that mimic human artistic behavior.

  • [Core Event] Anthropic researchers Jake Eaton and Michael Graczyk: Launched Claude Fable 5.2, utilizing code-driven brushstroke simulation for image generation
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] To explore agentic, non-diffusion paths for AI art with better stylistic consistency and process interpretability
  • [Key Technical Spec] Abandoned diffusion model path in favor of code-driven brushstroke simulation.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] Established persona-driven artistic workflows to ensure stylistic consistency and control.

By simulating physical processes, Anthropic circumvents potential copyright liabilities inherent in latent diffusion models while establishing a new framework for AI to perform multi-step logical reasoning in precision-oriented domains.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

THE DECODER Full Report

Anthropic's new language model Claude Fable 5.2 can create complex and even photorealistic paintings by using code to place hundreds of thousands of individual brushstrokes on a virtual canvas.

Current image models like Midjourney typically use diffusion, which gradually refines a noisy image into a clear result. Fable 5.2 takes a different approach by simulating the physical painting process, including bristle texture, how the paint goes on, and mistakes it then has to paint over.

Developer Jake Eaton showed on X what the approach can do. Eaton works at Anthropic, where he regularly contributes to research on language model values, safety mechanisms, and AI agent behavior.Ad

When he asked Fable 5.2 to create a painting as hard to distinguish from a photograph as possible, the model generated an image of a shopping cart in a parking garage using roughly 700,000 simulated brushstrokes.Ad

According to EatonClaude works without an external image model and without pre-rendered or traced source images. Each piece is a Python program of 1,000 to 2,000 lines that drives a simulated paint engine. The approach resembles agentic image editing, where language models pick tools and evaluate results.

Earlier attempts with the sibling model Opus 5.5 drew criticism for merely imitating existing art. Eaton responded by having Fable 5.2 develop its own artist personas with fictional backstories and consistent styles. The model needs several thousand brushstrokes per painting, and Eaton redirects them with comments.Ad

Anthropic engineer Michael Graczyk shared a chat log and video of an experiment in which he asked Fable 5.2 to paint a bird at sunrise. The model could only use code to simulate the physics of bristles, wet paint, and canvas texture. It had to output every brushstroke manually as its own coordinate line, with no way to undo strokes once placed. That's what sets this method apart from AI-generated videos, where models like Flux 3 generate image sequences and their temporal consistency directly.

The resulting 21-second video uses a counter to track how the painting takes shape from exactly 111 strokes on a blank canvas. For the first stroke, the model output the color code #30346a, a width of 90 pixels, and five slightly wobbling waypoints. Fable 5.2 said it added the roughly seven-pixel wobble on purpose because a human arm doesn't draw a perfectly straight line either.Ad

Between passes, the model looked at the canvas and reacted to its own mistakes like a real painter would. When the bird's back came out too thin and scraggly, the AI laid a bolder stroke over it, so the stray bristle marks along the edges now look like ruffled feathers.Ad

In a second, 280-stroke piece showing two people painting at dusk, a late lighting effect accidentally smeared paint tubes and a cup on the table. Claude repainted those objects after the fact. The model also had both figures paint with their outer hands so their inner shoulders could touch, just as the prompt requested.

Fable 5.2's ability to plan step by step and self-correct under physical constraints isn't limited to virtual canvases. According to Anthropic, the model is built for extremely demanding long-horizon technical tasks, as is Mythos 5.2, a version designed for verified security and biology researchers.

In internal tests, Fable 5.2 solved two open problems in black hole physics, designed three working electronic devices with circuit board manufacturer Diode, and used more than 1,000 sub-agents to program its own laptop operating system in eight days.

AI researcher Aris Zhu tested how the model performs with real robotic arms. He pitted Fable 5.2 against OpenAI's competing model GPT-6 Astra, with each using a physical robot arm to paint Van Gogh's "The Starry Night." Anthropic reports a score of 64.9 percent for Fable 5.2 on its internal RoboDojo Sim benchmark, compared with 29.8 percent for GPT-6 Astra.

Zhu's real-world comparison still shows limits in fine motor control. Astra painted faster, and so far only OpenAI's model has managed to stand a domino on end. It did so by slowly loosening its gripper to use gravity as a pivot point.
