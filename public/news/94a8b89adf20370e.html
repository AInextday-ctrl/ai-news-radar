Anthropic revenue target: $600bn, soonish

Good morning. Yesterday, while we were all waiting for Anthropic’s IPO to price (more on that below), our colleagues reported that Leopold Aschenbrenner of Situational Awareness was scheming to reboot the hedge fund after its spectacular meltdown in July. The timing of this attempt with the huge IPO may not be a coincidence. Situational Awareness has a $5bn stake in Anthropic — and Aschenbrenner’s wife is chief of staff to its CEO Dario Amodei. It’s a small world, after all. Send us your thoughts: [email protected].

It is IPO day for Anthropic, and the shares have priced at $83, valuing the company at $1.5tn. This newsletter is therefore obliged to add a few drops to the roaring river of ink already poured out on the topic of whether the company can be worth so much.

We haven’t the slightest idea how to answer this question with any rigour, and we don’t think anyone else does either. So we will change the topic slightly and ask: what will Anthropic have to do over the medium to long term — the next five to 10 years, say — in order to be considered a financial success? Specifically, how much revenue does it need to earn?

Start with return on invested capital (which is operating profit after tax/capital invested). Anthropic has approximately $901bn in off-balance-sheet financial commitments, “substantially all of which is related to [buying] compute and infrastructure” (that’s from the S-1, page 173), plus another set of commitments the exact dollar value of which is “not yet determinable”, but that the company estimates at $150bn to $175bn. So let’s call it an even trillion. The commitments are spread out over the next 15 years or so, but $700bn comes due in the next five, reckons the Zion Research Group, which specialises in accounting research.

Let’s suppose, generously, that this trillion is all the capital that is going to sit inside the business. What sort of return will investors expect on that? I think something like 20 per cent is fair. This is the neighbourhood of other tech mega-companies. Five-year average ROIC at Alphabet, Apple, Meta and Microsoft are 19, 43, 19 and 20 per cent, respectively, according to S&P Capital IQ. Twenty per cent ROIC implies $200bn a year in operating profit after tax.

Proceeding in this same gestural but hopefully not insane fashion, assume an operating margin of 40 per cent (that same cohort of peers comes in at 30, 31, 37 and 44 per cent, respectively), and an effective tax rate of 17 per cent (same drill: 16, 17, 19 and 17). All that together implies a revenue run-rate of $602bn a year (ie, {200/[1-.17]}/.4).

(Don’t like my calculation? By all means, email me yours.)

This is a big number — even if some Wall Street analysts have pencilled in an even bigger one for 2030. For comparison: current annual revenue at Alphabet, Apple, Meta and Microsoft is $471bn, $478bn, $241bn and $346bn, respectively. Alternatively, you could compare the number to the entire software and IT services sector of the S&P 1500 index (which captures almost all large-, mid- and small-cap US stocks): 76 companies with $908bn in revenue.

So, if you plan to buy the IPO and hold it, you just need to answer a simple question, put to me by Richard Kramer of Arete Research: where is all that revenue going to come from?

The Jensen Huang, er, affair has drawn renewed attention to semiconductor export controls. Until last week, Nvidia and Anthropic represented competing poles in that debate.

Huang, Nvidia’s former chief executive, was the AI industry’s loudest opponent of restrictions on chip sales to China. In April, he told the Dwarkesh Podcast:

“. . . of course we’re going to send them compute. So what? The fact of the matter is that we get the benefit. Don’t forget, we get the benefit of American technology leadership.”

. . . of course we’re going to send them compute. So what? The fact of the matter is that we get the benefit. Don’t forget, we get the benefit of American technology leadership.

Huang was, of course, motivated to argue for greater market access for his company. The short seller Culpepper Research estimated in May that more than a fifth of Nvidia’s computing revenue was driven by demand from China, despite US export restrictions.

Huang wants to be a free trader and national security hawk at the same time. He told the Dwarkesh Podcast:

“To concede that market [China] for the United States technology industry is a disservice to our country. It is a disservice to our national security. It is a disservice to our technology leadership”

To concede that market [China] for the United States technology industry is a disservice to our country. It is a disservice to our national security. It is a disservice to our technology leadership

Anthropic’s chief executive, Amodei, has spent years arguing for restrictions on China’s access to Nvidia’s most powerful chips. According to Kevin Roose’s The AGI Chronicles, Amodei and co-founder Jack Clark were advocating for export controls as early as 2021. But, again, this is a position Amodei is motivated to take. Export controls hobble his Chinese competition.

The difference in views is striking because Nvidia is one of Anthropic’s financial backers. It invested in the AI model maker earlier this year and committed to buying $10bn of the stock at the IPO price today.

Huang’s fall may prove to be Amodei’s gain. Nvidia said on Tuesday that it would stop selling advanced chips to China and established a committee to oversee national security and trade compliance. Meanwhile, the US is moving to close the loophole that allowed Chinese companies to access those chips by renting computing power from data centres in south-east Asia and Brazil. The upshot is Nvidia may find itself selling fewer chips to Asia and more to Anthropic. Two-nil to Amodei.
