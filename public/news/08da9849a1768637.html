A.I. Helps Find Major Security Bug in Java, a Software Platform Vital to the Internet

✍️ By Dustin Volz and Dylan Freedman

OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber, a specialized model for cybersecurity research that significantly enhances security audit and patching performance through optimized alignment. It has uncovered 81 high-severity flaws in Firefox and developed 948 exploits for Linux kernel vulnerabilities, currently restricted to authorized security teams due to its 'Critical' classification.

  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] To improve security across large codebases and preemptively close zero-day threats
  • [Regulatory & Investigation Focus] Features capabilities for autonomous vulnerability discovery, patch generation, and red-teaming.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New York Times Full Report

A cybersecurity start-up revealed on Tuesday it had used a new A.I. model to uncover a critical software bug in Java, a popular programming language that is used by government agencies and about 90 percent of Fortune 500 companies.

It was the latest evidence of a reality facing companies and the government, as A.I. is revolutionizing the field of discovery in computer vulnerabilities, identifying flaws in code so rapidly that cybersecurity professionals are struggling to keep up.

Using leading A.I. models, the start-up Calif has now unearthed a string of serious vulnerabilities in recent months. The start-up’s latest effort exposed a flaw, using OpenAI’s recently released GPT-6 Cyber model, in Java, which is widely used by banks, hospitals, the aviation industry and technology giants, as well as government agencies, including those with national security missions.

“Java is the beating heart of the modern economy,” said Bruce Dang, a senior researcher at Calif. “Practically speaking, all major companies” will be affected by the bug, he added.

Mr. Dang and his colleagues have worked in recent days to coordinate with all government agencies in the intelligence-sharing alliance known as Five Eyes — which consists of the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Britain — and major vendors in the private sector, including Google and Microsoft.

The National Security Agency, whose classified network went down on Tuesday for what it described as “routine software maintenance,” did not respond to a request for comment. It was not clear whether that disruption was related to addressing the Java bug.

Oracle, the owner of the Java trademark and provider of commercial Java licenses, did not respond to a request for comment. The company released a software patch Tuesday.

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, in an alert, pressed organizations to “immediately patch” the vulnerability.

Announcements about exploits from Calif and other groups using A.I. tools have startled the cybersecurity world and upended what was considered achievable by a small band of coders just a few months ago. Those efforts, which involve working with A.I. models before they are publicly released, help alert network defenders to undetected problems in order to fix them before malicious hackers find them and wreak havoc.

But the Java discovery may be Calif’s most serious yet, one that could enable a hacker to gain remote access to sensitive internal systems. It comes on the heels of a cascade of seemingly independent disruptions in recent days — outages of Microsoft products, a nationwide grounding of flights and banking interruptions — that have shaken public confidence in the reliability of everyday technology and fanned fears that A.I. may be to blame. (So far, the explanations for each appear to be relatively benign.)

A.I.-assisted bug hunting and cyberattack building has made it much easier to identify weaknesses in systems, said Ollie Whitehouse, the chief technology officer of the National Cyber Security Center in Britain. “We have seen this taking shape for some time, with A.I. being used to discover a growing number of latent vulnerabilities across software and the digital services we all rely on.”

The Java vulnerability is the second major bug Calif has unearthed with the aid of OpenAI models in the past few weeks. It discovered last month security flaws in PHP, a programming language estimated to be used by 70 percent of websites and apps on the internet.

The PHP disclosure set off fears around the world about significant disruptions for consumers and businesses and a mad scramble by companies to patch PHP before hackers could pounce. Government agencies, including the N.S.A., warned businesses to take websites offline if they could not immediately deploy a patch. Disruptions ultimately proved less serious than some predicted, however, as it became clear hackers could not easily reverse-engineer the attack method.

But the PHP flaws chiefly concerned software that helps websites function. In contrast, Java helps power the nuts and bolts of the modern digital world, including cloud computing architecture, identity management tools, email services and more.

“Java is everywhere,” said Thai Duong, the chief executive of Calif. He noted that the Java bug was not easy to weaponize into an attack, giving defenders more time to patch the issues.

“But vulnerabilities have a very long tail,” he added, predicting the bug could be a threat to systems that do not update in the next five to 10 years.

Among the most significant findings, Calif demonstrated that it could use this bug to break into Keycloak, a widely used authentication and log-in system. Keycloak protects thousands of organizations, including CERN, Hewlett Packard, UnitedHealthcare and NASA.

Quang Luong, a security researcher from Calif, discovered the bug in Java’s base64 decoder, a common algorithm that is used to translate binary computer data into a legible palette of text that includes lowercase and uppercase letters, numbers and punctuation. On certain computers, researchers found that Java encounters a bug when it tries to decode 64 “A” characters followed by one equal sign:

“AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA=”

This bug, called Java4Shell, causes Java to erroneously execute code by tampering with a section of computer memory, which typically makes the program immediately crash. With the help of A.I., Calif researchers demonstrated that they could corrupt data by carefully arranging the memory — a technique called “heap grooming” — granting them full unauthorized access to the underlying system.

The New York Times independently verified Calif’s discovery after the start-up shared proof-of-concept details about the attack. The successful test took less than an hour.

The rapid advances with artificial intelligence in cybersecurity efforts mirror what the chess world champion Garry Kasparov observed after he lost a match to IBM’s Deep Blue computer nearly 30 years ago.

The best games of chess would not be played by humans or computers, he predicted, but by a hybrid of the two. The concept, which he called a “centaur” in a nod to the mythological half-human, half-horse creature, took off. For years, centaurs were seen as the best chess players, reaching new heights in the game.

Now companies and the government are contending with the threat of the “centaur hacker” — half A.I., half human, and fully capable of creating chaos on the internet.

The most alarming discoveries — for now, at least — still require human expertise. In recent months, small teams of veteran security researchers have used top-shelf A.I. models to build in days or hours the kinds of advanced cyberattacks that once took platoons of elite hackers at spy agencies like the National Security Agency months to construct.

Fouad Matin, who leads cyber preparedness and safety at OpenAI, said Calif’s Java research “shows how A.I. can assist expert security researchers with finding serious flaws in software we all depend on” and helps lead to collaborative fixes. “We want more security teams to have these capabilities and tools to help make critical software more secure for everyone,” Mr. Matin said.

Eduardo Vela Nava, an information security engineer at Google, said that “once notified, we swiftly rolled out mitigations and patches across Google services, developed detection to ensure there was no exploitation of the vulnerability, and provided guidance to our customers.”

Security experts expect those risks are likely to grow, at least in the short term. For about 20 years, centaur chess players were widely seen as capable of playing the best game of chess. But eventually, the centaurs were eclipsed by more advanced supercomputers.

Mr. Duong believes it may be only a matter of time until A.I. can independently build the most advanced cyberattacks, which could usher in a new age of unpredictable problems. The F.B.I. this week issued an alert warning that hackers were using new autonomous A.I. hacking tools to target government agencies and businesses around the world.

“I don’t know how much time we have — maybe three months, maybe three years,” Mr. Duong said. “But right now, the best hacker is the centaur hacker.”

As these capabilities scale, software supply chain security will shift from manual audits to model-based adversarial defense, forcing regulators to re-evaluate export controls on high-tier offensive AI tools.
