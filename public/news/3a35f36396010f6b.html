Marco Rubio says there's "zero evidence" linking the recent FAA ground stop, Microsoft outage, and bank service issues, and the US isn't "under systemic attack" (Kiki Intarasuwan/CBS News)

✍️ By CBS News

U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated during a press conference in the Philippines that recent technical outages affecting Microsoft, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), and major banks are isolated incidents. Rubio emphasized that intelligence assessments show no evidence of malicious cyber activity, foreign state involvement, or a systemic attack on U.S. infrastructure.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Marco Rubio, U.S. Secretary of State: Disclaimed any link between recent tech outages and malicious cyber/geopolitical attacks
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] To mitigate public and financial market concerns following a series of infrastructure failures
  • [Key Fact Detail] Official denial of foreign state or cyber-attack involvement in recent outages.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] Reiteration of administration's vigilance regarding infrastructure vulnerability monitoring.

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

High-frequency infrastructure failures reveal critical vulnerabilities in the U.S. software supply chain, where single-point failures in complex systems trigger massive cascading effects. While the executive branch's attribution aims to curb market panic, the reality underscores a systemic deficiency in disaster recovery architectures and oversight protocols for critical digital assets.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

CBS News Full Report

Secretary of State Marco Rubio said there's "zero evidence" that recent tech outages in U.S. air travel, banking and other systems are connected. "The Microsoft incident has nothing to do with what we saw with the FAA. The FAA has nothing to do with what we saw with the banks a few days ago.

These issues that we've seen from a technical standpoint are completely unrelated one from another," Rubio said Wednesday morning local time during a news conference in the Philippines. Rubio acknowledged that people are fearful and uncertain, saying he wanted to be clear that the United States is not under systemic attack.

"We have zero evidence or any reason to believe that any of these isolated or individual instances have anything to do with nefarious actors, with cyber hackers, with criminals, with another nation state, with a malign actor of any kind," Rubio said. Rubio, who is traveling with President Trump ahead of a trilateral meeting with Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping this week, added that the administration is "always on the lookout" for any potential attacks and reiterated multiple times that reports of technical issues are not interconnected."Each of them are distinct, and they're different in their causation," he said.On Thursday, Microsoft said it experienced a worldwide outage affecting services like Outlook and Teams, impacting users, companies and government operations worldwide.

The company said the outage was linked to a technical issue, not malicious or cyber activity.The following day, the Federal Aviation Administration issued a nationwide ground stop on all departing U.S. flights for several hours, throwing the aviation industry into turmoil. Federal officials identified a contractor performing maintenance updates as the cause of Friday's disruption, but key questions remain about the underlying cause of the systemwide failure.Other recent outages that have since been resolved include disruptions to major banks like JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America and U.S.

Bank, and issues with Apple's services. On Tuesday, the National Security Agency's internal network used to store and share classified information went offline, multiple U.S. officials familiar told CBS News. Democratic Sen.

Mark Warner, vice chair of the Senate Intelligence Committee, also said on Tuesday that there's no indication these incidents are the result of an attack, but noted that artificial intelligence is creating pressure for companies to deal with vulnerabilities."AI is making it possible to identify vulnerabilities across enormous numbers of systems at unprecedented speed, creating enormous pressure on companies and government agencies to patch those vulnerabilities immediately, often all at once, putting tremendous strain on systems that weren't built to handle that pace of change," Warner said in a post on X.

"It feels like every day brings another reminder that AI is moving faster than our ability to manage its consequences. This is exactly why I've been pushing for commonsense guardrails on AI and stronger cybersecurity protections."When asked Wednesday about the role of AI in recent incidents, Rubio said he didn't want to speculate because every incident is unique and referred to individual companies.
