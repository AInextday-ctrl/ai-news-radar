Fitbit Edge review: notable upgrade over the Charge 6 for GPS and heart rate, Google Health app is an improvement, but lacks software polish and NFC payments (Ray Maker/DC Rainmaker)

✍️ By DC Rainmaker

Google has launched the Fitbit Edge, a spiritual successor to the Fitbit Charge 6, featuring a 46% larger display, upgraded GPS and heart rate tracking, an altimeter, and a clickable physical button. However, reviewers note it still lacks software polish and NFC payment capabilities.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Google / Fitbit team (Wearable tech and fitness market): Released Fitbit Edge with upgraded display, GPS, and physical button, but lacking NFC and software polish
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] To refresh the classic Charge lineup and incorporate Google Pixel Watch design elements
  • [Key Metrics] 46% larger screen with upgraded GPS and heart rate.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] Lacks software polish and NFC payments.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

DC Rainmaker Full Report

The Fitbit Edge could most easily be called the Fitbit Charge 7, but sometimes switching things up is a good refresh for companies and brands, and so here we are. In many ways, the Fitbit Edge will feel very familiar to Fitbit Charge users, yet it’ll concurrently feel very much like a recent Google Pixel Watch. That’s both good and bad. There’s a lot of good things coming from the Pixel Watch 5 & Fitbit Air that have landed here. Yet equally, having used it the past few weeks, there’s a number of smaller things that aren’t quite what we had on the very popular Fitbit Charge series.

Speaking of which, both my wife and I have been using Fitbit Edge units over this last while on all of our workouts. Runs, rides, hikes, walks, gym stuff, and everything in between. With that, let’s dive into what’s new, and then all my daily and workout usage experiences.

The Fitbit Edge is the next version of the Fitbit Charge 6, which came out in 2023. Here’s how it’s different:

– Increased screen/display by 46% (has 1.34” display) – Case thickness is 11.5mm – Added physical side button that actually clicks (previous was just a touch area) – Added altimeter (for stair tracking, as well as elevation tracking) – Added automatic workout detection (now supports 100 sports) – Increased supported manual workout profiles to 50 workout profiles – Made user-replaceable/serviceable battery (the first Fitbit-branded product to do so) – GPS accuracy improvement claimed at 40% more accurate (via new GPS chipset, and new aluminum case design) – Switched to a more modern user interface, matching the Pixel Watch 5 series – Added structured workout support – Added support for dual usage with Pixel Watch and Fitbit Air (e.g., switching back and forth seamlessly) – Added fast charging (5 minutes gets you 1 day of usage) – Removed Google Pay (NFC Contactless payments) – Removed EDA Scan feature (but does have continuous stress monitoring in the background) – Changed the band attachment mechanism (old Fitbit habits die hard) – Switched the charger to the same charger as the Fitbit Air (hey, at least they picked something else they already had this time) – Added a bunch of band accessory types – Price increased to $179 (officially the Charge 6 was $159 previously, but realistically it sat between $99 and $129 virtually all the time) – Also a Stephen Curry model at $219 (US, Canada, EMEA)

So that covers the piles of new stuff, and some of the bits that were there before. But there are still numerous color schemes/models to choose from.

The Fitbit Air case comes in 5 different colorways, and then with that a huge slate of band options. Starting off with the core options you can buy, they are as follows:

Now, for most of our testing (my wife and I), we’ve been wearing the relatively basic/default Active band, which works perfectly fine and is easy to adjust/etc.

Still, we both wore other bands as well. Yesterday we had a fancy afternoon thing to go to, and swapped out to the Metal Link band. Hers was the champagne gold, and mine the brushed silver. Obviously neither quite perfectly matched with the models we had, but…well…that’s the models we had:

My only complaint here is that I found the clasp release button could actually get reasonably easily pressed. Ironically, it didn’t happen all day during most of the day, and then towards the end of the day, it was triggering constantly. My wife didn’t have this issue, so….yeah.

I’ve also worn the Active Sport band a bit, though I didn’t honestly find it all that meaningfully different than the Active band, in terms of sport wearability.

And finally have the Magnet Band, which is super cool and just snaps tight/shut.

– Display is 1.34”/34mm AMOLED display, supporting an always-on configuration – Display supports adjustable brightness – Bluetooth 5.4 – Processor: Ambiq AP5 MCU – GPS chipset supports GPS+Galileo in US, and GPS+Galileo+GLONASS+BeiDou+QZSS outside of US – Onboard memory: 64MB NOR flash memory, 7 days of detailed motion data (minute intervals), 30 days of daily totals, heart rate at 2-second intervals – Internal sensors: Altimeter, Accelerometer, Gyroscope, Red and infrared sensors for oxygen saturation (SpO2), Multipurpose electrical sensors compatible with ECG app, Photoplethysmography (PPG) optical heart rate sensor, Ambient light sensor, Far-field skin temperature sensor – Battery is 65mAh – Fully end-user replaceable battery – Charging speed: 5 minutes of fast charging for 1 day of battery life, or 100% in 90 minutes – Requires iOS 16.4 or newer, and Android 11 or newer – Fitbit Edge Weight: 17.7g without band – Fitbit Edge Dimensions: 39.22 x 25.56 mm – Fitbit Edge Height/Thickness: 11.58mm – Case is made from 100% recycled aluminum – Active band: Silicone with “soft-touch coating” – Active Band Sizing: Small fits wrists 130-170mm around, Large fits 170- 210 mm around – Stephen Curry band: “Recycled polyester yarns with water-repellent coating and stainless steel lugs, as well as a raised interior print for increased airflow.” – Stephen Curry band sizing (US): Small fits wrists 135-170mm around, Large fits 170-210mm around – Stephen Curry band sizing (EU/Canada): Single-size fits wrists 150-195mm around – Water resistance: 5ATM/50 meters – Dust immersion resistance: IP68

Phew, got all that? Good, let’s roll right into the basics of the unit day to day.

In this section, I’ll go through all the daily usage basics of the Fitbit Edge, like activity/step tracking, sleep tracking, and general health bits. Then after this I’ll cover workout pieces.

First up is interacting with the device. The Fitbit Edge gained a physical button on the right side, which quickly opens the apps menu, for things like accessing the alarms, ECG, exercise, media controls, and weather. Previously on the Fitbit Charge 6, this was a touch-sensitive area, but not a clicking button:

The button is great, and I’ve been super happy with it. Note that at present, long-pressing it doesn’t do anything.

Instead, for most other interactions, you’ll use the touchscreen, which allows you to swipe left/right/up/down, and tap it for what you need. Of course, the first thing you’ll see is your watch face:

You can customize these in the Google Health App, allowing you to save multiple watch faces to the Fitbit Edge for quick swapping between them. You can customize each of the complications (data bits) on the watch face, as well as the coloring. There’s roughly a dozen watch faces to choose from:

From anytime on the display, you can swipe down to access the controls menu. This includes things like the flashlight, do-not-disturb mode, heart rate broadcasting, and more. I’ll cover some of these in more depth throughout the review.

However, the most odd part here is that you can’t tap any of these metrics to see more details on-device. For example, want to see what time it thinks you went to sleep or woke up? Can’t do that here. Have to go to the phone. Want to see why your Readiness Score is what it is? Can’t do that here. Have to go to the phone.

In discussions with Google/Fitbit, they seem to hint that changes are coming here in this area. I sure hope so, as this seems pretty half-finished at best.

In any case, as you keep swiping through these to the left/right, you’ll essentially see other data metrics for stuff I already showed in the Focus Metrics. For example, swipe again, and you’ll see the very small sleep tidbits. You can’t tap into this, though, for any additional details beyond what you see below:

And finally, there’s another page for media controls. There is no music storage on the Fitbit Edge, which seems logical/fine to me. However, what’s less logical is the lack of NFC/payment support. This was in the Fitbit Charge 6, and has been in countless cheper Fitbit devices over the years, yet isn’t here. Given Google doesn’t support the higher-end Pixel Watch series on iOS, this means there’s really no option for iOS users to use a Google device if they want contactless payments. And, as both Google and Apple know well, very few people are going to change phone platforms merely to support a wearable. That ship sailed long, long ago.

In any case, since we can’t see many details on the screen itself beyond core metrics, a lot of that is shifted to your phone and the Google Health app (the Fitbit app is dead, live the Fitbit app!). Here you can see your summary at the top for the day, in terms of steps, readiness scores, sleep, and cardio load for the week. This is all free. However, anytime you see lots of descriptive text, that’ll be part of Google’s Premium subscription. In other words, the data is free, but the wordy analysis requires a subscription.

Looking at my sleep metrics, for example from last night, those look accurate and have been consistently accurate over the last few weeks. The only one that caused many wearables to stumble was yesterday’s daylight saving time change, which seemed to truncate an hour of sleep. However, otherwise the times I woke up and went to sleep have been spot-on.

Note that I don’t compare accuracy of sleep stages/phases, as that’s simply not an accurate metric. In a best-case setting for so-called gold-standard devices, those basically top out in the mid-80% accuracy range, which is pretty poor for a gold-standard device to compare against. Thus, I’m not going to mislead you here.

Instead, I will get annoyed at one sleep-related thing that Google/Fitbit lack on the Fitbit Edge, which is automatic sleep mode toggles. Specifically, while you can setup sleep schedules and do-not-disturb schedules, that actually won’t prevent the screen from illuminating all crazy-pants in the middle of the night (or, perhaps lack of pants in the middle of the night). Instead, anytime the sheets or you brush the screen, it’ll light up. Same goes for raise to wake. Which is both annoying and a long-solved problem on wearables.

Instead, you need to tap the bed icon each night to force it into sleep mode. That by itself isn’t actually as annoying to me as remembering again in the morning to un-tap that bed icon, so that things like raise to wake actually work. Again, like the other details piece, I get the impression there’s a fast-follow to enable this on the Fitbit Edge, but it does feel like the Google/Fitbit team is just a bit behind on getting this device fully baked.

There does appear to be a bug here in that my Blood Oxygen levels have been updating nightly, both on the Fitbit Edge since putting it on, as well as for months on other devices, but since wearing the Fitbit Edge, it’s stopped trending for me. This is a minor nit for me, though it does make my metrics ‘4 out of 5’, instead of ‘5 out of 5’.

When it comes to text notifications, note that you’ll see those come in on the device, though on iOS you’ll be unable to respond to them. But you can read them and clear them:

Finally, in terms of charging, you’ve got the same charger as the Fitbit Air, so kudos to the Google/Fitbit team for managing to keep the same charger for more than one product. I can only assume they had a grand celebration for finally unlocking that accomplishment, complete with Haribo gummy bears and rosé wine. Well deserved.

Indeed, you can fast charge silly-quick to get you through the day/night. And in terms of battery life, if you leave it for the default gesture-based display, it seems to be on trend for the week-long battery life. However, I quickly changed it to always-on display, and that pulls things back real-quick to basically two days of battery life. Again, all dependent on how much GPS-outside workout time you have. With that, let’s talk sweaty stuff.

The Fitbit Edge takes virtually all of the best features from the recently launched screenless Fitbit Air (which is basically a Whoop competitor), and rolls them into the Fitbit Edge. This means that you’ve got equal parts manual activity/workout controls, as well as the ability to just totally ignore the unit on your wrist, and know that it’ll track things behind the scenes automatically. Both my wife and I have done both. Sometimes manually starting a workout, and other times just letting it figure it out.

I definitely appreciate having all of these profiles available on-device, rather than just a very basic set. Which I suppose is the general trend in watches/wearables the last year or two: More workout profile options is better.
