📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Nikkei Asia Full Report

TAIPEI -- China's Huawei Technologies and a local partner say they have overcome a U.S. crackdown on its use of advanced chip design tools, a market long dominated by American leaders Synopsys and Cadence, paving the way for the Chinese tech titan to design next-generation chips for phones and servers domestically.

Phlexing Technology, a little-known electronic design automation (EDA) software provider, said it has been partnering with Huawei to develop toolkits to design chips based on Huawei's chip design principle known as the Tau Scaling Law. Qing He, chairman of Phlexing, said strategic collaboration began in 2019 with work on a new suite of EDA tools, later dubbed Tau EDA.

"Huawei brings telecom-grade stringent standards to enable Phlexing's EDA development. It lifts the whole EDA sector," He said on Tuesday at Huawei's Innovation and Intellectual Property Forum in Beijing, adding that Huawei was linking Phlexing EDA with other homegrown EDA tool suppliers for real project tool training. "Fabless [chipmakers], fabs [chip plants] and packaging houses work together via this mechanism. Phlexing EDA unlocks huge productivity across the whole chain," He said.

Yu Liu, Huawei's Kirin chip architect, said the Chinese company had made major efforts to overhaul the chip design process and overcome many hurdles to drive a comeback for the company's own chips.

"We had to tackle eight major technological challenges. We ran a large program covering architecture, design, process workflows, and EDA tools," Liu said.

The disclosure came as Huawei announced record revenue from its patent licensing business. It said it generated $800 million last year, up from $630 million in 2024, and had made $6.9 billion in accumulated patent licensing revenue between 2011 and September 2026.

China has aimed to cut its reliance on foreign companies for EDA ever since Huawei lost access to U.S. technologies when Washington added it to its so-called Entity List in May 2019.

In 2025, Huawei's research and development spending was 192 billion yuan ($29 billion), or 21.8% of its annual revenue. More than half of its employees, or 114,000 people, work in R&D. It has established 86 foundational technology labs, and more than 180 joint innovation labs.

Wireless communications account for 41% of Huawei's active granted patents, terminal hardware for 16%, and artificial intelligence and compute for 13%. Huawei now holds more than 8,000 AI-related patents.

Alan Fan, Huawei's chief intellectual property officer, told reporters that the company has elevated its IP division to a top-level department reporting directly to the board, underscoring its commitment to developing proprietary technologies and patents. The move also reflected the leadership's push to strengthen Huawei's legal capabilities and give intellectual property a more prominent role in its strategy.

Asia has become the predominant player in intellectual property worldwide, according to Francis Gurry, former director-general of the World Intellectual Property Organization.

"Seventy per cent of all intellectual property applications are filed in Asia ... and that reflects Asia's preeminence as a technological power, and as a market with 58% of the world's population," Gurry told a forum in Beijing.

Huawei's licensing partners include Qualcomm and HP in the U.S., Mercedes-Benz and Volkswagen in Europe, and Seiko Solutions and Mitsubishi Fuso in Japan, according to the Chinese company.

Huawei's announcement comes as the AI industry experiences severe volatility over Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's abrupt departure and dramatic return as CEO following an alleged relationship with a suspected Chinese spy. U.S. President Donald Trump touted his efforts to secure Huang's return, saying China could have poached him to work for Nvidia's rival Huawei.

Huawei has accelerated its chip-building ambitions, debuting a supercomputer chip cluster for the overseas market earlier this year and introducing a UnifiedBus and near-packaged optics (NPO) standard to compete with Nvidia's interconnect networking technology. In May, it said it had overcome U.S. restrictions by finding a new way to design chips with its Tau Scaling Law.

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

This collaboration marks Huawei's attempt to shift chip design standards from US-dominated paradigms to a proprietary mathematical framework, representing a fundamental 'hardware-software co-design' reconfiguration. It aims to build an isolated compute supply chain by linking fabs and packaging houses, effectively bypassing international EDA monopolies.
