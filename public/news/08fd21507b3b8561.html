F.A.A. Shelves Routers Like the One Linked to the Nationwide Ground Stop

✍️ By Neil Vigdor

The U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has suspended the installation and operation of Juniper Networks routers, following findings that a recently installed unit in El Dorado, Arkansas, contributed to last week's nationwide air traffic control ground stop. Despite Hewlett Packard Enterprise (HPE) asserting no defects, the incident, coupled with recent outages at the National Security Agency's classified communications network, highlights mounting concerns over the resilience of critical infrastructure supply chains and maintenance protocols.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New York Times Full Report

The Federal Aviation Administration has suspended its installation of routers like the one that transportation officials said they believed had contributed to a nationwide ground stop on Friday after it overloaded communications systems used by air traffic controllers and pilots.

The broadcasting function of more than 60 other identical devices on the network was disabled after the disruptions wreaked havoc on air travel, according to the F.A.A., which said on Tuesday that the changes had taken effect in quick succession in response to the chain of events on Friday.

Made by Juniper Networks, which was acquired last year by Hewlett Packard Enterprise, the routers were intended to replace older Cisco Systems models as part of a sweeping program by the F.A.A. to modernize its equipment.

For decades, copper wiring has been the backbone of that antiquated system, which the federal government has slowly started to replace with modern fiber-optic cables.

But while an engineer was installing one of the replacement routers on Friday at an F.A.A. radio center in El Dorado, Ark., data began flooding the network, overwhelming the system and hampering communications, federal officials said. That engineer was working for L3Harris, the company that owns the network used by the F.A.A., and was being monitored by an F.A.A. technician, officials said.

A second router at an F.A.A. radar facility in Amarillo, Texas, also became unstable, according to the F.A.A., which said technicians had resolved the problem by disabling both routers.

“So we thought it’s only prudent to pause the use of these routers,” Sean Duffy, the transportation secretary, said at a news conference on Tuesday on Capitol Hill.

Mr. Duffy suggested the router glitch was isolated, saying that federal officials had grounded flights out of an abundance of caution.

“Sixty-eight times it worked well,” he said earlier in the news conference. “This one router started to broadcast into our network, and it started to overload the network.”

Federal transportation officials said on Tuesday that the replacement routers had been tested for a year before being installed.

“We don’t know the fault trigger,” Bryan Bedford, the F.A.A. administrator, said at the news conference. “We do know that this router could be enabled to do what it did.”

In a statement on Tuesday, Adam Bauer, a spokesman for Hewlett Packard Enterprise, disputed that the Juniper Networks device was defective.

“Based on what we know and have been told by others, our equipment was working as configured,” Mr. Bauer said. “The F.A.A. is assessing the configuration and performance of all infrastructure involved to determine the cause.”

On Monday, Democratic members of the Senate Commerce, Science and Transportation Committee sent a letter to Mr. Duffy and Mr. Bedford urging them to pause the use of the Juniper Networks routers.

“Identifying the device that failed does not establish why it failed,” the senators wrote. “F.A.A. must investigate further to determine the root cause and report to Congress on how it intends to prevent future issues.”

On Tuesday, federal officials were less measured in their assessment of the performance of L3Harris, which Mr. Duffy said had held contracts with the F.A.A. for more than 20 years and was responsible for monitoring the network.

“They didn’t identify and isolate,” he said. “That concerns me. We have to have them perform better.”

A representative for L3Harris on Tuesday referred to a previous statement, which said the company was “supporting the F.A.A. in its investigation.”

While Mr. Duffy said there was no evidence that the system had been hacked, he noted that F.B.I. analysts were examining the router from the F.A.A. radio facility in Arkansas.

Mr. Bedford said that air traffic controllers had never lost contact with planes while the network was experiencing disruptions. He added that the F.A.A. had backup systems in place but that safety was paramount.

“And people can fly with confidence,” he said.
