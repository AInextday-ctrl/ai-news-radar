Musk Tries to Lock In His Tesla-SpaceX Dream Before Trump’s Exit

✍️ By Bloomberg News

Advisers within Elon Musk's empire are pushing for a merger between SpaceX and Tesla, targeting a completion by H1 2027. The move is strategically timed to leverage the current political landscape under President Trump to finalize the $3.7 trillion deal.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Bloomberg Full Report

No one could accuse Elon Musk of thinking small. And the vision he described last month of merging his two companies SpaceX and Tesla is characteristically ambitious: a digital conglomerate of unprecedented scale, spanning rockets, satellites, electric cars, robots and social media.

Mr. Musk is not alone as a digital-age corporate empire builder. Other tech giants are also now sprawling, multi-industry enterprises.

Alphabet’s businesses range well beyond Google search and cloud computing to include video streaming, self-driving cars, drone delivery service and drug development. Amazon has e-commerce, cloud computing, video streaming, a movie studio, robots, warehouses and delivery services.

But a SpaceX-Tesla merger would eclipse the others in its striking array of different businesses, forming a mega-conglomerate worth about $3.5 trillion.

Conglomerates of the past were typically driven by purely financial considerations, like the 1960s merger frenzy on Wall Street, or to diversify a company in order to make it better able to weather economic cycles and international competition, like General Electric.

But today’s conglomerate impulse has a different source — a drive for technological supremacy by broadening a corporation’s market reach and gaining an upper hand in the global A.I. race.

“These big companies are trying to deploy digital technologies, and increasingly artificial intelligence, across industries,” said David Yoffie, a professor at the Harvard Business School.

Mr. Musk acknowledged that the logic of a SpaceX-Tesla merger was a corporate move to seize the A.I. moment. The A.I. businesses at SpaceX include its Grok chatbot and A.I. software models, its data center-building unit and its Cursor A.I. coding assistant. Tesla would bring self-driving cars, robotaxis, humanoid robots and A.I. chip design.

During a SpaceX investor call last month, when Mr. Musk disclosed the two companies were exploring a merger, he said there was “a lot of merit to combining the companies as we are pretty much in the superintelligence singularity,” the stage at which A.I. surpasses human intelligence.

Mr. Musk, the world’s richest person, is chief executive and the largest shareholder of both companies. So special committees of independent directors at both companies will weigh whether to go ahead.

Corporate conglomerates in America have a rich, colorful and mostly disappointing history. The 1960s saw an explosion of deal making to build the fast-rising conglomerates of their day like ITT Corporation, Litton Industries, Gulf and Western Industries and Ling-Temco-Vought.

These companies were agglomerations of unrelated businesses. ITT (International Telephone and Telegraph) began as an communications company operating outside the United States, but it came to own Avis rental cars, Levitt home builders, Sheraton hotels, Scott’s fertilizer, Wonder Bread and Morton’s frozen foods, among others.

Wall Street was hot. So the currency of acquisition — the buying company’s stock price — was inflated. Debt was cheap and the accounting was creative.

Sleight-of-hand financial deals were the order of the day. One of its foremost practitioners was James Ling — “Jimmy Ling, the merger king.” He started an electrical contracting business in Dallas. Over the years, in deal after deal, he acquired companies in steel manufacturing, meatpacking, car rentals, military aircraft, car rentals, an airline and resorts. At its peak in 1969, LTV, as the company was known, ranked 14th in the Fortune 500.

But the 1960s bull market on Wall Street ended abruptly as inflation rose. LTV’s stock price plummeted, Mr. Ling was ousted and LTV went through a string of bankruptcies.

Still, even after the 1960s conglomerate bubble, there were multi-industry companies that thrived. The leading example grew out of the power generators to lightbulbs conglomerate formed in 1892, when Thomas Edison’s Edison General Electric Company merged with another company.

Throughout the 1980s and 1990s, General Electric enjoyed two decades of prosperity under the leadership of Jack Welch. G.E.’s revenue jumped nearly fivefold, to $130 billion, during Mr. Welch’s tenure. The value of its shares on the stock market soared from $14 billion to more than $410 billion, making G.E. one of the most valuable companies in the world.

In 2000, Fortune magazine named Mr. Welch the “Manager of the Century.”

G.E. seemed proof that a well-run industrial conglomerate could enjoy the advantages of size, funding, shared research and expertise. Like the digital giants of today, G.E. executives spoke of critical industrial technologies — physics and materials science — that could be applied to different industries. A gas turbine power generator, they explained, was essentially a big jet engine bolted to the ground.

But under Mr. Welch, G.E. diversified beyond industrial businesses into finance and commercial real estate. G.E.’s leaders viewed the company’s greatest asset as its management excellence, which they figured could be applied to any business. The money to be made on Wall Street looked easy compared to manufacturing.

For years, the buildup of that business paid off. The company’s finance arm, GE Capital, accounted for up to half of corporate profits in some years and became the largest nonbank financial institution in America. But the 2008 financial crisis hit the division hard.

In 2021, G.E. announced it was splitting into three companies: GE Aerospace, for jet engines; GE Vernova, for power generators and wind power; and GE Healthcare, for medical imaging equipment. The breakup was completed in 2024, as the once-iconic American corporate behemoth bowed to changing times and fortunes.

Studies of conglomerates over the years have found that the stock market value of the combined corporation is typically worth 10 to 15 percent less than if the businesses were stand-alone companies, known as the conglomerate discount. The benefits of combining businesses — the much-praised “synergies” — are overwhelmed by the complexity of managing varied operations and the difficulty of getting a clear picture of the performance of individual businesses.

Skeptics of a SpaceX-Tesla deal point to those challenges. “Shareholders can already own both companies, and this transaction would have all the drawbacks of a conglomerate,” said Baruch Lev, a professor emeritus of accounting and finance at New York University’s Stern School of Business.

Mr. Musk is an experienced corporate acquirer and he has proved skeptics wrong in the past, though a SpaceX-Tesla tie-up would be his largest deal by far. But the merger is not just about corporate size, it is a bet on the “A.I. synergies” that Mr. Musk hopes will result. Much will depend on how much shared A.I. technology and knowledge can be spread across the varied businesses, yielding cost savings and competitive gains, better products and services.

Some analysts say an A.I. conglomerate may well enjoy advantages as the technology is increasingly widely adopted. A combined SpaceX and Tesla, they say, will have greater power to develop, buy and build the technology and deploy it across industry after industry.

They cite the increasing collaboration already between SpaceX and Tesla, led by the giant SpaceX computer chip plant in Texas, announced earlier this year, which will cost at least $55 billion and in total spending could reach $119 billion. Both companies will use the chips manufactured in Texas, with Tesla-designed semiconductors made there as well.

“In this A.I. economy, where computing capacity rules the world, putting SpaceX and Tesla together makes a lot of sense,” said Shay Boloor, chief market strategist at Futurum Equities, an investment research firm.
