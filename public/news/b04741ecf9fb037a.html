Why using rocks for carbon removal could eventually reduce crop growth

✍️ By James Temple

Research by CarbonPlan suggests that 'Enhanced Rock Weathering' (ERW), a climate solution heavily backed by Google and Microsoft, faces significant scalability challenges. The study indicates that spreading basalt or olivine at levels required for climate impact may degrade soil health and decrease crop yields; scaling back applications to protect harvests would likely cap the method's CO2 removal potential to under 1 billion tons per year.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

MIT Tech Review Full Report

Tech companies are investing a lot of hope and money in rocks.

Google, Microsoft, and others have committed hundreds of millions of dollars to get startups to spread crushed basalt, olivine, or other alkaline materials across farms, in an effort to capture carbon from the air and lock it away. A number of influential models have found that this approach, known as enhanced rock weathering (ERW), could eventually remove 3 billion to 8 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide per year, helping to slow or one day even reverse global warming. An additional promise is that these applications of rock could help farmers by improving the quality of their soil.

But a pair of preprint studies set for release today raise serious questions about how much material could be added to that soil before it may actually reduce the crop yields that are essential to farmers’ livelihoods, MIT Technology Review can exclusively report.

Cutting back on the rock applications to prevent these negative effects would shrink the method’s carbon dioxide removal capacity, likely capping it at less than a billion tons per year. The analyses were led by researchers at CarbonPlan, a San Francisco nonprofit known for rigorous scientific analysis of carbon removal methods.

These concerns come on top of a series of scientific questions about how to accurately estimate the carbon removal achieved through this approach and the possibility that some projects have overstated it , which researchers at CarbonPlan and elsewhere have raised in recent months and years.

“There’s still a heck of a lot we need to learn about ERW,” says Tyler Kukla, a research scientist at CarbonPlan and lead author of the new reports. “But a real tension, to me, exists between this massive amount of uncertainty and the big questions we have about whether or not this works, sitting alongside tremendous efforts to scale it up really quickly.”

There is no scientific dispute that certain rocks and minerals help to sequester carbon. Rain captures carbon dioxide molecules as it falls through the atmosphere, forming carbonic acid. When it lands on exposed silicate materials, that weak acid dissolves or chemically “weathers” the rocks.

The resulting calcium, magnesium, and carbon (in the form of bicarbonate) then travel on a long journey through creeks, streams, and rivers until they reach the ocean, where corals, mollusks, and other marine organisms may eventually use them to form calcium carbonate shells. As they die, those shells fall to the seafloor, combine with sediments, and form limestone rock that locks away the carbon.

If you thought that explanation was long, just imagine how long the process takes to unfold in real life! It’s not called the slow carbon cycle for nothing. “Enhanced” rock weathering promises to accelerate the process. Grinding up the rocks and spreading them across fields creates much more surface area for carbonic acid to react with—and that should ultimately direct more carbon into the oceans.

ERW doesn’t require as much energy or infrastructure as other carbon dioxide removal options like direct-air-capture plants, and at least in the early rollout stages it can largely rely on waste material from rock mining operations.

But the big selling point is that there can be an economic benefit to doing it; it’s not just a cost like other forms of carbon removal. Added to agricultural soils, these sorts of rocks can increase nutrients, reduce acidity, and improve water retention, helping to boost crop yields (at least up to a point). Indeed, many farmers already routinely add crushed-up limestone to their soil to counteract the increased acidity caused by fertilizer use and other agricultural practices.

So carrying out enhanced rock weathering could merely be a matter of switching to other types of rock with the potential to lock away more carbon (about twice as much as limestone).

“It’s something the Earth has been doing for billions of years,” says Mary Yap, chief executive and cofounder of Lithos Carbon, an ERW startup. “It can scale. We know it works kinetically.” “If we can figure out where in the world are the best places to do this and then make that happen,” she adds, “it can help both the communities and also help our atmosphere.”

Enhanced rock weathering could be of particular benefit to farmers who haven’t been able to manage their soil acidity, including many subsistence farmers in poorer parts of the world, says Tim Jesper Suhrhoff, a postdoctoral fellow at the Yale Center for Natural Carbon Capture. One recent study found that ERW boosted the median yields of smallholder farmers in India by more than 20%.

“That is huge for subsistence farmers,” he says.

But the new findings, which have not yet been peer-reviewed, suggest there could be a steep trade-off, at a certain point, between benefits for farmers and benefits for the climate.

A number of modeling studies that inform UN climate reports and national climate efforts have estimated that adding crushed rock to agricultural land could store away four to 12 tons of carbon dioxide per hectare per year (though some estimate lower levels). But the authors state in one of the new papers that these findings don’t account for the ways that significant levels of enhanced rock weathering will alter the pH of agricultural soil over time.

Initially it should merely reduce the soil acidity, but adding too much of these materials would eventually push up the pH beyond neutral and into alkaline territory. That could cause micronutrient deficiencies, disease susceptibility, and other problems for certain crops, potentially reducing yields—and, by extension, food supplies and farm revenue. That would likely force farmers to halt the practice, at least until soil pH returns to favorable levels.

The analysis found that preventing the more extreme effects of soil alkalization could limit ERW’s total potential to around 600 million tons of carbon dioxide removal per year, and even that may push pH levels too high for some crops, Kukla says. That’s a fraction of what some models have estimated.

“All of these models are assuming really, really optimistic things about what enhanced weathering can accomplish,” he says. “And when you just layer in the geochemistry that they’re missing, the numbers not only go down but get really uncertain.”

The researchers intend to submit the papers to peer-reviewed journals.

Companies have been investing more and more money in ERW. In September, Google announced its largest carbon removal deal, as well as the biggest for this method to date: an agreement with the company Terradot to work with rice farmers in southern Brazil to apply enough crushed rock to their fields to draw down a million tons of carbon by 2040. (The deal includes a separate effort to cut methane emissions by draining the flooded paddies.) Google didn’t respond to an inquiry from MIT Technology Review .

Frontier, a coalition of companies that has committed to purchase $1.8 billion of carbon dioxide removal, has dedicated $121 million to prepurchasing 333,000 tons’ worth through enhanced rock weathering. That includes a $57 million deal with Lithos to remove more than 150,000 tons.

Zeke Hausfather, climate research lead at Frontier, says the early data from their deals has demonstrated consistent weathering of rock across hundreds of fields.

He acknowledges there are remaining uncertainties, including how much of the carbon may be released before it’s permanently sequestered in the ocean. But he adds that those and other issues are all the subject of ongoing studies—including some Frontier has backed—by both ERW companies and academic groups.

All told, companies have committed to spending about $670 million on enhanced rock weathering to date, based on an average cost of about $350 per ton, according to CDR.fyi, a public-benefit corporation that provides independent data on the carbon removal sector. A working group of the UN’s climate panel, the European Commission, and other government agencies are also in discussions about the possibility of incorporating ERW into national or regional climate efforts.

The new papers add to a growing recognition that enhanced rock weathering is more constrained and “ more complicated than we initially thought,” says Rebecca Neumann , who leads a hydro-biogeochemistry research group at the University of Washington but was not involved in the studies.

In April, researchers at CarbonPlan, the nonprofit that led the new studies, raised concerns about an allotment of carbon credits that Lithos received for this method. The startup claimed to have removed 8.3 tons of carbon dioxide per hectare per year through ERW, but the CarbonPlan team noted that this figure exceeds around 90% of the rates reported in previously published experiments.

CarbonPlan followed up on that report the following month with an analysis finding that modeling of enhanced weathering has often relied on an equation that could inflate the surface area of the crushed rock—and, in turn, overstate the amount of carbon dioxide removal achieved.

Finally, several studies last year highlighted a known but important issue: Magnesium and calcium ions can get trapped in soil for long periods instead of traveling with the bicarbonate into the oceans. That creates a risk that the bicarbonate will simply dissolve back into carbon dioxide and water again, “resulting in no carbon removed,” explained Robert Höglund, cofounder of CDR.fyi, in an online post. Or it may delay the carbon removal by years to decades.

“I’m not saying it doesn’t work,” Höglund said in an interview. “I’m pointing out there’s a lot of uncertainty about what’s permanent and how we should measure it.”

Lithos’s Yap stresses that the high rate of carbon removal the company reported in that instance simply reflected large applications of more finely crushed rock on farms with favorable soil chemistry, adding that the project applied as much as 10 times more per hectare than some of the studies cited in the CarbonPlan post. She also criticized the nonprofit for not contacting the company before posting its results and for publishing them on a blog instead of in peer-reviewed literature. Kukla says CarbonPlan did account for higher applications of crushed rock in its analysis.

A few additional findings further underscore the complexity and uncertainty surrounding this approach. For example, a more recent preprint suggests that the equation CarbonPlan critiqued does accurately capture the rock surface area in some cases. Meanwhile, other papers have found that the problem of trapped calcium and magnesium ions could be mitigated through location choices and certain land management practices.

But all this back and forth raises an obvious question: how can commercial deployments of this practice proceed while the scientific debate is still unfolding?

Kevin Sutherland, a carbon removal scientist at Isometric, one of the first carbon registries to develop methods for certifying how much carbon a project draws out of the atmosphere, says the company accounts for these scientific uncertainties by quantifying the climate benefits of ERW conservatively.

“In practice, this means projects receive fewer certificates than our best estimate of the carbon they remove,” he explained in an emailed response. “We would always rather issue too few certificates than too many.”

Despite the recent findings, Neumann says that ERW is still a promising approach for helping farmers and the climate, even if the carbon removal potential is significantly lower than initially hoped.

Yale’s Suhrhoff, who also read over the new findings but was not involved in the research, says that providing a clearer sense of ideal application levels can help the approach catch on. “If we figure out where enhanced rock weathering makes sense agronomically and how much enhanced rock weathering to do to reach agronomic optimal conditions, I think this can actually greatly improve the realistic uptake speed of this technology,” he says.

Kukla himself stresses that none of CarbonPlan’s research suggests enhanced rock weathering doesn’t work or can’t make a meaningful contribution to slowing down climate change.

But it’s important to understand the potential scale, he adds, because the world needs to know how much we can count on any given form of carbon removal—and how hard we’ll need to pull on other climate levers, including the basic job of cutting greenhouse-gas emissions.

“This is all just about calibrating our expectations and grounding them in the science as best as we can,” he says.
