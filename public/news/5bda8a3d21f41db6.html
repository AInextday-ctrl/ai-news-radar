Many Etsy Sellers Close Up Shop Over Site’s New A.I. Policy

✍️ By Reggie Ugwu

Etsy has introduced new terms of service, effective December 16, allowing the company and its third-party partners to use seller content for training and testing AI models. This prompted widespread backlash and shop closures from creators, despite claims from James Ossman, VP of customer operations, that data use is restricted to fraud detection and advertising, as users remain concerned about the lack of opt-out mechanisms and potential IP exploitation.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New York Times Full Report

Many longtime owners of shops on Etsy, the online marketplace known for its handmade goods, said this week that they had left the site over a policy change that they said leaves them vulnerable to exploitation by artificial intelligence companies.

Language in Etsy’s new terms, set to take effect on Dec. 16, says the company and its “third party partners and vendors” reserve the right to use sellers’ content to “develop, train, test and improve artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies.”

The terms say that Etsy and its partners will use such technologies in part to improve the platform and keep it safe for users. The previous version of the terms did not include a clause about using content for artificial intelligence.

“Etsy decided that selling on their platform now means letting them train AI on our artwork AND resell that data to third parties, with no way to opt out,” Aaron Millard, an illustrator, wrote in an Instagram post announcing that he was deleting the shop that he has run for nearly a decade. “My work took years to learn and create. I won’t let it be stolen.”

Kristen and Geoff Zephyrus, the founders of the home goods and stationary brand Cognitive Surplus, which has sold its products on Etsy for 13 years, also deleted their shop. In an Instagram post, they accused the company of claiming “perpetual, free, boundless permission to not only train AI on our art, but also to resell the data for profit.”

“For Etsy to be helping to train algorithms with our human content is just unimaginably awful,” Kristen Zephyrus said in an interview. “We don’t want anything to do with that.”

In a statement posted to Etsy’s website on Monday, James Ossman, the company’s vice president for customer operations, addressed the site’s sellers directly.

Etsy will not “use your content to train A.I. models designed to copy your original work or replace your creativity,” Ossman wrote. He cited the development of fraud detection tools and advertisements on the site as among the reasons for granting third parties access to sellers’ content.

A representative for Etsy did not immediately respond to a request for comment on Friday.

Many publicly traded internet companies like Etsy have been caught between pressure from shareholders to embrace artificial intelligence and the ire of users who reject the technology.

Big bets by major firms and their investors on the A.I. revolution have helped the U.S. stock market set records in recent years. But Americans in general are much less enthusiastic. A Gallup poll from the summer found that nearly 40 percent of Americans feel that A.I. does more harm than good. Many of the Democratic political candidates who swept into power during the midterm elections this month campaigned explicitly against A.I. companies and data centers.

Etsy, which was founded in 2005 to help independent artisans find customers online, is no stranger to user revolt. In 2022, more than 20,000 Etsy sellers went on strike to protest a 30 percent increase in its base-line sales fee, to 6.5 percent from 5 percent. In October, the company announced tighter restrictions on so-called A.I. slop after many users decried such content.

This incident highlights the structural conflict between platform data-mining mandates and creator IP rights during the AI transition. Without transparent authorization and value-sharing mechanisms, the forced extraction of creator data to train AI risks long-term ecological depletion and the exodus of the platform's core creative capital.
