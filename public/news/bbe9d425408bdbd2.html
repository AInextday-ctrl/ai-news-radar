Jeremiah tried out a few of these apps. They had names like KashKick, Swagbucks, and Mistplay. After wasting hours on games in KashKick for low payouts with too many restrictions and caveats, he switched to an app called Freecash. Most of the featured games are traps, he said — the rewards decrease as you play and give you unreachable targets — but he eventually put together a routine. At 44, he had spent years playing World of Warcraft and ’90s strategy games before it. “I can grind,” he said, “and that’s what most of these games are.” Now, he was grinding for cash by playing iPhone-era strategy games like Rise of Kingdoms, which involves building cities and battling other players for virtual resources and land. By assembling his army and succeeding in the game, he could eventually reach goals in Freecash, then redeem them for a few real dollars. Beyond his gaming background, Jeremiah had the time — he worked in farming for years in eastern North Carolina but had since gone on disability — so he professionalized his operation. He played multiple games at once: Age of Dragons, Legend of Mushroom, and countless similar ones that would pop up in Freecash’s list of offers with better terms. He set up multiple profiles on a folding Samsung phone where he could check on his progress and track his earning tasks at the same time.

Jeremiah’s fate within the games, which were themselves about the slow and precarious accumulation of virtual resources, soon had real stakes. He started setting timers so he wouldn’t miss chances to upgrade or progress. He also had to be constantly vigilant against raids, in which other players, some grinding for credit in apps like Freecash themselves, could attack and steal in-game resources, indirectly robbing Jeremiah of real earnings in the process. He defends himself by working with a clan of allied players gathered from the strange and ephemeral social environments that briefly materialize at different levels of the get-paid-to, or GPT, app ecosystem. “90 percent of the time, you get up on a server that’s full of Brazilians, but there are also a lot of people from Malaysia and the Philippines,” he said. With the arrival of automatic AI translation, these in-game chats have become somewhat more useful and less alienating. “We’ll discuss the strategy for getting higher on the game,” he added. Some are in it for the money. Others are just addicted.

For mobile-game companies, apps like Freecash are a way to buy downloads but also to filter their customers, paying out small amounts to find out which might end up spending even more. (The industry borrows a term, whales, from casinos for the big spenders who make the business model work.) Earning money by playing these games is hard. Spending it is easy. Using real money to buy in-game resources in Rise of Kingdom, for example, can speed up progress and increase your power in the game, allowing you to skip over hours of play or to dominate other players. If you’re using Freecash, this may get you closer to a real payout. But progress is often elusive and hard to track. It’s easy to overspend in pursuit of slippery goals or to become straightforwardly addicted, disengaging from the get-paid-to ecosystem entirely.

If players do eventually make enough money to withdraw, they’ll often be greeted with a string of solicitations to funnel their funds through an affiliated gambling platform like Stake, where they’ll be enticed by new and arcane promotions, rules, and fine print. Even for a grinder, it’s a grind. Since Jeremiah started keeping track and paying for some of his in-app purchases with other GPT apps like Scrambly, he has cleared $8,000 in total payouts. Still, after weeks or months of attention-draining, repetitive check-ins and resets and blurred-together earnings, wins, and losses, the notion that you’re getting paid to do things you would be doing anyway, or getting “paid to scroll,” sounds absurd. This is a common complaint in get-paid-to advice communities, which suggest that far more people wash out of these apps than stick with them. “They make gaming a grind and not fun anymore,” wrote another user on Reddit. “They are greedy af and have unrealistic timelines and crap payouts.” Jeremiah still plays mainstream games in his spare time. As for the hypermonetized slopware games that send him notifications all day and night: “It’s work.”

For Jeremiah, the get-paid-to regimen is working well enough that he hasn’t called it quits yet, even if it wears him out sometimes. “I’m on my phone all day anyway,” he said, “and it’s not the only thing I’m doing.” A few days before I spoke to him, he won a bet at cash-out and made a few hundred dollars. He was relieved, he said, and planned to take a few days off.

Freecash briefly broke into the top five in September — and meeting the American public at a moment of intense economic worry and disillusionment. Hiring is low, and the entire economy is being propped up by a multitrillion-dollar bet on a general-purpose job-automating machine. Even more people than usual are worried about work, specifically and in general. The global financial crisis was followed by the rise of the gig economy. As Americans clench through the late Trump years — and inflation and high interest rates and the AI boom — it’s reasonable to wonder if a newer, harsher, and more convoluted vision of gig work is what’s waiting for us on the other side.

Mostly, though, it’s because of the advertising, which is all over social media. According to Appfigures, Freecash grew from fewer than a million downloads in October 2025 to 6 million in February 2026, boosted by a flood of ads that promised, for example, up to $35 an hour to watch TikTok, while giving users the impression that they would somehow be working for the social-media company. TikTok pulled some of these ads for being misleading, according to Wired; eventually, both Apple and Google temporarily booted the app from their stores. Now, the apps are back, and so are the ads.

Scott Sweeney, an IT and marketing professional living in Ohio, had known about this strange industry for years but had no reason to partake in it. Then, after 11 years at his tech job, he was laid off. “In my mind, I was trying to close a small gap,” he said. He had severance but also a kid. So he gave the apps a try. “The earliest experiences were pretty frustrating,” he said. Apps like Freecash are the “gateway,” he added. “You put in that first initial effort, it shows you only spent 15 minutes, and you made three dollars.” Most apps, he says, are “front loaded” — the opportunities dry up quickly and soon you’re spending “god-awful amounts of time and work to make pennies.”

My own brief experience on Freecash bore this out immediately. The first app it suggested I download was Disney Solitaire, a simple-looking game from one of America’s most wholesome conglomerates.

At first, the game ran without friction, dealing easy tableaux and offering frequent hints, bonus cards, all with a deck seemingly stacked in my favor. In the background, I was accumulating Mickey Mouse coins, a currency I had been made aware of minutes earlier, by the thousands. Things soon slowed down, and the game’s scenes stretched long, bonuses became less frequent, and my draws felt worse. Back in Freecash, my first $12 arrived within an hour. It was clear, though, that getting the rest of the way to the $25 withdrawal threshold would take thousands more taps and hundreds more small check-ins. If I wanted to skip ahead, of course, I could pay: For $5, I might get to the next milestone in Disney Solitaire, which could allow me to cash out in Freecash. (The first in-app purchase offer Disney made to me, the “BEST VALUE” package for 200,000 virtual coins, cost $49.99.) Or I could grind, like the player on Reddit who “barely even went outside” for a month, spent around $60, and then couldn’t redeem their $600 of earnings. “Probably would’ve made more money working at a random gas station in the same time,” they wrote. “At least I would’ve actually gotten paid lol.”

Sweeney didn’t have time for this. He decided, first, to focus on surveys, another available option in many get-paid-to apps. By answering market-research questions or responding to polls, he could make a few dollars during slack moments in a busy day. (A fair number of these tasks, he said, were just “asking about thermostatic opinion in American politics,” drawing a broad vibe check from a group of people who were, at least on their phones, surrounded by bad vibes.) He also got a lot of micro-tasks with pretty clear mechanics behind them. He might be asked to download an app and spend five minutes using it, which would produce a favorable engagement measurement for the developer. There were the popular data-harvesting apps, like Fetch and Receipt Hog, which offer payment for sharing receipts, with higher rates for buying specific products. There were also some newer, seemingly AI-centric get-paid-to app opportunities. “You’d get a task like ‘Take a bunch of selfies and give up your personal biometrics for $5,’” Sweeney said.

He too started treating this activity more like a job, drifting further from spare-time tasks and layered points and rewards into more explicit forms of micro-gig work. Apps like iVueit, which offers business clients “on-demand property inspections” to, for example, “verify current conditions” or “monitor retail execution remotely,” would assign him tasks nearby, where he might be able to step out during a swim class to take photos of, say, the state of the landscaping outside a Dollar General; those like Prolific offer payment for “judging AI,” ultimately feeding into models at companies like Google and OpenAI.

“A lot of people are pivoting to that sort of work,” Sweeney said. It isn’t lost on them that such work feels sort of self-eliminating, he suggested, “and it doesn’t feel great knowing that your attention is the only commodity they’re interested in.” The apps don’t let you forget it, either: To do this sort of gig work is to be confronted by endless ads, confusing promotions and sweepstakes, and a barrage of bar charts, spinning wheels, and confetti explosions, creating a feeling of constant movement and progress on the way to goals that remain just out of reach. (This may sound familiar to anyone who has used gambling apps or just browsed Shein or Temu.)

In the end, Sweeney said, this doesn’t particularly matter. “A lot of people who are doing this are just trying to find means to an end,” he said, bridging a gap as he was, stay-at-home parenting, or looking for a side hustle that isn’t too disruptive. Sure, the companies offering these tasks were coy about their various “endgames” and seemed to view users with disrespect bordering on contempt. But they were paying.

Sweeney has since rejoined the traditional workforce but still does some task work in his spare time, setting aside earnings to pay down debt or save for family vacations. He turned his research into a series of Reddit advice posts, which got a lot of attention, before identifying a better opportunity in the space: He turned those posts into an online guide to get-paid-to apps, some of which pay him affiliate commissions for new users.

AI leaders are fond of wondering aloud what work will be left for humans once their products can do much, or most, of what we consider work today. Get-paid-to apps provide an unsentimental vision of what’s left: raw attention, harvested by ads and converted into app metrics; out-of-distribution, low-context tasks meant to close gaps in machine capability; the possessions and expression of consumer preferences, which can be used to improve marketing.

But the truth behind all those TikTok ads isn’t really a novel thesis about the value of time and labor but rather something more familiar and zero-sum. After a period of trial and error, Jeremiah was cash-flow positive, and GPT apps had become a major part of his routine. His mother noticed and thought she would give them a try too. She logged onto Freecash and was also funneled into Disney Solitaire, to which she quickly became addicted. “It was a huge trap,” he said. “Before I knew it, she was dropping $200 to $300 to get $100.” Both sides of the get-paid-to model were now playing out in the same house, so he intervened and got access to her Freecash account. They were playing a lot of the same games, he noticed, but as a new player, she often got better offers and rates. She needed to step back, but Jeremiah saw an opportunity: “I use her account on an iPad now.”
