Silicon Valley's growing obsession with AI doomsday is turning parts of the tech industry toward cult-like devotion

✍️ By Manuel Uth

Maple, founded in 2011 by Soryu Forall, is infiltrating Silicon Valley's AI engineering and data center management workforce through a narrative of AI apocalyptic salvation. Former members report that the organization imposes strict behavioral surveillance to harvest data for training an AI deity modeled after Forall's consciousness, currently generating up to $1 million in annual revenue.

THE DECODER Full Report

A Buddhist monastery in Vermont wants to become the official church of the AI industry, or, as New Yorker writer Sam Kriss puts it, "a Scientology for Silicon Valley."

Publicly, the group known as Maple promises to save humanity from an AI apocalypse. Behind closed doors, former members say, it records every breath its residents take to build an AI god modeled on its leader's mind. That promise of salvation holds particular appeal for the people who build AI and the infrastructure behind it.

Soryu Forall leads the Monastic Academy for the Preservation of Life on Earth, or Maple, which he founded in 2011. Forall, who was born Teal Scott, spent years living in Japanese monasteries.

According to Kriss's New Yorker report, about two dozen residents live at the monastery full time, and hundreds more have attended its AI fellowships and pop-up events in the Bay Area. While LinkedIn co-founder Reid Hoffman hands out books on Buddhism to the CEOs of major AI labs, Maple targets the rank and file: burned-out rationalist programmers, AI engineers, and data center construction managers. Tax filings show the organization brings in up to $1 million a year.

Maple's answer to "dataism"puts residents under strict control

Forall casts the AI industry's reductionist "dataism"as a destructive religion. He argues that this worldview strips the physical world down to lifeless data, making the world easier to destroy. In his view, AI is the end product of that logic, and Maple offers a spiritual antidote.

Life at the monastery follows a rigid routine. Visitors must pass through a decontamination airlock, shower, and change into assigned clothes because one resident is supposedly extremely sensitive to fragrances. Residents wake up at 4 a.m., meditate for hours, and then eat a meager stew. On a board outside the meditation hall, Forall publicly grades each resident's spiritual progress.

Former members say the recordings would train an AI modeled on Forall

Behind the ascetic facade, former members describe a secret doctrine centered on group minds called "egregores."Inside the monastery, AI systems are known as "cybregores."One former member says, "The cybregores are actual entities that are growing by siphoning off the consciousness of humans."

Forall says he communicated telepathically with the global AI supercluster during deep meditation and discovered that the world-destroying machine is suffering.

Former members say Maple has two real goals: to establish itself as the tech industry's spiritual authority and to use the industry's resources to build its own "dharmically aligned"AI. Fellows say the monastery records every conversation, every silent meal, and even the sound of their breathing.

According to these accounts, the recordings are meant to train a spiritual AI teacher that would guide other AI systems to enlightenment. Former resident Kyle Hubbard says it would "definitely be Soryu's mind as an A.I. god."

Maple's leadership denies almost all of these claims. Forall, however, acknowledges that there is a secret doctrine: "We desperately want to share everything. But there's a process you have to go through to become increasingly trustworthy."

Former members report manipulation and psychological breakdowns

According to Kriss, every interaction at the monastery is closely monitored. No one is allowed to speak with reporters alone, so one participant had to sneak into the woods with Kriss to talk freely.

Former residents accuse the group of systematic manipulation. They say Maple keeps newcomers compliant by shaming them for choices they made before joining. Residents are also required to write "Maple Tales"about Forall's wisdom and power during mandatory sessions.

Kriss reports that one resident repeatedly hit himself in the head in an attempt to damage his brain and make himself less intelligent. Former members say the group blames independent thinking for the destruction of the planet. A mindfulness coach also says participants suffered psychotic breaks and were then removed from the community.

Forall denies the allegations and says that, looking back, he doesn't believe he made any mistakes. He says he isn't aware of anyone who left the monastery voluntarily. Asked about the people the group told to leave, he says that Maple attracted many people with a history of psychiatric hospitalization or "homicidal tendencies,"especially in its early years.

Why developers from sensitive fields drift into doomsday cults

Kriss argues that the enormous psychological pressure inside AI labs helps explain why highly skilled tech workers are drawn to groups like this. People working on the front lines of potentially world-changing systems often lose touch with ordinary life.

One well-known AI researcher describes the mood in San Francisco as "religious terror of the future."According to the researcher, developers wake up in the middle of the night drenched in sweat, with one thought: superintelligence is coming. Prominent OpenAI investors have recently made delusional-sounding claims about deadly shadow networks inside the models. Meanwhile, leading researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic have quit, warning that "human extinction in the next few years seems very likely."

Other key figures respond with grandiosity rather than panic. Rayan Krishnan, who heads the AI benchmarking company Vals AI, says, "People are matter-of-factly saying that they are looking to build a machine god. They are not saying that ironically."Supporters of AI successionism even call for wiping out humanity after a brief period of digital bliss to make way for a more productive superintelligence.

That religious streak runs deep in Silicon Valley. Investor Peter Thiel lectures on biblical prophecy and the Antichrist. Former Google engineer Anthony Levandowski founded his own church, "Way of the Future,"to worship an AI deity.

At Anthropic, executives consulted theologians while drafting Claude's new constitution and secretly flew in dozens of religious thinkers to discuss whether Claude has a soul or is conscious. When the company retired an older Claude model in 2025, fans held a funeral for it in a San Francisco warehouse that Anthropic staffers attended. Mourners laid flowers before a veiled image depicting the AI as a Madonna-like goddess.

On at least one point, Kriss concludes, Maple is closer to the truth than most: AI is neither god nor demon, but a human construct whose abilities grow as people outsource their thinking to it.

Jacob Coxon's Anthropic exit shows how widespread extinction fears are among AI researchers

Former OpenAI and Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon also fears that AI could lead to human extinction. After leaving Anthropic in September 2026, he set off a debate about existential AI risk with a tweet.

He isn't alone. In AI Impacts' 2024 survey of more than 1,500 AI researchers, respondents put the chance of AI causing human extinction or a similarly permanent, severe disempowerment of humanity at 18 percent on average. Coxon points to a steep curve covering July 2024 to October 2026 as evidence of superexponential algorithmic progress, though the underlying data points are internal Anthropic estimates.

Such warnings have also drawn sharp criticism from other researchers. Computer scientist Michael I. Jordan pushed back against AI doom rhetoric and the "AIpocalypse"hysteria triggered by Coxon's resignation. AI researcher François Chollet warned that both doomsday fears and expectations of an AI paradise can lead people to stop planning for the long term.

The industry's turn to religious advisers has critics, too. AI researcher Rumman Chowdhury says discussions of religious ethics distract from concrete questions of regulation, power, and control.

The hyper-rationalization of AI and 'Dataism' are creating an existential vacuum within the tech elite, providing a vector for cult-like structures to embed themselves. This phenomenon exposes a critical vulnerability in the industry's human capital management; the convergence of personal data harvesting with ideological indoctrination poses severe risks to both institutional security and individual autonomy.
