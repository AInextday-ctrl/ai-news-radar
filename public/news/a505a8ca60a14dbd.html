Tech suppliers raise AI server prices up to 45% months after deals are signed

✍️ By Nikou Asgari

Global data center equipment suppliers are significantly increasing prices for previously contracted AI servers, with hikes reaching up to 45%. This surge is driven by extreme supply-demand imbalances in AI infrastructure, forcing manufacturers to bypass existing contract terms to pass on rising memory and component costs.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Data center equipment suppliers; Buyers of Nvidia-powered AI servers: Unilaterally increasing AI server prices by up to 45% post-contract
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Explosive demand for AI compute infrastructure and upstream memory shortages squeezing supplier margins
  • [Key Metrics] 45% price hike post-contract, shift in supply chain bargaining power.
  • [Industry Impact & Outlook] Market impact: Transition from budget-driven to supply-security-driven compute procurement.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Financial Times Full Report

Tech companies that sell equipment for data centres are significantly raising prices for Nvidia-powered AI servers, months after contracts with buyers have been signed, as soaring AI infrastructure prices are passed down to customers.

Suppliers including Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro sell servers containing components such as graphics processing units — the primary AI processors made by the likes of Nvidia and AMD — as well as memory chips and networking gear to customers who are building their own data centres. Such deals are typically agreed well in advance of delivery.

Months later, when the equipment is ready to be delivered, these tech companies are demanding substantially higher prices than the figures originally agreed, according to people familiar with the matter.

Even large financial services and trading firms, which are among the biggest buyers of computing equipment after the Big Tech hyperscalers, are at the mercy of their suppliers’ pricing power, at a time when demand for AI equipment is far outstripping supply.

The price rises are hitting some of the world’s biggest financial trading firms, including Jane Street, Optiver and Hudson River Trading, as they build vast data centres to power the research that underpins their trading, the people said.

“It is disconcerting when you get a 25, 30, 45 per cent [price increase],” said one person familiar with the matter. “There is a significant disparity” between the price initially agreed months in advance and the amount that companies end up paying once the components are delivered, they added.

The price jumps highlight the ferocious demand for computing power, as tech giants, trading firms and other large companies race to create their own data centres. The price rises are largely due to tech companies passing on soaring memory costs, which have risen more than 400 per cent since the start of 2024, according to JPMorgan research.

“The standard now is we’ll sign a contract with an indicative price and the price could be arbitrarily high by the time they deliver it,” said another person familiar with the matter.

“We’ve never said no,” they added. “At the end of the day, the compute is valuable to us and we’ll pay for it.”

Buyers approached by the FT did not confirm whether the contracts included terms stating that prices might increase by the time of order delivery.

Financial trading firms are huge buyers of AI equipment and are largely agreeing to the higher costs because they need the specialised equipment to build their data centres.

Trading companies are building vast data centres around the world to fuel the research that underpins their secretive and highly profitable trading strategies. Hudson River Trading runs a big data centre in a converted underground mine in Norway, while British firm XTX Markets is building its own data centres in Finland.

HPE and Supermicro declined to comment. Dell and Lenovo did not respond to multiple requests for comment.

Hudson River Trading and Optiver declined to comment. Jane Street did not respond to requests for comment.

The price of GPU servers has risen at least 20 to 30 per cent, according to one of the people. CPU nodes, where memory is a large fraction of the cost, have tripled or more in price, they added.

One dispute over price rises was thrust into the open earlier this year, when it reached court.

In June, XTX sued Dell in Irish court. The companies had agreed a fixed price of $549mn for servers, plus XTX paid an extra $5mn to “ensure price certainty”. But Dell later told the British trading firm that component prices for the servers had increased and said XTX would have to pay “at least an additional $62-70mn to obtain the servers”, according to court filings.

“XTX was shocked that Dell sought to increase the agreed price,” the company said in court filings, which also claimed that Dell told XTX executives that “other companies faced with price increases had gone and looked at other suppliers but had come back to Dell and agreed to price adjustment increases”.

Dell told XTX that it “was not able to absorb those increased costs” from rising memory prices, XTX said in the court filing.

XTX declined to comment. Dell did not respond to requests for comment on the lawsuit. The case is ongoing.

This trend reveals a robust seller's market in the AI hardware sector, where traditional contract enforcement is fracturing under extreme supply-demand pressures. For compute buyers, supply chain stability risks have now superseded procurement costs as the primary constraint on business scaling.
