German researchers call for computer science to reinvent itself as "Computer and AI Science"

✍️ By Alan Truly

Researchers at the Department of Computer Science at the Technical University of Darmstadt published a position paper advocating for the transition of the field into 'Computer and AI Science.' The proposal highlights that generative computing shifts software development from manual coding to the oversight and validation of AI-generated systems, necessitating a fundamental rethink of software verification and runtime certification.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

THE DECODER Full Report

AI can significantly accelerate software development and automate parts of the process. That makes traditional computer science more important, not less, 40 researchers at the Technical University of Darmstadt argue. They want the field to evolve into "Computer and AI Science,"because AI is becoming a core technology for building and using tomorrow's computer systems.

The researchers are affiliated with the university's Department of Computer Science and lay out their case in a joint position paper. They identify three major shifts: lower barriers to building software, a dramatic acceleration in research, and the emergence of a new computing paradigm.

Authors want computer science degrees to shift from manual coding to overseeing AI systems

The paper argues that the sheer effort of manual programming has long held computer science back. Generative AI now moves that work to a higher level of abstraction. Developers can increasingly describe the features they want in natural language and have a nontrivial first version in days instead of months. AI can also help with time-consuming tasks like integration and testing.

For the first time, the authors write, building software is becoming economically viable for those who couldn't afford it before, from small towns to midsize manufacturers and specialized research projects.

In the authors' view, computer science programs need to shift away from just writing code toward verifying and directing AI-generated systems. Because that takes a deep grasp of the fundamentals, they propose, among other things, courses where students must complete assignments without any AI help. Humans also remain essential for making value judgments, and taking responsibility for them, when goals like safety and speed conflict.

"Generative Computing"challenges how software is verified and certified

The authors call the shift from rigid, preprogrammed software to "Generative Computing"the most far-reaching change. Future systems and AI coding agents are expected to interpret human intent and dynamically generate their own behavior, along with the code they need, at runtime. That blurs the line between the people who specify workflows and the people who use the systems.

This shift, they say, requires rethinking computer science from the ground up. They ask how safety, privacy, and correctness can still be verified and certified when a system's behavior emerges dynamically at runtime.

The problem is most acute for embodied AI such as robots and autonomous vehicles, whose physical actions have to be computed in real time on limited hardware and can't simply be undone. The paper also calls on computer science to help cut the enormous energy and resource consumption of today's AI through more efficient algorithms and hardware architectures.

Darmstadt researchers say AI speeds up research and computer science must face its social impact

AI is also changing research itself in major ways, according to the paper. Lines of inquiry that might once have taken years, or an entire PhD, could in some cases be explored and validated within weeks or months.

Still, the authors warn against using AI just to move faster. Scientific progress, they say, requires humans to understand why an AI-generated solution works and where its limits are.

The signatories close the paper with sharp criticism of their own discipline, which they say has historically put technological progress first without adequately weighing its social consequences. Given AI's potential effects on the labor market, privacy, and democracy, that needs to change fast, they argue. The paper says the future of AI can't be left to technological and economic forces alone, and calls for computer science to engage now with other academic disciplines, policymakers, and civil society.
