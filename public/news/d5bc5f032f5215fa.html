Anthropic says the key to agent memory is knowing what to forget, not how much to store

✍️ By THE DECODER

Anthropic research indicates that Claude models improve agent performance by optimizing selective memory rather than exhaustive archival, as redundant storage leads to inefficient metadata management. Implementing a 'dreaming' review mechanism, which checks past notes against raw transcripts, effectively mitigates logical loops caused by false memories.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Anthropic: Validation of selective storage importance and implementation of self-correction mechanisms
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Addressing performance bottlenecks caused by memory overload and hallucinations in multi-session tasks
  • [Key Technical Spec] Selective recording outperforms exhaustive backup in agent tasks.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] Long-term memory efficiency correlates directly with complex task completion.

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

The performance bottleneck for AI agents is shifting from raw inference capacity to the management of 'information entropy' in long-range context. Building memory systems capable of self-purging and cross-verification will define the threshold between conversational bots and high-level autonomous agents.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

THE DECODER Full Report

Anthropic studied how Claude retains knowledge across sessions. Its tests show that selective notes outperform exhaustive archives and that having Claude review its own memories after the fact can correct errors.

Anyone who works with an AI agent on a project over several days hits a wall fast, because every new session starts from scratch. In a research post, Anthropic describes how Claude is supposed to retain knowledge across sessions while the model stays unchanged. Claude writes notes to a folder that it can read back later.

The core problem, according to Anthropic, is selection. Before it knows what comes next, the agent has to decide in the moment what might matter later, so every note is a bet on the future.Ad

In an Anthropic test with Pokémon Red, the older Claude Sonnet 3.7 treated everything as potentially important and, across roughly 10,000 game steps, created 413 files in 225 folders, often a separate one for each battle and item it found. Because the game state keeps changing, the archive goes stale fast. At times, the model did nothing but reorganize its notes instead of playing, and it finished with just a single badge.Ad

The newer Claude Fable 5 limited its memory to a few files and earned four badges. Fable 5 didn't record information already visible on screen and instead wrote down plans, strategies, and rules it had tested. Anthropic's comparison, however, covers only a single playthrough per model.

Anthropic recommends explaining the purpose of memory to Claude and letting the model decide how to use it. In one benchmark, according to Anthropic, rigid instructions such as "only record mistakes" wiped out most of the advantage for stronger models. Older models, by contrast, benefited from clear instructions, such as being told to actually read their notes before every response.Ad

The bigger risk comes from faulty notes that the agent blindly trusts later. Dreaming, an idea that builds on research from Letta and UC Berkeley, is designed to address this. Much like the human brain processes experiences during sleep, Claude goes through transcripts of earlier sessions during a break and checks its notes against them.

In the Pokémon test, Claude had incorrectly noted which side of a mountain a landmark was on. Relying on that faulty note, Claude fell into the same trap three times, eventually wanted to give up, and missed its goal. After a Dreaming pass, Claude caught the error by checking the note against the transcript and found the right path.Ad

According to Anthropic, Dreaming can also organize larger knowledge bases. Claude built a linked wiki from about 350,000 internal documents from Stainless, a software company Anthropic acquired. Dreaming is supposed to generate new pages while fixing gaps, duplicates, and broken links. The payoff was greatest when Claude could combine the wiki with search, so the wiki worked as a complement rather than a replacement.Ad

In a separate legal test by Anthropic, a wiki helped most with questions whose answers were spread across multiple documents. Claude scored 87 percent with the wiki, compared to 73 percent with search alone. For simple lookup tasks, the wiki performed roughly on par with search, and in one case it even grew larger than the source documents.

Anthropic acknowledges that key questions remain open. Taking notes mid-task distracts the agent from its work, Dreaming costs extra compute, and its results arrive only after a delay. Wikis and notes also need regular upkeep, or errors and hallucinations pile up. Anthropic also warns against automatically loading too many memories into every session, because they eat up context and pass any bad note along to every future session.

All of these tests come from Anthropic. The overall takeaway is that a useful agent memory depends less on storing as much as possible than on keeping the right information and correcting mistakes later.

Anthropic introduced Dreaming in May as a research preview for Claude Managed Agents, the company's cloud platform for AI agents. The service reads an existing memory store and up to 100 past sessions, removes duplicate and outdated entries, and creates a reorganized store, leaving the original untouched. At launch, Dreaming supported only Claude Opus 4.7 and Sonnet 4.6.

OpenAI also calls ChatGPT's memory, which it overhauled in June, "Dreaming." That system maintains a user profile in the background and automatically corrects outdated information. According to OpenAI, this raised temporal accuracy from 52.2 to 75.1 percent.

Researchers at Google and Google DeepMind also let AI agents dream, but they use dreaming to improve an agent's search strategy. Their agent works through alternative solutions using results it has already stored, without calling the model again. Google has also built WikiSkill, a wiki for agents. As in Anthropic's system, WikiSkill keeps raw transcripts, distilled knowledge, and specific instructions in separate layers.
