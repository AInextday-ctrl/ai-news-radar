The Backyard Battle Over Amazon Delivery Drones ‘Stealing the Sky’

✍️ By Kris Maher

Amazon currently operates Prime Air drone services across 18 sites in 10 US states, recently launching in Markham, Illinois, with further expansion planned for Matteson. The deployment has triggered local opposition regarding noise pollution, lack of prior municipal communication, and privacy concerns, despite Amazon's assertions that onboard cameras are restricted to navigation and flights are limited to daylight hours.

  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Scaling last-mile logistics while facing friction regarding local airspace usage and community noise standards

Wall Street Journal Full Report

| Photography by Emily Kask for WSJ

BATON ROUGE, La.—They rise early here like bees from a hive—in this case, an enormous Amazon fulfillment center.

Fans watch as the Prime Air drones dart over trees, roads and power lines before they make a beeline to customers, descend in a rush of air and drop boxes onto their lawns.

WESTBOROUGH — Stephanie Stuchlik lives about a mile from Amazon’s colossal warehouse, where dozens of blue-vested drivers haul packages every day. But at home, under a clear sky in her lush backyard, she doesn’t notice any of that action.

Soon, her reality could transform, as Amazon plans to launch a fleet of 83-pound delivery drones that would zip overhead — up to 1,000 times a day.

“It’s going to ruin our town,” said the 40-year-old mother of two, who works in real estate. “It will ruin how I use my home to the point that I would want to move, frankly.”

That science fiction-esque reality is approaching fast. Too fast, for some residents, like Stuchlik, who are chasing any way to hit the brakes on the project. Amazon wants to convert a parking lot at the Westborough Fulfillment Center into an Amazon Prime Air Drone Delivery launching pad. From there, drones would fly in and out to deliver small packages to more than a dozen cities and towns in a 7.5-mile radius that would also affect parts of Worcester and Shrewsbury.

According to Amazon, Westborough drones are all but a done deal. But on Wednesday morning, members of the state’s congressional delegation weighed in; US Senators Ed Markey and Elizabeth Warren, with Representatives Lori Trahan and Jim McGovern, sent letters to Amazon and the FAA, demanding a study to assess the impacts of drone delivery in the region. They wrote, “Amazon is now trying to quickly move drone operations into Massachusetts under a streamlined regulatory process the state itself is challenging.”

“From noise pollution to fire risks, communities have plenty of reasons to question Big Tech’s plan to fill their skies with drones,” Markey said in a statement.

The town cannot regulate the airspace, as that is FAA territory. But it does hold one card: how the site will be zoned.

Amazon first presented the 6,800-square-foot launch pad as an “accessory” to the facility, meaning it would only need the town Planning Board’s approval. John Gelcich, the board’s vice-chair, said the town cannot deny a site plan as long as it meets safety requirements.

Amazon says it wants to serve 500 communities nationwide using drones by the end of the year. It already operates about 15 drone delivery centers around the country. This would be the first drone delivery service in New England. Amazon has proposed launching drones in Connecticut, but it’s met opposition there as well. As the technology rapidly spreads nationwide, residents are left questioning what, if any, power they have to challenge Big Tech.

In late September, roughly 13 residents, including Stuchlik, formed an opposition group they call Mass Blue Skies, and filed an appeal to the Zoning Board of Appeals. The group challenges the accessory classification and argues the launching pad is technically an airport, under the town’s by-laws, and should be zoned under a special permit. This pushed Amazon’s expected project start date of October to at least early November, when the Appeals Board is slated to deliberate on the classification.

“This is an incredibly pivotal moment for Westborough, as the epicenter of a potential drone delivery expansion,” said resident Christine Farmer, 63, a founding member of Mass Blue Skies. “If Amazon launches, other companies can come in as well.”

Amazon says it’s aware of the appeal. “We are continuing to work through the required planning, reviews, and approvals,” Lindsay Hamilton, an Amazon spokesperson, said in a statement. “Community and customer feedback helps to shape how we operate, and we’ll keep listening as we move forward.”

The company landed in this suburb 30 miles west of Boston in 2021 and has often been referred to as a “good neighbor” by community members and town officials. Half a dozen residents told the Globe in interviews they’re not against Amazon; many enjoy the convenience of Prime deliveries. Their resistance to commercial delivery drones is part of something bigger — fears of a grimmer, dystopian future, in which machines clutter the sky and erase the serenity of the place they call home.

Residents voiced their concerns at an Amazon presentation about the concept last month, where hundreds of people buzzed with disdain.

“Do we want to look up in the sky and see blue sky, or do we want to see a highway of drones?” one Hopkinton resident said at the Sept. 23 meeting, posted on the town’s YouTube channel.

Tareq Wafaie, an Amazon Air representative who took questions from residents for more than two hours at the meeting, told them, “There is no opting out of drones flying over your property. That is [Federal Aviation Administration] controlled airspace.”

He said he would relay residents’ concerns about noise, safety, and privacy to the company.

For attendees, that was not enough. Some complained that opting-out of the service would not prevent neighbors from making a different choice and summoning drones. Anything under five pounds, from pet food to medical supplies or last-minute mac and cheese, could be dropped at a customer’s doorstep in under one hour — or as little as 30 minutes. (The service is optional and can be free of charge or cost up to $4.99, depending on the order price and whether a customer is a Prime member, according to the company.)

Kade Crockford, director for Technology and Justice Programs at the American Civil Liberties Union of Massachusetts, has tracked the backlash that surrounds Flock Safety, a company mired in controversy over data security concerns and misuse of its automatic license plate reader cameras.

For Crockford, the Westborough community organizing against the drones does not come as a surprise.

“People are sick and tired of having [tech] companies dictate to us what our rights will be and what our lives will look like,” she said. “People don’t just want to be acted upon by the wealthiest men in the world. They want to have power over their own lives.”

The FAA issued a ruling this summer that it found “no significant” environmental impact from package deliveries by drones after a study conducted in 23 states — none in New England. Massachusetts is one of 15 states suing the FAA over the nationwide decision.

The four members of Congress argue the agency and Amazon have bypassed site-specific study requirements, and demand a response by Oct. 29.

“As new drone operations expand, operators and local community leaders have primary responsibility for raising awareness and addressing community concerns regarding those operations,” an FAA spokesperson said in a statement, adding that the agency’s role is to ensure compliance with safety and federal regulations.

Mass Blue Skies surveyed community members to gauge response to the delivery center. Out of more than 1,500 responses, 92 percent said they oppose it, with noise and residential overflights being the top concerns. Amazon says fewer than 1 percent of customer service inquiries it receives are about noise. It compares the noise to that of a passing car.

In other parts of the country, residents took action too late. In Texas, Brian Rowe offered a cautionary tale, as a drone zoomed overhead during an interview with the Globe. “One just flew past my house,” he said from Richardson, a Dallas suburb.

Richardson’s City Council approved Amazon’s drones last year, and Walmart’s fleet also flies above, generating a gentle but constant buzz throughout the day. He likened the sound to a lawnmower in a neighbor’s yard — only the drones’ whirring goes on all day, until nightfall.

Rowe, a tech and cybersecurity executive, lives about half a mile from the city’s Amazon warehouse, and at any given moment, he said, he can see or hear a drone.

“What’s the impact to the quality of life of my children growing up in a space where there’s a constant drone overhead?” he said. ”I see a future where the air is just full of this mess, and we’ve done nothing to think about the implications or the impact it has."

He offered a warning to Massachusetts residents: “You better make your voice heard before it becomes a reality,” he said. “It’s easier to stop it than undo it.”

Not everyone is displeased with the drones. Some people have recorded videos of their first deliveries and posted them on social media to songs such as Ariana Grande’s “Just Like Magic.”

A handful of videos have captured incidents like a drone hitting an apartment building or dropping a package into a swimming pool. Amazon says such misfires are “extremely rare,” and that drones use cameras and sensors for navigation. The videos and data captured are stored on encrypted drives and can only be accessed by authorized workers, per Amazon. The company says the stored data is used only for “safety purposes and to improve the customer experience.”

Versions of a consumer data privacy bill passed by the state Senate and the House could address some of the privacy and surveillance concerns raised by residents; the measure is currently in reconciliation.

Meanwhile, Westborough residents continue to raise the alarm over this, demanding Amazon listen to their concerns.

“We need to look at Amazon as a wonderful neighbor as they’ve been,” Farmer said. But for this project, “The people have spoken. They don’t want this.”
