Engineers turn to tech to squeeze more out of constrained electricity grids

✍️ By Martha Muir

The wind has long been harnessed to generate electricity. Now, with the advent of new technologies, a breezy day gives engineers the opportunity to increase the capacity of the grid to deliver more power where it is needed.

Utilities have traditionally placed hard limits on how much power can flow through their wires to prevent damaging them. But the development of a suite of sensors and sophisticated software that tracks local climatic conditions is allowing engineers to predict the cooling effect of the wind on the cables — as the temperature of a metallic conductor drops, so does its electrical resistance — allowing them to raise the safety limit to pump more power through the grid.

This process, known as dynamic line rating, is one of a number of “grid-enhancing” technologies that hold out the promise of increasing the capacity of existing networks. Other innovations include software that redirects power away from congested transmission lines, sensors to identify spare capacity across the grid and controls to balance supply and demand in real time.

Squeezing more out of installed transmission lines is key for grid operators as they struggle to deal with an increasingly urgent mismatch between supply and demand for power, as the world moves into what the International Energy Agency has dubbed the “age of electricity”. Global electricity demand is forecast to grow 3.6 per cent annually through to 2030, 50 per cent faster than the average rate over the past decade, according to the IEA.

Installing new electricity-generating capacity takes time — power plants and transmission lines can take more than a decade to build — but data centres can spring up in a few years. The rising demand, which is being driven by AI, industrial electrification, electric vehicles, heat pumps and air conditioning, has put utilities under pressure to find faster, cheaper ways to try to address the mismatch without spending billions of dollars on infrastructure.

In Britain, National Grid is using dynamic line rating on 900km of its network, which is expected to increase capacity by 8 per cent. The company says it has already saved customers more than £21mn over the past five years by reducing the costs of managing congestion on its electricity network.

In Norway, the grid operator Statnett has installed sensors on a transmission line built in the 1950s, estimating the technology could boost capacity by up to 30 per cent. “It gives us the opportunity to get more out of old lines,” says Halvor Lund, a senior analyst at Statnett.

In the US, utility PPL spent $1mn installing sensors across 31 miles of transmission lines in Pennsylvania, which meant it could scrap a $12mn project to replace existing cables.

As well as the financial savings, the potential to increase the capacity of existing infrastructure is substantial. The IEA estimates that dynamic line ratings and other grid technologies could unlock capacity for 330 gigawatts globally of generation, storage and demand, equivalent to roughly 3 per cent of the world’s capacity. In contrast, accommodating an extra 330GW of generation, storage and demand by expanding the grid would cost around $100bn.

Freeing up 0.5 per cent of peak demand at a utility serving 1mn customers would provide enough electricity to connect a new data centre to the grid rather than have to spend years building a new power plant, explains Peter Mainz, chief executive at Landis+Gyr, the Swiss grid technology group. “I know that in three years, you’re not going to have a nuclear power plant up and running,” he adds. “So I think technology can do much more.”

Other technologies can be used to change how electricity moves through the grid. Power controllers — devices which include long-established mechanical transformers and newer automated electronics — redirect electricity to underused transmission routes, while separate software can identify alternative routes to bypass congested lines.

More substantial gains can be made by replacing conventional wires with advanced conductors, which have much more capacity, but these more ambitious projects are costlier and come with construction risk. “If you string them with new types of conductors, you’ll double the total capacity of that line,” says Jigar Shah, a former director of the US Department of Energy’s Loan Programs Office.

Utilities are also using software to offer users a quicker connection to the grid in exchange for more flexible energy use on their part. In California, PG&E’s Flex Connect programme allows businesses to connect before grid upgrades are completed in exchange for occasional reductions in power use.

Customers such as data centres and electric vehicle charging stations can get connected 18 months earlier, according to the utility, while using batteries to cover shortfalls. “They’re willing to accept whatever the grid can give them right now,” says Alex Collins, a distributed energy resources manager at PG&E.

The pace of adoption of the new technologies is reflected in the changing utility supply chain. Switzerland-based Landis+Gyr, which started as a manufacturer of electricity meters in the late 1800s, says 43 per cent of its $4bn backlog is now made up of software and software services that energy companies use to monitor their networks.

The changes are also creating opportunities for developers of batteries, which can be used to supply extra power to smooth shortlived peaks in electricity demand and reduce the need for expensive grid upgrades. “I don’t need to design for the maximum if it only occurs for 15 minutes,” says Doug Chambers, chief development officer at Aston Power, a data centre power systems developer.

But these technologies have their limits. The extra energy unlocked by dynamic line ratings is weather-dependent and can disappear on hot, still days when demand might be highest. Relieving congestion on one stretch of the grid can also expose bottlenecks elsewhere, at substations and transformers.

While software can help utilities defer or avoid costly upgrades, it cannot eliminate the need for new infrastructure altogether — particularly as demand continues to grow.

The bigger obstacle to more widespread use of technology to eke more out of existing networks may lie in the utility industry’s financial incentives. Regulated utilities have traditionally earned returns on investments in physical infrastructure, giving them less reason to embrace cheaper alternatives that reduce the need for construction.

Even when technology is being considered to increase capacity, integrating it into ageing operating systems and established practices can be difficult. “It’s not a technology problem. It’s a change management problem,” says Aamir Paul, chief executive of Regal Rexnord, a manufacturer of power transmission components.
