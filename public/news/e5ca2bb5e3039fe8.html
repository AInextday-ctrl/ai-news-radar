How Billions in Safe Insurance Money Is Being Bet on A.I. Debt

✍️ By Niko Gallogly and Maureen Farrell

As of June 30, major US life insurers like Athene held $85.2 billion in AI-related debt, a 24% increase within six months, with projections reaching $185 billion by 2027. Tech giants are utilizing complex Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) to offload computing procurement debt, concentrating risk among a few vendors and sparking concerns regarding the stability of insurance capital exposures.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Athene, Corebridge Financial, Moody's: Insurance firms are heavily invested in AI infrastructure debt, reaching $85.2 billion
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Tech giants are seeking off-balance-sheet financing for large-scale chip and compute procurement
  • [Key Metrics] AI debt holdings by life insurers surged to $85.2B in six months.
  • [Industry Impact & Outlook] Risk concentration on the solvency of a few top-tier compute suppliers.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New York Times Full Report

When Elon Musk needed help financing a large purchase of artificial intelligence chips earlier this year, Wall Street offered an elegant solution.

Mr. Musk and his team of advisers created a financial entity that would borrow billions to buy the chips and then lease them back to xAI, his artificial intelligence effort. This way, xAI wasn’t technically the borrower, which helped make its official debt load look smaller to investors.

To help fund this complicated arrangement, $2.4 billion of the debt was sold to an insurance company called Athene, which manages life insurance policies and annuities for retirees, according to a Moody’s Ratings report.

The deal was part of an increasingly popular playbook: tapping insurance companies, which are historically among the most cautious investors, to make loans to tech titans like Mr. Musk and further fuel the A.I. boom.

The amount of A.I. debt being held by insurance companies has soared this year. U.S. life insurers, including some of the largest like Athene and Corebridge Financial, held $85.2 billion in A.I. debt as of June 30, up 24 percent from six months prior, according to Moody’s.

While this accounts for just 2.1 percent of life insurers’ total debt, their exposure is expected to grow quickly. Life insurers could hold up to $185 billion of A.I. investments by the end of next year, according to Moody’s.

Life insurers owned about 15 percent of A.I. data center debt as of June 30, according to a recent estimate by Barclay’s.

These debt investments carry multiple risks. For one, only a few technology companies, including Nvidia, Meta, Alphabet, SpaceX, Anthropic and OpenAI, are providing the guarantees for these deals, which concentrates the risk. There are also often ways for tech companies to walk away from these A.I. projects if their development is delayed.

“The life insurance industry is now in effect backing large swaths of the A.I. industry,” said Andrew Granato, an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin School of Law, who has studied the overlap between private equity and insurers.

“We should all be concerned about this, especially as the debt deals get more opaque,” he added.

Making possible this flood of insurance money into risky A.I. deals are credit rating agencies like Moody’s and S&P Global Ratings. They have blessed this rapidly expanding pool of debt with relatively safe credit ratings, which meet the criteria that insurance firms need to invest in a particular asset.

Much of this activity is being driven by giant Wall Street firms that are affiliated with insurance companies and sometimes help manage their investments. Athene, which bought some of the xAI debt, is a subsidiary of the Wall Street firm Apollo.

“Athene finances what we view as the safest and most defensible parts of the A.I. ecosystem,” an Apollo spokeswoman said in a statement.

SpaceX, which owns xAI, did not respond to a request for comment.

Insurers are attracted to A.I. debt because it is regarded as secure, but still offers market-beating returns. A.I. companies, in turn, have come to view the life insurance industry as crucial to supporting their data center expansions.

Last month, Brannin McBee, a co-founder and chief development officer of the data center company CoreWeave, told The Financial Times that he viewed insurers as an “unlimited” source of capital for future borrowing.

“Like, that’s all the money in the world that sits in the insurance tranche,” he said.

His comments spooked CoreWeave’s investors and lenders. Its stock dropped sharply the next day and the price of its bonds fell quickly, too. The bonds of other A.I.-related companies and data center projects have also dropped in recent weeks as investors have become more wary of debt risks.

A spokesman for CoreWeave declined to comment.

One of the lenders loudly voicing concerns is Ares Management, one of the largest private credit firms with $691 billion in assets.

In a recent newsletter, Ares argues that tech giants have found ways to bring insurers into risky debt deals with “two-faced transactions.” The tech companies are creating complex financial entities that are being assigned nearly the same secure credit ratings as the tech companies themselves. But, Ares said, there are fewer guarantees that these entities will repay the debt than if the tech giants had borrowed the money directly.

“Sometimes structural complexity and financial engineering are not used to improve risk, but to hide it,” the firm wrote in the newsletter.

Ares cites Meta’s data center in Richland Parish, La., as a prime example.

Rather than owning the project outright, Meta took only a 20 percent stake while the investment firm Blue Owl owns the rest.

Yet, S&P Global was comfortable enough to rate the project’s $27 billion in debt just one notch below Meta’s strong corporate rating because of guarantees the tech company provided. Those included a $28 billion lease guarantee over 20 years or to find a tenant if Meta walked away.

That rating helped open the door for life insurers, which, according to Moodys, funded $3.4 billion of the deal.

But Meta is not promising to repay the project’s debt investors. In a statement, a Meta spokesperson wrote that “Meta has not guaranteed repayment of the bonds.”

In a statement, a Blue Owl spokesman said: “When institutional investors and insurers buy this debt, they are not taking a hidden risk. They are buying exposure to the contractual obligations of some of the strongest corporate credits in the world.”

In another example, Meta signed a deal in March to procure A.I. computing capacity from CoreWeave in four data centers.

When CoreWeave sought a rating for an $8.5 billion loan tied to these projects, the rating agencies looked at Meta’s strong credit rating rather than CoreWeave’s, which is in junk territory, and gave the deal one of its highest ratings. Insurers were buyers of the CoreWeave projects’ debt also, according to S&P Market Intelligence.

But investors are increasingly questioning whether giant companies like Meta deserve such high ratings.

“These companies are not as safe as they once were,” Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh, a professor at Columbia Business School, said. “They’re sort of playing this game where they can pretend like they don’t have these liabilities.”

Meta, for example, has signed $356 billion in leases, including for data centers that it has not yet started. That amount is four times larger than the $84 billion of debt that it reported in its latest quarterly filing. Meta has committed an additional $455 billion in deals to buy chips, other infrastructure and hardware, agreements that it cannot cancel.

Last month, S&P Global announced it had revised its 2027 outlook for Meta’s credit rating to negative from stable, citing the surge in future leases and uncertainty about Meta’s ability to turn A.I. spending into revenue. For now, though, Meta’s rating remains the same.

In an interview, Naveen Sarma, an S&P Global Ratings analyst focused on Meta, said his firm did not consider a future lease commitment as existing debt because Meta does not have to start making the lease payments until it starts using the data center.

The Meta spokesperson said the company “respectfully disagrees” with S&P’s outlook. “We see significant product and business opportunities ahead of us, supporting our very strong credit profile,” he said.

Moody’s acknowledged the risk of all the debt accumulating off the tech companies’ books in a recent paper. But the firm does not consider those commitments to be existing debt when rating a company’s credit, a Moody’s spokeswoman said.

As for SpaceX, the company’s auditors determined the money that it intended to borrow off its books earlier this year to buy chips should ultimately be recorded as company debt.

But Mr. Musk and Wall Street continue to find innovative ways to keep borrowing. Earlier this month, Mr. Musk raised $40 billion to finance more A.I. chips through a financial entity that will keep that debt off SpaceX’s books for now.

SpaceX could pull off the deal, Mr. Musk said in a post on X, because “we’re investment grade.”

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

The infusion of life insurance capital signifies that AI capital expenditures have transcended traditional venture capital, infiltrating the core financial system via credit mechanisms. These debt instruments rely heavily on the unconditioned solvency of a few hardware and cloud titans, creating significant cross-industry systemic risk in the event of technology cycle failures.
