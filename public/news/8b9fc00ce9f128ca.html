Amazon’s $17bn Ohio AI data centre bet threatened by voter backlash

✍️ By Rafe Rosner-Uddin

Amazon's plan to deploy 1.8 gigawatts of data center capacity in Ohio faces administrative stagnation after voters approved 21 restrictive measures in recent midterm elections, threatening $17 billion in infrastructure investments. This grassroots 'NIMBY' backlash signifies a shift from isolated incidents to systemic regulatory friction, forcing hyperscalers to confront escalating community opposition and compliance costs in energy-intensive infrastructure expansion.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Amazon, Ohio voters, and grassroots organization Conserve Ohio (Ohio, USA): Passed infrastructure restriction measures blocking three major data center projects
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Local concerns over high power consumption, lack of local employment benefits, and project opacity
  • [Key Metrics] Affected projects involve $17B in capital expenditure and 1.8GW of power capacity, facing ongoing legal risks.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] The 'NIMBY' backlash against data center energy consumption is scaling into a nationwide organized movement.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Financial Times Full Report

Amazon’s multibillion-dollar bet on turning Ohio into an AI data centre hub is at risk after the state’s voters backed measures to stall development, threatening to derail more than $17bn in planned investment.

The cloud giant has emerged as one of the largest corporate casualties of a backlash against the US data centre boom, with three of its proposed projects impacted by restrictions approved during November’s midterm elections, according to FT research and data from regulatory tracking platform Regbase.

Ohio was the centre of specific ballot measures against data centres, with voters backing 21 initiatives to slow down development in the state. Several proposals directly targeted Amazon developments. Across the country, a further 19 such measures went in the same direction last week.

The affected Amazon projects were expected to consume roughly 1.8 gigawatts of power and account for approximately $17bn in planned spending by the company in the region, according to filings. The spending figure excludes chips and other equipment to fill these data centres. While other so-called hyperscalers such as Meta and Microsoft were also impacted by this month’s ballot measures across the US, the moves in Ohio meant Amazon is the most visibly impacted cloud computing giant.

“Amazon is going to have an uphill battle if they want to get these sites developed,” said Natalie Aresta-Katz, founder of Regbase. “They will have to find ways to convince communities to let them in or find another place to build.”

Amazon is racing to bring computing capacity online, having committed to spend the bulk of its planned $220bn in capital expenditure on AI infrastructure. It has invested billions of dollars in Ohio and committed to making it one of several data centre hubs across the US in a bid to service huge AI demand, with plans to invest $23bn in the state by 2030.

But Nick Owens, a lifelong Republican and co-founder of Conserve Ohio, a grassroots movement behind several ballot measures, said that voters had been angered by opacity around data centre projects.

“I don’t know how you can say that this is going to be a community partnership if people are completely in the dark about what’s going on,” he said.

Local residents are continuing to oppose a $4bn Amazon development in Wilmington, Ohio, with the potential for further legal challenge, according to two people familiar with the matter.

Prologis, a real estate company developing a site for Amazon in Trenton, filed a suit last Thursday challenging recent ballot measures.

The FT and Regbase analysed local permit documents for developments in dozens of counties across the US to determine how many projects were now threatened with delays or cancellation. The bulk of measures passed impact sites not yet under construction, while some measures threaten to delay development.

Nearly a dozen sites impacted by ballot measures last week did not disclose the end tenant for the project. The measures across the US impacted at least 10 gigawatts of proposed data centre capacity, according to figures compiled from public statements, hearings and local planning documents.Campaigners have challenged vast AI developments on environmental and social grounds, raising concerns around rising air and noise pollution.

Three sites across Maine and Ohio had already seen developers abandon data centre projects earlier this year after ballot measures were approved for the November poll, according to Regbase data.

“Voters do not believe that they can trust these companies to regulate themselves . . .[Big tech’s] response thus far has been symbolic and performative,” said Alli Finn, an organiser at the AI Now Institute. Big Tech companies have tried to respond to the concerns around data centres. Microsoft in March said it would stop using non-disclosure agreements with local officials around data centre projects, in response to local concern about a lack of information around the sites. Amazon and Google followed suit in October, while OpenAI said it would do so in the wake of the midterm results.

The companies have also made commitments around energy and water consumption alongside setting out billions of dollars in community-oriented investments. Amazon said: “We remain committed to setting a high bar for responsible data centre development, continuing to engage communities early in our development process, and delivering capacity for our customers.” Microsoft declined to comment. Meta did not respond to a request for comment.

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

The tension between hyperscale data centers and local grid/land resources has hit a breaking point, where purely capital-driven expansion is now being thwarted by localized political activism. As regional pushback becomes institutionalized across the US, hyperscalers must pivot their infrastructure strategy from pure cost-efficiency models toward a new paradigm of deep local social integration and public-interest advocacy.
