📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New York Times Full Report

Three of the largest U.S. school districts, New York City, Los Angeles and Chicago, are considering using alternate search engines after Google this month refused to remove artificial intelligence from search tools used by students, officials said.

Starting next year, Google said, its A.I.-related products, including Search and its Gemini app, will be turned off automatically for schools, and administrators can choose to turn those A.I. tools on. But there will be no version of search available without A.I. chatbot features, meaning schools that want to forgo A.I. will lose access to an important learning tool.

Google’s approach created a firestorm of criticism from lawmakers, government officials, education leaders and parents, some of whom said the tech giant was trying to force its A.I. into public schools.

Now, some schools across the United States are blocking Google’s A.I. in search — or considering alternative providers.

“If Google doesn’t reconsider their position, we will explore other search options,” Kamar H. Samuels, the chancellor of New York City schools, said in a statement. “We refuse to accept the false binary Google has laid out.”

But some educators worry that the generative A.I. that Google has embedded in its search engine for consumers could hinder students’ critical thinking, undermine learning and present safety risks. Some students have used the chatbots embedded in search to cheat, they have said, or scanned A.I.-generated search summaries instead of reading historical documents and news sources.

Educators are balancing those concerns with the reality that A.I. has already become an important tool in the work force, that a majority of U.S. teenagers already use chatbots and that many students will need to use A.I. in their future jobs.

Medical associations have also warned that developing relationships with chatbots could have negative mental health effects on young people. Earlier this month, two parents in North Carolina sued Google, saying their daughter had developed a plan to die by suicide after prolonged chats with the company’s A.I. on search on her school Chromebook. (Google said it had built safeguards into its A.I. features in search to help young people find emergency resources and was reviewing the claims in the lawsuit.)

Now districts like Chicago Public Schools, the fourth-largest U.S. school system, are urging Google to provide them with a version of search without A.I. features. “If such an arrangement can’t be reached,” Chicago Public Schools said in an email, “the district will look to other search solutions.”

Among the search engine alternatives for schools: DuckDuckGo and Microsoft’s Bing. Both offer options to disable A.I.-generated answers.

Google argued this month that it would be providing schools with more options to control student A.I. access — not fewer. Administrators could give students access to A.I. Mode, a chatbot-like function in search, as well as A.I. Overview, which summarizes search results, the company said. It is also pilot testing Guided Learning, a new chatbot widget intended to break down academic questions for students without providing easy answers.

“We are actively building these experiences in partnership with educators, listening closely to ensure our products support students and teachers,” Google said in a statement, noting that next year the company would turn off its Gemini app and search engine for schools “until schools decide which services to enable.”

Over the last three years, the company has made building generative A.I. a top business priority. And Google has integrated its Gemini A.I. chatbot throughout its software suite.

The fight over Google’s A.I. products has been brewing for months, with district leaders and teachers across the country approaching Google employees at meetings and online to request greater control over how students interact with generative A.I.

School tech directors in four states said they and their colleagues had repeatedly pressed Google this year to let schools decide which A.I. tools, if any, might be appropriate for students in different grades. And they warned that Google’s all-or-nothing approach to search essentially overrode more nuanced school policies on student A.I. use.

“We need to be able to control our own pace,” said Chris Warden, the technology director at Central R-3 School District in Park Hills, Mo., “not have Google tell us what the pace of A.I. adoption is going to be.”

In September, the New York City Department of Education and the Los Angeles Unified School District announced moratoriums on student use of generative A.I.

At the start of this school year, Google’s education team helped Los Angeles schools turn off A.I. mode for students logged into their school Google accounts, according to school board members. But Google said during its announcement this month that its A.I. features were so “deeply integrated” in search that it could not provide schools with a chatbot-free search engine.

Google’s decision prompted groups of parents to protest last week in front of the company’s Boston, Boulder, Chicago, New York and Portland offices.

In Chicago, several dozen parents and educators chanted slogans like, “Hey, Google, nice try, we don’t want your Gemini.”

Parents and students also protested in Venice, Calif., carrying signs like “We don’t need Alphabet to teach the alphabet.”

At a meeting of the Los Angeles school board’s A.I. committee two days later, Jack Kelanic, the district’s chief information officer, was applauded when he said, “We have choices” as to which company’s software to use. “We can change the default search engine literally overnight,” he added.

This week, Jenn Brown, a Google program manager, offered an explanation for the company’s refusal to provide schools with a chatbot-free search engine for students. Google now considers search to be fundamentally an A.I. product, she said during a monthly Google Meet for school tech leaders, according to a school administrator who attended the discussion.

New York City, Los Angeles and other school districts said they were using a combination of software controls and filters to independently block student access to A.I. in Google search. Even so, some New York City parents have found students can still easily access A.I. Mode at home when signed into their student Google accounts.

New York City has declined to participate in the pilot of Google’s new chatbot feature for students, according to the district. Instead, the district is conducting its own research of five other A.I. tools for potential use at the high-school level.

In the absence of A.I. controls from Google, some smaller school districts are also working to independently disable chatbots in search. In Missouri, Mr. Warden said his rural school district, which serves about 2,000 students, had signed up for a new service from Lightspeed Systems, an online student monitoring service for schools. The software is designed to block student access to A.I. Overview and A.I. Mode in Google search.

The backlash against Google has complicated some school districts’ plans to teach students how to use chatbots effectively, said Nick Melvoin, a Los Angeles school board member.

“Ironically, it undermines the goal of trying to get some A.I. into schools,” Mr. Melvoin said.
