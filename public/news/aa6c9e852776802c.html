US companies turn to robots to ease labour shortage crisis

✍️ By Christian Davies

North American manufacturers are accelerating robot adoption to mitigate severe labor shortages, with Q3 2026 orders reaching 11,000 units, a 25% year-on-year increase. Notably, orders for collaborative robots (cobots) surged by 56%, serving as a critical lever for firms to scale output amid persistent workforce limitations.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Financial Times Full Report

American companies are racing to install more industrial robots in factories and warehouses, as rising costs and worsening labour shortages threaten to choke off a nascent US manufacturing boom.

North American companies ordered almost 11,000 robots in the third quarter of 2026, up 25 per cent on the same period last year, according to the Association for Advancing Automation (A3). Orders of collaborative robots, or “cobots”, rose by 56 per cent.

Peggy Johnson, chief executive of Agility, a humanoid robotics company that listed on the Nasdaq this month, said that many manufacturing firms were facing “an existential problem with the labour that they can’t get”.

“There’s an older workforce doing manual, repetitive jobs that is retiring, we have a younger workforce that is not seeking those jobs, and then we have a government that is reshoring and that’s increasing the need for those jobs,” Johnson told the FT.

Successive US administrations have used a combination of generous tax credits and punitive tariffs to encourage companies to “reshore” manufacturing amid concerns over post-industrial decline in America’s heartlands and increasing competition from China.

Along with a post-Covid desire to reduce dependence on fragile global supply chains, the push has driven hundreds of billions of dollars of investment into US manufacturing over the past decade.

But rising inflation and President Donald Trump’s erratic application of tariffs have caused spending on manufacturing-related construction to fall since 2025, while employment in the sector has remained broadly flat.

Mike Conway, a partner at McKinsey who specialises in advising industrial companies, said that rather than building new factories, most US-based manufacturers were looking to unlock the “latent capacity” in existing facilities, including through automation.

“Given the need to scale, we’re seeing automation becoming the new normal,” he said.

Industrial robots range from humanoids to robot arms, cobots and so-called “gantry robots” that can ferry goods along perpendicular axes. Advocates argue they free human employees from “dull, dirty and dangerous” tasks such as routine inspections, lifting heavy items or handling hazardous materials.

Traditionally, only very large manufacturers such as carmakers have been able to deploy robots at a scale necessary to justify their cost. But technological advances, including the ability to train robots in virtual environments, have lowered barriers to entry for much smaller companies.

Sebastian Montalvo, chief executive of Texas-based aerospace and defence supplier M3 Aerospace, said that automation would allow his company, which employs 11 staff, to grow faster with fewer people.

“How we operate now is we have one operator, one machine, but I think with technology and robotics we may have one operator, five machines,” he said.

Alex Shikany, executive vice-president of A3, noted that whereas automotive companies had historically placed the bulk of North American robot orders, non-auto sectors including logistics, agriculture, life sciences and electronics together now account for more than half the sector.

“It bodes well for the robotics industry as we move forward because it’s less reliant on just one type of customer,” he said.

However, some experts argue that the speed of automation in the US is still not fast enough to make the country internationally competitive.

Robert Atkinson, founder and senior fellow at the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation think-tank, said that with US labour costs so much higher than in China, American companies should be automating even more aggressively.

“We need to double, triple the amount of robotic automation within five to eight years, or I believe we will lose a significant share of what remains of our manufacturing to China,” he said. “But we’re nowhere near.”

Analysts note that most of the robots being installed in the US come from abroad, feeding a tension in the Trump administration’s policies between its stated desire to revive US manufacturing, and its unhappiness about a surge in robots being brought into the country.The White House said last month that the Department of Commerce had found that “robotics, industrial machinery and their components” are being imported into the US “in such quantities . . . as to threaten to impair the national security of the United States”.But it added that it was holding off on applying extra tariffs on national security grounds because the US “still produces less robotics and industrial equipment than it consumes” and “lacks the capacity to satisfy the need for robotics and industrial machinery with domestic sources”.

Experts note an irony whereby the same shortage of skills that is driving companies to automate is likely to undermine US automation efforts. According to a recent labour shortage index compiled by the Alliance For America’s Skilled Trades, technicians needed to install and maintain automated systems are in shorter supply than almost any other category of skilled worker.

Agility’s Johnson believes that demand from companies for robots that can operate at facilities designed for human workers will continue to grow.

But the ITIF’s Atkinson warns that investing in robots to eke out incremental productivity gains at old facilities will not revive American manufacturing.

“When you build new things from scratch, you put in global state of the art equipment and technology — that’s what we see the Chinese doing,” he said.

“But we’re sort of retrofitting existing plants and it’s just harder to do. Maybe in 25 or 30 years we’ll have built everything we need, but we can’t wait that long.”

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

Industrial automation has crossed the threshold from high-capex investments to a scaling cycle driven by cobots and virtual environment pre-training. This shift indicates that manufacturers are moving away from dependency on labor arbitrage, focusing instead on unlocking latent capacity in existing facilities to hedge against macroeconomic volatility.
