Signal to Restrict Screenshots to Block AI Agents, Urges Regulation on Apple, Google and Microsoft

✍️ By Reuters

Signal has introduced default screenshot restrictions to prevent OS-level AI agents, such as Apple Siri and Google Gemini, from scraping screen data and bypassing end-to-end encryption. The company stated that these agents pose a severe privacy threat by leveraging system-level permissions and has urged Apple, Google, and Microsoft to provide more granular platform control tools.

  • [Core Event] Signal (Secure messaging service provider) (OS-level privacy and security): Implemented default screenshot blocking and urged tech giants to restrict AI screen-scraping capabilities
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] OS-integrated AI agents are effectively bypassing encryption by reading content directly from the screen
  • [Key Metrics] After news of Apple's AI agent development emerged, Meta shares fell about 2% and Anthropic more than 3% in after-hours trading.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Reuters Full Report

Secure messaging service Signal said it will restrict the screenshot function on smartphones to prevent AI agents from reading private conversations. In a blog post published on the 20th (local time), Signal pointed out that AI features built directly into operating systems (OS) are accessing device data in ways app developers cannot control, and demanded that platform operators such as Apple, Google and Microsoft provide concrete blocking tools.

Signal itself acknowledged that the measure is not complete. "The current wave of agentic AI integration is creating an environment that is increasingly hostile to privacy," the company said, calling it "an existential threat to Signal and our ability to uphold our mission." It added that "operating systems govern the very environment in which apps like Signal must exist."

The biggest concern is AI that is deeply integrated into the OS, such as Apple's Siri AI or Google's Gemini Intelligence. According to Signal, these agents can read what is displayed on screen and act on the user's behalf based on that, meaning that Signal messages protected by end-to-end encryption can be pulled outside the encrypted environment. The company noted that the moment AI scans the screen, Signal's security environment is neutralized.

With this measure, Signal will introduce a setting that blocks screenshot access on users' devices and turn it on by default. When Signal detects a screenshot attempt or an AI assistant's screen access, it displays a pop-up window that lets users lift the block depending on the device type. Signal also blocked Microsoft's Recall AI from taking screenshots on Windows last year. However, it said such protections are difficult to provide reliably on Apple's macOS.

Signal's concerns are backed by a recently confirmed real-world case. It was confirmed that Meta's AI agent Muse bypassed official integration tools to access Signal messages on Apple computers and Google Android smartphones. Muse used the broad OS-level permissions granted during the app's initial setup to pull messages in ways users did not expect. On Apple's macOS, it combined accessibility tools with the screenshot function to capture Signal conversations, extract text from the images and reproduce it inside the Muse app.

Meta countered that an agent must be able to see and interact with the computer to act on the user's behalf, and that Muse accesses other apps only through permissions the user has explicitly granted. Apple blocked that path with an OS update released this week, but at the same time introduced a new permission system aimed at agents that allows similar functions with additional warnings.

Signal urged that app developers be given a clear way to fully exclude their apps from AI assistants' data processing, including screen reading, notifications and file system access. Signal, which is run by a nonprofit foundation, has minimized the data it stores on servers, excluded advertising and provided end-to-end encryption. The company's judgment is that these protections are neutralized the moment AI scans the screen.

The issue comes as Apple accelerates development of its own AI agent. In a recent job posting, Apple disclosed plans to develop a general-purpose AI agent that handles a computer like a human, performing web searches, coding, app control and coordination of multiple sub-agents. It is considering a launch as early as the end of next year, but development is still at an early stage. Apple CEO John Ternus is said to have urged Craig Federighi, senior vice president of software engineering, to push the AI strategy faster and more boldly.

The competitive landscape is also heating up quickly. Meta's Muse ranked second among free apps on Apple's App Store with restaurant and travel booking and news briefing features, while OpenAI's ChatGPT and Anthropic's Claude also placed in the top five. Anthropic has expanded user testing of its own agent Duet, OpenAI has added features to Dots, and Google overhauled its agent tasks this week. After news of Apple's AI agent development emerged, Meta shares fell about 2% and Anthropic more than 3% in after-hours trading.

The market expects tensions between platform operators and privacy apps to intensify further. The fact that platforms can control the paths through which OS-integrated AI accesses other apps' data shows that competitiveness in the AI agent era depends not only on model performance but also on the design of access permissions at the operating system level. Apple has already been sued by the US Department of Justice, including over allegations that it blocked rivals' access to features and hardware, so attention is focused on how Signal's demands will be reflected. Signal assessed that in Apple's upcoming iOS 27.3, Siri will be able to call a car or book a restaurant through external apps, but that this is far from a full agent that moves across multiple apps and handles complex tasks on its own.

End-to-end encryption is facing a systemic failure risk at the 'display layer,' where AI agents' ability to scrape screen pixels renders traditional encryption moot. Unless platform owners establish more granular 'privacy fences,' we will see an escalation in conflicts between app developers and OS providers over data accessibility, likely necessitating a fundamental rewrite of privacy protocols.
