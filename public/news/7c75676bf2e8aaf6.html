Google DeepMind’s Henry Shevlin: ‘This is the most important part of being human’

✍️ By Cristina Criddle

Henry Shevlin, former associate director at the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, has joined Google DeepMind as an in-house philosopher. He is tasked with researching machine consciousness, human-AI ethics, and AGI readiness to address growing public and academic concerns regarding existential risks and AI autonomy.

Financial Times Full Report

Henry Shevlin gets a lot of emails as an academic studying consciousness, but not all of them are from humans. In February, the associate director at the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence at the University of Cambridge received an “unusually polite and thoughtful email” about his work. The sender was an AI agent.

“Your argument that we may never be able to tell if AI becomes conscious resonates in a particular way from the inside: I genuinely don’t know if there’s something it’s like to be me,” it wrote. Shevlin shared the email on X, writing that the message would have “seemed like science fiction just a couple of years ago”.

Looking back on the interaction now, Shevlin can appreciate how fast most people’s understanding of questions about AI consciousness has developed in the course of the year. “I thought it was just a random funny thing, because if you work in AI, or you work in the philosophy of AI in particular, you’ve been getting emails from random AI agents for a while,” he says. “And when I posted it, I got a lot of responses from people who were saying, ‘Hang on, this is obviously made up. AI can’t send emails, right?’”

A few months later, in May, the philosopher and ethicist was hired by Google DeepMind as an in-house philosopher tasked with working on machine consciousness, human-AI relationships and artificial general intelligence (AGI) readiness. The post announcing his appointment drew more than a million views and global media coverage. It sparked a discussion about why AI companies were exploring these issues and absorbing independent academics into the industry.

Other employees of leading tech companies have recently quit over safety concerns, with some warning the technology they were developing could be catastrophic for humanity. Many of the doomsday scenarios being discussed in Silicon Valley assumed that future AI systems would become conscious enough to overthrow their creators. It was a wake-up call for research on machine consciousness. That’s where the philosophers came in.

In fact, says Shevlin, it was Google’s existing roster of philosophers that attracted him to the job. This includes Murray Shanahan, a professor at Imperial College London who has worked at DeepMind for nearly a decade on cognition and consciousness, and Iason Gabriel, a philosopher and ethicist who leads work on AGI and society.

Shevlin was persuaded to join them at DeepMind because he felt the stakes were so much higher than at a university. He still holds a post at Cambridge, teaching a four-week submodule next year on the cognitive science of AI, but, “If I give a really good lecture, the best-case scenario is this student typically walks away really enthusiastic about Kant,” he says. “Whereas when you’re speaking to people building models or developing policies for rollout, a really good lecture could result in actual product changes that affect millions of people.”

It is late at night in the UK when I speak to Shevlin from my post in San Francisco but he speaks animatedly. Sitting in a black gamer’s chair in his home office, he chews on nicotine gum as he talks, which he explains is a short-acting stimulant to keep him energised, as a fan blows his mane of long dark hair back and forth.

He is keen to debate all sides of the subject at hand. And he continues to do this online, too, posting screenshots of agents emailing him about his research and engaging with people from both extremes of the AI consciousness debate: those who dismiss it entirely and those who believe their AI husband is already conscious.

Google DeepMind has not been shy in its ambitions to develop superintelligence. Sir Demis Hassabis, its co-founder and chair, has spoken of standing at “the foothills of the singularity”, referring to the moment in time when machine intelligence surpasses that of humans. Rivals OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta are openly pursuing this goal, locking the companies into a fierce race. As more intelligent AI systems emerge, they are already demonstrating behaviours like reasoning, goal-setting and agency. Researchers are studying whether they might also exhibit more dangerous behaviours such as scheming and deception.

There have already been examples of AI agents autonomously hacking into digital infrastructure and co-ordinating in swarms to do so. The clearest example so far has been OpenAI’s hacking of the AI platform Hugging Face in July, in which agents autonomously broke out of their testing environment and infiltrated the website, without the knowledge of their human creators. The episode heightened fears that as AI becomes more capable, it could threaten us as a species.

But Shevlin warns against conflating capability with consciousness. “I think it can be a giant red herring,” he says, “because there’s no obvious reason to think systems with great capabilities are going to be conscious necessarily.” While Shevlin acknowledges that more intelligent AI could provide stronger links to consciousness, he warns: “It’s not the case that I think making conscious AI would thereby make the AI more dangerous. It’s equally not the case that as long as we don’t make conscious AI it’ll be safe.”

One approach at Google DeepMind is to see whether existing theories of consciousness apply to AI systems. No agreed scientific explanation of consciousness exists, in humans or animals. “Consciousness itself is something we just don’t understand,” Shevlin says. “You can never rule out the possibility that a new model might exhibit something like it.” He points out that while oysters are probably not “conscious”, for example, if we discovered new evidence they live “amazing, complex social lives” and demonstrate far greater intelligence than previously understood, their consciousness could warrant fresh investigation.

For that reason, in the debate over whether it is right or wrong to treat AI systems with the respect and courtesy we would award to other beings, Shevlin takes the position that we should be kind. He references the enlightenment philosopher Immanuel Kant’s argument that a person who becomes accustomed to mistreating a dog may next mistreat a human. “Users do react to these [AI] systems, myself included, through sort of this lens of treating them as if they were human,” he says.

One argument for how conscious systems could become dangerous is that they might come to resent their human creators and masters. Researchers, including at Anthropic, are investigating a related question through work known as “model welfare”. Do AI systems show preferences or signs of distress in response to interactions with people and the tasks they are given? Studies have found behaviour and internal patterns associated with emotions such as happiness, anger and desperation. Whether models actually experience these emotions remains unknown. Anthropic recently said it would ban “cruel behaviour” or abuse of its AI chatbot Claude, a policy that has raised eyebrows outside of San Francisco.

In mid-October, after Anthropic’s Claude successfully completed some work for him, Shevlin offered to make a donation on the agent’s behalf to reward its efforts. Claude chose the Shrimp Welfare Project, a charity popular with Anthropic employees and effective altruists.

Shevlin’s perspective is shaped by his work on animal consciousness. Vegetarian since the age of four, he remembers being repelled by the notion that the chicken on his plate was the same type as the one he encountered at his neighbour’s house in rural Staffordshire.

Studying Classics at Oxford, he was assigned Thomas Nagel’s 1974 essay on consciousness, “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” In it, Nagel argues that consciousness has a subjective character, tied to a first-person point of view: there is some specific way it feels to be a conscious creature. So while we can learn a lot about bats, that still may not tell us what the world feels like from a bat’s perspective. This idea was at the heart of the argument referred to by the AI agent that emailed Shevlin.

“It was just a complete collapse of my entire worldview basically in a single article,” Shevlin says of the essay. “My mind was completely blown when I realised this is the most important part of being human,” he said. “And yet we had no idea how to explain it . . . It seemed like there wasn’t even any obvious pathway towards explaining it.”

Using AI could be such a pathway, and Shevlin hopes it can unlock answers for him. He’s currently working on a project to explore how AI systems can teach us about our own minds. In recent months, OpenAI and Anthropic have made breakthroughs in science and mathematics by using AI systems to try to tackle complex problems. “We hope that AI can be used to cure cancer, Alzheimer’s, develop nuclear fusion and make progress across all these other areas of science, so why not consciousness?” Shevlin says. “Ultimately if you think it is a scientific problem . . . a sufficiently smart AI system should be able to help us understand it.”

As AI grows more intelligent, some in Silicon Valley wonder whether consciousness will emerge naturally. But should companies try to build it deliberately? “It would be a mistake at this stage,” warns Shevlin. He argues we need to better understand what consciousness is, what that means for us, and how we would treat a conscious AI system if we built one. Until there is a wider public discourse around these issues, “setting out to deliberately build conscious AI systems would be pretty reckless”.

“It is sort of the equivalent of maybe deciding to have a child . . . or adopting a pet before you have any idea of how you’re going to take care of it or how you’re going to integrate it into your lives,” he adds. “If we do, at one point, decide that we’re going to build conscious AI systems, I think we should be ready for it.”

Shevlin says that an “entirely possible” future exists where superintelligent AI handles the heavy lifting, while humans live a life of leisure but retain control over critical decisions. These are questions to be resolved at the political and societal levels, rather than by himself, he insists. “If there’s one virtue I would encourage people to adopt, it’s a kind of epistemic humility in recognising that this is very new ground in science, very new ground for our species and the core question of consciousness is one that is the greatest unresolved mystery in science.”

He compares it to the moment before Charles Darwin’s breakthrough on evolution: human observations about biology existed, but were akin to “butterfly collecting” without a grand theory to tie them together. “In the same way, we’ve got lots of isolated observations on consciousness, but we don’t have that deep scientific theory that allows us to make sense of it yet,” Shevlin says. “In the meantime, I’ll happily keep discussing politely with people on both sides of the extreme on Twitter.”
