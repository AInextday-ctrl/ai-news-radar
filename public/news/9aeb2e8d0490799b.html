Fed chairman Kevin Warsh will almost certainly add another 0.5% to U.S. interest rates in December, according to the CME FedWatch futures index, and Wall Street is angsty that the move will cause a recession. “The Fed will put the U.S. and [the] world in recession if it continues to raise rates in a failed attempt to drive inflation to 2%,” Bill Ackman said recently.

“The economy is super strong right now, but the Fed is about to kill it,” Jamie Cox of the Harris Financial Group ($1.5 billion AUM) told Fortune.

The problem is that inflation—which at 3.7% is high above the Fed’s 2% target—is being driven by the price of oil (which the Fed can do nothing about) and the price of computer chips and tech equipment (which the Fed can also do nothing about).

If the Fed increases interest rates, it may derail areas of the economy that are sensitive to higher borrowing costs while doing nothing about the roaring trade in oil. AI is increasingly dependent on debt funding, and higher rates could kill off that liquidity.

“Tech drove 44% of GDP growth [in the last 12 months], leaving growth vulnerable as capex slows,” according to Wells Fargo’s Ohsung Kwon. “The fear is hikes into a slowdown, and an overly hawkish Fed could potentially drive recession fears.”

Obviously, the situation with Iran is out of the Fed’s control. But as Fortune’s Ellie Pringle noted yesterday, if Warsh continues to “look through” oil-driven inflation then bond investors will start questioning the Fed’s credibility.

“The path of the war is crucial for the Fed's path,” Kwon said in a note.

Investors have continued to dump tech stocks over the past 24 hours on continued worries about the stability of the AI trade and a report that the Trump administration was proposing a new set of trade tariffs on China, Europe, and 14 other countries. S&P 500 futures moved lower ahead of the open in New York.

“The risk-off tone has returned over the last 24 hours,” Jim Reid and his team at Deutsche Bank said this morning.

At UBS, Paul Donovan said: “The concern is the impact on U.S. inflation, and thus U.S. interest rates. Reports suggest U.S. importers will face only modest tariffs on goods from several countries. There is less certainty about goods from China, but U.S. importers became adept at avoiding those tariffs. A 10% tariff on a finished good translates into around a 4% consumer price increase, as tariffs occur early in supply chains.”

The tech sector saw the worst losses. The tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite fell 1.48% yesterday, much farther than the broader market. In fact, the equal-weight S&P 500—a notional index that values each stock equally—rose 0.53%, suggesting buyers in a range of non-tech stocks.

S&P 500 futures were down again this morning, but Nasdaq 100 futures slipped even farther: down 2.39%, another big step down for an index of its size.

The S&P 500 may have flirted with a downward “correction” around the Nvidia drama, but it’s nowhere near an official bear market, defined by a decline of 20% from the peak. In fact, a bear market is unlikely to happen, according to Deutsche Bank’s Henry Allen. The bank’s official forecast is for the S&P 500 to hit a heady 9,000 by the end of next year.

“It’s worth bearing in mind that the outright bear markets of the last 50 years (S&P 500 down 20% or more) have previously occurred when there was at least one of (1) a recession, (2) widespread expectations of a recession, or (3) after a Fed tightening cycle with multiple hikes.”

Note to self: We will officially be in a tightening cycle if the Fed raises interest rates in December, which it is widely expected to do.

Fanatics has become a one-stop shop for sports fans looking to rep their favorite teams and athletes, and now generates roughly $14 billion in annual revenue. For cofounder and CEO Michael Rubin, however, the road to building that empire nearly ended before he turned 20.

When Rubin was just 13, he started a business selling ski equipment out of his parents’ basement in Pennsylvania. A year later, he opened his own storefront. He was making money quickly—and even bought a Porsche before he could legally drive.

But around 1988, there was an unusually warm winter, and so his customers weren’t coming. “It didn’t snow that year,” Rubin recalled recently. “End of the year, I had $200,000 of bills, $80,000 of inventory, and I was sitting right in front of bankruptcy.”

To dig himself out, he negotiated his debts down to $35,000, borrowed money from neighbors, and used the proceeds from a new venture in the closeout business to repay his loans. Five years later, at 21, Rubin was generating $150 million in sales buying and selling closeout merchandise.

“I went from death in its eyes, bankrupt, thought I was a complete failure, giving up on entrepreneurship, to five years later, doing $150 million, making $10 to $15 million a year as a kid,” Rubin said. “And so to me, each disaster, if you learn from it, leads to your next success.”
