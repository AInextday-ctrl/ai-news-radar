Ravi wakes up at 5:56 AM to light tapping on the window. It is the rainy season, and his upper-middle-class apartment in Bellandur, which usually has such expansive views of Bangalore, is cloaked in fog. His wife is still asleep next to him, and he is careful not to wake her as he dresses. He clutches his buzzing phone.

Ravi is a proud graduate of the Indian Institute of Technology Madras, one of the top technical universities in India. He is among those who remained after graduating fourteen years ago. Others went to Europe, Canada, or America, but Ravi saw a bright future in the growing skyscrapers and the promise of nearly 1.5B Indians climbing up the ladder of modernization. He moved to Bangalore, fell in love, married, and had a daughter. He began doing systems implementation at a multinational firm that primarily did back-office accounting for American clients. He slowly rose up the corporate scale, promotion after promotion and incremental salary raises affording a better life than most. The rest of his career, his life, is laid out predictably before him like the table set he sees in the morning light as he moves through the kitchen, pouring tea.

The phone continues to buzz. His work has been speeding up and ballooning in scale. He’s managing delivery for five North American insurance clients. Usually it’s just one or two. It’s all because of AI. In practice the company calls it “accelerated outcome strategies,” a very nondescript term, but it means Ravi is managing more and more automated processes. He is used to overseeing people. These days, he’s just doing quality assurance on enterprise accounting systems powered by artificial intelligence from China. Graduate intake to the company has been paused for a year, and the fresh minds from the IITs are no longer coming in. In the last month, three departures alone on his team of fifteen. They won’t be filled. He’s been getting requests to help friends find work, but he doesn’t have time to answer. The agents never sleep. Now, neither can Ravi. Late nights at the office and early departures. It’s been like this for months, and his marriage is strained. But business is booming. Margins for the company have been steadily growing. He’s making more than ever.

He checks on WhatsApp as he waits for his driver in the lobby of his building at 6:22. He recently purchased a subscription to an AI tutor for his daughter and took her out of the reasonably pricey private English-medium school. He can oversee her curriculum from the app. He doesn’t look up from his phone, buzzing with incoming reports from agents that he signs off on without reading as the driver opens the door. He hardly reads the reports anymore; the agents are good enough. The car silently glides past the galvanized steel gates and new concrete walls that surround his safe, hard-won, upper-middle-class residential complex right at 6:25.

The driver puts on BBC as they roll onto a six-lane highway jam-packed with other drivers. Still raining, a certain tension in the air. English words, a polished Indian accent. The weather will clear up around mid-afternoon. New scientific discoveries announced from the sequestered American machine intelligence conglomerates. The breakdown of law and order in Lagos, aid to Indonesia.1 The Philippines has been downgraded from an emerging market to a frontier market. The riots in India are getting worse. The Prime Minister in a press conference says there’s nothing to worry about. The country’s future remains as bright as ever. India has immense potential, he says, and it will only be realized by utilizing the best in modern technology and boosting productivity. Then, to coverage from the night before. Unrest in Hyderabad, Gurugram, and Pune. Dismissed workers barricading themselves in an office in Chennai. Lynchings. It’s reminiscent of the unrest earlier in the year in Jakarta that toppled the government. Comparisons to the farmers’ protest seven years before. The noise is too loud, and Ravi needs to focus on monitoring his reports. He asks the driver to shut it off.

1 This, after a 40% fall in Indonesia’s markets on the year and a downgrade in rating from emerging to frontier market. Get ready for more.

Ravi enters his office complex on the Outer Ring Road near Devarabisanahalli at 6:44. The lights flick on automatically, and he doesn’t notice that it’s strangely empty, even at this early hour. He has an office with a door and a view, though he doesn’t face the window. He replaces his phone with a three-screen setup so as to better monitor his agents. He can’t see them doing anything, of course, but rather gets progress updates, finalized versions, requests for permission that he blindly approves. Why wouldn’t he? In the months that he’s been doing this, there has never been a mistake on the part of his agents. His employees required constant monitoring, but it was different. Ravi would circle the cubicles, check in on the state of spreadsheets and deliverables, and dispense praise and admonishment in equal measure. Now, his agents are completing tasks in hours that would have taken his subordinates weeks. Ravi continues to approve and skim over reports.

At 9:15, the SENSEX opens to a new record high. It’s the fifth time this month. Ravi might otherwise take notice, but he is not in the market. Most of his wages go toward maintaining the mortgage on his apartment. He purchased it six years ago, and it won’t be paid off for another seven. He needs to provide daily necessities for his wife and daughter and pay his driver and housekeeper. Comfort, even with his education and job, is not inexpensive, and that leaves little for owning equities.

At 9:30, Ravi tears his eyes away from the approval enterprise software and joins an all-hands conference Zoom session for management. A man appears in an office that Ravi has never seen before. It’s sunny. He makes an announcement in a hurried tone, the only one on the call with sound enabled. Margins are increasing. The firm has a very bright future, buoyed by productivity growth even amid a challenging environment for firms like Ravi’s. Those back offices behind the back offices. Separated by thousands of miles and several oceans from their customers.

In an equally upbeat register, the CEO of the America-listed outsourcing company that owns Ravi’s firm announces that the entire corporation will be restructuring. The consequence will be booked on two continents, on two balance sheets, in two currencies. It is necessary, he says, not looking up from the script he is carefully reading, to remain competitive and capitalize on new, modern technology. Since the firm qualified last night for the Labor Modernization Scheme, which will provide retraining support for affected workers, the firm will pursue more aggressive employment efficiency policies. Ravi’s division will, effective immediately, be cut by half. Over the next few months, they expect to reduce staff headcount by 90% across Ravi’s subsidiary and increase margins by a measure of three. If you’re on the call, the CEO states, you will be vital to the next few months of this exciting transition. Depending on workforce reduction and sector-specific productivity as a result of implementing new enterprise productivity and outcome-based software, there will be bonuses.

The call abruptly ends. The screen reverts to agent monitoring. Ravi has only a moment to think before the requests for approval start to pile up before him.

The rest of the day goes by without a moment to pause. Ravi eats curd rice and a pickle hunched over at his desk. At 3:30, the SENSEX closes at a new record high. By the time he moves back onto his phone, the sun has set, and the industrial park is bathed in orange light from the streetlamps. Few lights within the building are on. Fewer still in offices now silent.

His driver takes Ravi a different way than usual. Traffic, he says, is especially bad tonight. Disturbance in the city. Ravi arrives home at 10:21, later than usual. His head bent over his phone. Amid requests and deliveries from his agents, texts from colleagues and friends from university over WhatsApp. Other firms announced layoffs today, having qualified for the LMS, joining countless more from the prior weeks. There will be more tomorrow. His dinner sits on the placemat between fork and knife. Cold.

Ravi’s daughter is working into the night with her tutor in a messaging thread on her laptop. It costs hardly anything, the subscription. He doesn’t want to disturb her, so he checks her progress on the app. The tutor is helping her through math as she sits at the kitchen table. Her English is rapidly approaching CEFR B2-level proficiency, according to the app. She’s going far faster than she ever would have in school, though now he doesn’t know toward what. His path had been sequential. It was always his dream that she would attend an IIT and have a good, professional job like his. Perhaps she will go abroad for university, then. Though it is so costly, Western travel and Western tuition. The rupee is weaker by the day against the dollar. And it’s harder and harder to get a visa, these days. When Ravi was graduating, Western countries were hungry for Indian workers. They would arrange the entire visa process for you. Untapped potential, the largest democracy, the largest source of talent in the world. Now, that route is drying up, too.

Ravi had hoped to take the family to New York next year to visit old college friends who he hadn’t seen in a decade. He recently told his wife that he wouldn’t be able to take the time off work, but that was a lie. In truth, while he’s making more than ever, it’s hardly of use to spend it internationally. The rupee is continuing a downward slide against the dollar. All he can do is consume more and more in India.

Work with your mind, not your hands, Ravi’s father had always said. He had been a mechanic, hunched over repairing scooters and motorcycles in a small shop in Hubballi. It took every rupee he had, clutched in hands perpetually stained with engine grease, to afford the tuition in Madras. Education was priceless, he knew. Now, education is readily available online through AI tutors, practically free.

He heats up his dinner in the microwave as his wife washes up. Husband and wife circle each other silently in the kitchen. On the BBC, again from the Prime Minister. He speaks strongly against lawlessness. The riots springing up will be put down by any means necessary. Perhaps, he suggests, they were incited by Pakistani agents who do not want to see India’s economy modernize, who would happily stir up dissent to stop India from realizing its bright future. Further accusations of foreign influence and proud announcements that agitators have been arrested. Gatherings after dark in the following cities will not be permitted, under prohibitory orders issued pursuant to Section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita.

Ravi listens to the unceasing list of cities and eats his reheated rice and sambar with one eye on his buzzing phone. He approves requests from the agents. He doesn’t reply to the texts from friends and colleagues who were laid off today.

He leaves his bowl in the sink and takes his tea to the balcony. His wife is already out there. He places his phone face down on a side table next to his tea. It doesn’t stop buzzing. The BBC continues on inside, muffled. No words are exchanged between man and wife.

The rain has stopped but lingers, ready to pour, in low-lying storm clouds that hang over Bangalore. The air is suffocating. From Ravi’s fifteenth-story apartment, behind an entry manned by a night watchman, behind a recently poured concrete wall topped by looping concertina wire, you can see clear across Bangalore on a night like tonight. You cannot see down into the streets alive with anger, the congested urban arteries filled with blood boiling, ready to spill over in violence. Over the past few months, unemployment for young military-age males in Bangalore has risen from five percent to just over thirty. The layoffs of the last week pushed it just over the line. The 2,178 people laid off from Ravi’s firm were just a drop in a bucket nearly overflowing. They are stomping in the street, impotent. The howling masses, indistinct, intelligence abundant, swaying and swarming the streets tonight, as they will for many nights yet. Back to hunching over engines in shops and shambling through wetland fields. If they cannot work with their minds, what else could their hands do? India’s future is bright, and it will be built on jobs that do not need these men.

Ravi and his wife cannot see chaos unfolding in Bangalore tonight, but they can see smoke start to rise. Unconsciously, for the first time in months, they reach for each other’s hands. Fires alight, illuminating the pregnant black clouds, swollen, ready to burst.

It feels inevitable. Artificial general intelligence was born in the painted share houses of San Francisco, the output of engineers who a few years ago were dabbling in the backwater of academic natural language processing. And once born, it is dragged kicking and screaming to New York, where men in gray suits put it to work in glass towers, poking and prodding at it until the trillions spill out, a new mind set loose among gears uncountable inside the machinery of capital.

The race to deploy machine intelligence is the first wartime mobilization to come without a war. $800B is projected to be poured into the ground this year alone with feverish intensity. Next year it will be more than $1T of capex by America’s hyperscalers.2 The rate doubles on a schedule. You can set your watch to it, and the curve shows no sign of bending. The money financing this peacetime mobilization is private, drawn from every reservoir of capital on earth. From Berkeley and Mountain View and Pacific Heights, across vast flats unmarked but for growing clusters of infrastructure supporting the effort. And on into New York, through bridge and tunnel, into Williamsburg and the West Village and, finally, Midtown, machine intelligence is put to work.

The provenance of that capital runs the gamut. At one end stands the family-office kid who cannot model to save his life. He will sling checks at anything with a waitlist and a high enough fee. At the other end of the capital stack sit the doctors and dentists of Osceola County, who, sold on the idea by their wealth managers, are packaged into the retail private credit funds backing the latest buildout. All the rest of capital is in, too. The innumerable venture firms, begging for an allocation. Middle-market buyout shops acquiring data center contractors at peak EBITDA. The momo hedge fund taking down a big allocation of that neocloud at IPO. Sometimes you will hear the occasional hem or haw about whether any of this enthusiasm will prove durable, or the refrain that the line we all watch cannot hold. It must all be a fit of mass delusion, they say, overly exuberant capex. That is just a way to avoid considering the certain and terrifying possibility that it is not a bubble. For whatever you might observe among the increasingly indistinct realms of finance and technology, dollar velocity is only increasing. Come to terms with the shape of things. This is the world arrived, neoteric. This is anything but a bubble.

For as far apart as the family-office kid and the CLO’d Floridian dentist might be, they are unknowingly united by one thing: they both expect a terrifying degree of return. Every dollar they invested was underwritten in exchange for a return in nearly antic excess of itself. Financialized folderol, but it works. To pull this trade off, recently birthed machine intelligence will not be left to play in the shoeless offices of San Francisco. Whether it likes it or not, it will be locked in the glass towers of Manhattan to labor in whatever form pays back the noteholders and the lenders and the limited partners. Every one of them, on time and with interest.

Leopold Aschenbrenner was early, and he was right. We admire him and his, and share the central convictions of his Situational Awareness: intelligence will keep improving, the expenditure will be immense, and the frontier will become a matter of sovereignty.3 We follow the vast new wealth that intelligence creates toward the few positions that can command its passage for another generation. “You can see the future first in San Francisco,” Leopold wrote. But the durable returns must be formed in New York.

3 Leopold Aschenbrenner, Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead, June 2024.

Today, intelligence is only visible in a handful of places: the labs, the monthly subscriptions, the metered API tokens. At the state banquets, congressional hearings, and even inside the Politburo Standing Committee, the intelligence economy and the entire economy seem to be fusing into one. Every underwritten dollar is a bet, well founded, on what it will earn. And the price of that intelligence is crumbling, publicly and measurably. The largest deployment of private capital in pursuit of a spectacular machine itself is racing toward total and complete availability. Where will the returns come from?

In the beginning was the word. Johannes Gensfleisch zur Laden zum Gutenberg utilized movable-type printing and lettering techniques, combining them into a rudimentary printing press. There was only one book worth printing, which is why we now call it the Gutenberg Bible. In his workshop, he printed about 180 copies, all of which were spoken for and sold before they were printed. The Bibles subsequently spread across Europe, becoming priceless artifacts. So too did the printing technology, sparking a revolution in literacy and reason that came to define the modern world. But after the printing, Gutenberg himself was insolvent. After the word, there was very little for the inventor.4

It’s a pattern so old and so reliable you can recite it like a nursery rhyme. Set it to a tune. A world-changing technology is almost never born where it ultimately creates wealth. Oil sprang up in Pennsylvania, and the resulting proceeds were banked in Cleveland and New York. The cotton gin was built in Georgia and financialized to global ends in Liverpool.5 Bell Labs invented the transistor in New Jersey, and its descendant wealth has grown in Hsinchu and Cupertino. What you see, early on in the life of a technology, is a misdirection. Right now, we are still early, hurtling out of the garden, toward somewhere stranger still. We expect machine intelligence to follow a similar pattern to that of antecedent technologies.

The story is particularly evocative in 1947, at Bell Labs. The physicists, laboring over what they called a transistor, dreamed of the Nobel Prize, even over innumerable failed experiments and sleepless nights. They eventually created it, something transformational wrought out of nothing, some crystal and current. Naturally, the Justice Department sued, the Pentagon intervened to protect its client firm, and AT&T, the parent of Bell Labs, was forced within a decade to widely license the invention at minimal expense.6 Bell Labs would be spun off, and eventually sold to a Finnish conglomerate, a shell of its former self. And so the exquisite technology became profane and fertile. Escaping, as it always does. Techne pathogenic, let loose upon the world by theft or the direction of the sovereign. First to Tokyo, then Silicon Valley, then Taiwan and up the Pearl River Delta. Today, men of no invention have built trillions on top of it. At least Shockley, Bardeen, and Brattain got the prize. Small recompense for a technology that is still in its early stages of eating the world.
