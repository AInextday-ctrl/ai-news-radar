The AI Futures Project, citing METR's report on Anthropic's R&D pace, has updated its forecast, predicting the arrival of a 'Superhuman AI Researcher' by 2027. The report notes that AI development is currently progressing twice as fast as historical trends, with AI itself driving roughly 80% of this acceleration.

METR recently estimated that Anthropic is now making AI progress about twice as fast as its historical rate (they would not be surprised by anything from 1.3x to 3x), and guessed that “perhaps ≈80%” of the current acceleration is due to AI. If they’re right, that implies an AI-driven acceleration that is 5 months ahead of the AI 2027 scenario.

This level of acceleration due to AI is very unexpected. Previously, Daniel, the most bullish of us, assigned a 50% chance of Superhuman AI Researcher (SAR)1 before 2029. SAR is roughly the point at which a company would rather fire all their human researchers and rely solely on automated AI researchers than have a team of the best human researchers in the world with no AIs.2

Now, all of us forecast that Superhuman AI Researcher (SAR) will likely arrive at some point in 2027 (assuming no substantial deliberate slowdown).3 We also predict a 35-65% chance that Top-Expert-Dominating AI (TED-AI), AIs that can automate all white-collar work, will arrive before 2028. While there were many factors and sources of evidence that influenced us, the single biggest source was the METR report.4.

If SAR is coming in 2027, it is incredibly urgent to pause or substantially ‘pace’ frontier AI development, with the ideal being an industry-wide pause followed by an international agreement. Companies are not on track to align a SAR, and using a misaligned SAR to do recursive self-improvement (RSI) will put us in an extremely bad position. Additionally, if we train SAR in the near future, China will probably be able to steal the training recipe and maybe even the model weights (more).5

We estimate that METR’s report implies a 1.7x AI-attributable acceleration in the pace of progress today, relative to historical levels. This surprises our model; we made our most recent timelines forecasts less than three months ago, and even the most aggressive model-based distribution (Daniel’s) assigned less than 4% to the pace of progress being sped up by at least 1.7x in November 2026.6 1.7x AI-attributable acceleration is a bigger deal than it may seem; e.g., it’s a much bigger deal than making employees 1.7x more productive.

We focus in this post primarily on forecasting when Superhuman AI Researcher (SAR) will arrive. We also discuss Top-Expert-Dominating-AI (TED-AI): AIs that are better than top human experts at virtually all tasks. (more)

Research taste linear extrapolation: Even assuming that the recent pace of progress doesn’t accelerate further due to automation, the fact that the recent trend is above historical rates is a big update to our timelines; a linear extrapolation of the recent rate of AI progress predicts that SAR will likely be achieved by the end of 2027.

Labor uplift extrapolation: An exponential extrapolation of self-reported labor uplift predicts SAR in mid-2028. In addition to not modeling automation, there are reasons to think that an exponential is too conservative. However, self-reports may be too bullish.

Extrapolating Anthropic automation levels: Anthropic recently shared a graph showing how the percentage of R&D tasks at various automation levels has changed over time. Extrapolating this predicts SAR in late 2027; this method also doesn’t model speedup from automation.

Extrapolating software R&D uplift per unit of ECI: AI software R&D uplift must have recently been increasing by a larger factor with each ECI point. If we linearly extrapolate the rate at which this “factor of extra uplift per ECI point” is increasing, we get SAR in Dec 2027 (if we assume that the self-reported AI labor uplift chart is accurate) or July 2027 (if we assume that there was no meaningful automation 12 months ago and all the speed-up has happened after then).

Tom Davidson’s toy model: Predicts superintelligence in around a year conditional on a software-only singularity, so SAR sometime in 2027.

Filtered AI Futures Model (AIFM): Keeping the existing AIFM (including parameter distributions), and filtering for trajectories that match METR’s AI-attributable pace of progress acceleration estimates. This serves 2 purposes on top of the simple extrapolations:

Modeling how automation changes the rate of progress, allowing forecasting reasonably beyond SAR.

Informing what parameter settings in the simple research taste extrapolation are plausible.

The simple research taste extrapolation, which is a simplified version of the AIFM, gives July or September 2027, depending on whether you account for parameter uncertainty. Other simple models predict late 2027 to mid-2028.

When conditioned on 1.7x Nov 2026 AI-attributed acceleration, the AIFM predicts a SAR median of Feb 2027. This is faster than the simple research taste extrapolation because it usually has a higher level of Sep 2026 research taste (in the median case, matching a median researcher),7 and it accounts for further automation.

When additionally conditioning on a 1.2x May 2026 AI-attributed acceleration, predictions get slower because it indicates a lower rate of growth than is typical when only conditioning on 1.7x in Nov.

We then explore hypotheses for why the AI Futures Model was wrong, including parameter adjustments and modeling changes. The analysis is highly preliminary, and we were unable to come to strong conclusions. Our top candidates for what explains where we went wrong are:

Near-AC coding skills result in more AI R&D uplift than we anticipated. This could explain the combination of ~1.7x AI-attributable acceleration in Nov and (very roughly) ~1.2x in May, without AIs already having high enough research taste to contribute to the acceleration. In this case, the gradual acceleration no longer implies a low research taste slope, so steeper slopes and faster taste-driven takeoffs are more likely.

Skills besides coding and research taste (the two included in the AIFM), e.g., data generation, are driving acceleration. This potentially would have similar implications to the above bullet. But it’s unclear.

Conversion of uplift to pace of progress is too conservative. I.e., we modeled diminishing returns to AI research effort too harshly. This would point toward a faster takeoff.

Note that the gap between a person’s SAR median and TED-AI median is not their median time from SAR to TED-AI. Because the distributions of the time until SAR and of the time from SAR to TED-AI both have long right tails, the difference of the medians is typically larger than the median of the difference, so subtracting the two columns tends to overstate the median time between the two milestones.

It seems that many people aren’t tracking how big of a deal 1.7x AI-attributable pace of progress acceleration is, often conflating it with a weaker form of uplift.

METR describes a 1.7x acceleration of overall AI progress (due to AI uplift). This is saying the entire company is making 1.7x as much progress as it was before. Software R&D and research labor are only parts of the company, and inputs into overall progress. Because AI progress also depends on other factors, like compute, you should expect a 1.7x acceleration in individual inputs like software R&D or research labor to translate into less than a 1.7x acceleration in overall progress.

Labor uplift is how much faster researchers are getting work done due to AI assistance.

It’s as if the researchers read, write, talk, and think that much faster.

According to the METR reportan informal poll of Anthropic researchers yielded an estimate of 12x – though that’s just self-reports, which may be biased.

AI software R&D uplift is how much faster software progress is going with the AIs in present day than it would be without them.

It is much lower than labor uplift because compute is an important input to software R&D.

Imagine, for example, that your researchers get work done 12x faster, but you only have the same amount of GPUs as before. You can’t do 12x as many experiments, at least not with the same compute budget; you have to economize.

Pace of progress acceleration is how much faster AI progress is advancing in present day, compared to a baseline reference period (in Anthropic’s case, the baseline is “the fastest rate of extended progress we’ve observed in the absence of significant AI contributions to AI R&D”). It is usually much lower than AI software R&D uplift because (a) it is subject to diminishing returns to more software R&D and (b) it includes compute scaling.

The compute scaling point is simple: Part of the reason why modern AIs are smarter than older AIs is literally that they are bigger, trained longer, etc. More compute goes in, more capability comes out. As AI companies build more datacenters they unlock the ability to make larger AIs and train them longer. Since overall progress comes partly from software R&D and partly from compute scaling, a doubling in software R&D will cause less than a doubling in overall progress.

To expand on the diminishing returns point: AI software progress in general is subject to diminishing returns; the low-hanging fruit gets picked first, and to keep finding better and better algorithms requires exponentially more investment (more and bigger experiments, more and smarter researchers thinking for longer, etc.). Historically, the AI industry has scaled up R&D investments in a way that’s roughly cancelled out these diminishing returns. But the point here is, to get two years of research progress in one year, you have to more than double the AI software R&D uplift.

As an analogy, imagine AI progress as a car driving uphill.

The speed of the car is analogous to the speed of overall AI progress. Right now it’s roughly twice as fast as before.

The engine is research effort. AI software R&D uplift means that there is more effective labor going into making algorithmic progress.

The hill gets steeper as you go. As the easy algorithmic improvements get used up, it takes more research effort to continue progress at the same rate.

To make the car go twice as fast, the engine has to work more than twice as fast, because of the hill getting steeper. On top of this, to make the engine work twice as fast, the AI needs to make humans more than twice as productive, because progress is also dependent on compute, which hasn’t significantly accelerated.10

AI 2027 predicted 2x AI R&D uplift in Dec 2026; as argued above, this would imply a substantially lower pace of progress than the 1.7x AI-attributable pace of progress that METR’s estimates imply for Nov.

In that trajectory, 1.7x acceleration over the 2024.0–2025.5 rate of progress happens in roughly April 2027.11 So by this metric, we are ~5 months ahead of schedule.

Modeling Operationalization We condition this section on there being a genuine 1.7x acceleration over the 2024-2025.5 rate of progress (we also test 1.5x and 2x), as of Nov 1. We discuss uncertainty about whether it might be slower or faster below.

We focus primarily on forecasting the median arrival date of Superhuman AI Researcher (SAR), and the chance of it arriving by the end of 2027. In the AI Futures Model (AIFM) and in this writeup, a SAR is operationalized as:

A SAR is an AI that, if dropped into an AGI project in present day, would be as good at research taste as the project would be if there were only human researchers, who were each made as competent at research taste as the top researcher.12 The SAR must also qualify as an Automated Coder (AC), i.e., be able to fully automate coding in the AGI project. [By research taste, we mean the ability to select research directions and experiments, and interpret their results.]

It’s a somewhat stronger condition than AI R&D parity, the point at which the company would rather fire their human researchers than fire their AIs, because the research taste must be at the level of the top human rather than the average.13 If one wishes not to rely on our abstraction of research taste, then you can think of our SAR arrival dates as upper bounds for when AI R&D parity will be reached.

We discuss forecasts of capability levels above SAR to some extent, but we focus primarily on SAR because (a) we think SAR might already represent a “point of no return” (more below) and (b) forecasting post-SAR milestones involves positing a translation between AI R&D and general capabilities, which introduces more complexity.

Ajeya Cotra has a toy model of future acceleration here: If the first doubling of the pace took 12 months, and we’re now moving twice as fast, the next doubling might happen in just 6 months instead. Then moving 4x as fast, the next might take 3 months, then 1.5 months, etc., hitting limits within 12 months.

We think there’s two important corrections to this argument, which push in opposite directions. To understand why, it’s useful to have the following model in mind:

Overall “research effort” (i.e. research accomplished per unit time) is equal to[AI software R&D uplift] x [exogenous inputs].

Where exogenous inputs are some function of compute, human researchers, and data.

Every doubling of cumulative research increases ECI by k points.19

Every ECI point causes d doublings of AI software R&D uplift. (This is the same type of value that Economics of RSI refers to as “the increase in R&D effort per additional unit of ECI”.20) This has been approximately 0 for most of AI history (since AI hasn’t helped with AI R&D) but now seems to be above 0.

If dk>1, a doubling of cumulative research more than doubles research effort, and progress can accelerate even holding exogenous inputs fixed.

The toy model effectively assumes that the exogenous inputs were constant, and assumes that the growth of [AI software R&D uplift] was large enough to both overcome diminishing returns and to increase the rate of ECI/y by 1.7x. However, in reality, the research produced during 2026 was due to a combination of an increase in the growth rate of [AI software R&D uplift] and the normal growth of exogenous inputs. The fact that exogenous inputs still grew at their normal rate cancelled out a lot of the diminishing returns, which means that the growth rate of AI software R&D uplift needed to increase somewhat less in order to achieve the 1.7x increase in ECI/y. (Relative to how much it would have had to increase without the ‘normal’ growth in exogenous inputs.) Taking this into account, we will infer lower values of dk, and dk>1 is less likely.
