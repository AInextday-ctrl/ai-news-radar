Archive of Our Own (AO3), operated by the nonprofit Organization for Transformative Works, has resumed online services two weeks after an undisclosed security incident. The platform is currently working with security consultants to mitigate risks and has advised users to reset their passwords, while some features like external work imports remain temporarily disabled.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New York Times Full Report

It’s a library of nearly 19 million works, more than double the circulating materials of the New York Public Library, so when it was knocked offline in a “security incident” more than two weeks ago, its 12 million users were aghast.

The volunteers who run the fan fiction site AO3, short for “Archive of Our Own,” had quickly assured its enormous community that no data — especially all those works — had been lost and that it would be restored in four weeks.

But on Sunday, the repository of homespun stories based on works like “Harry Potter,” “Twilight” and “Heated Rivalry” was mostly back online, with some features — like importing external work — temporarily disabled, to help the site return earlier than expected.

“The intensity of the response speaks to how deeply fan fiction has become woven into our lives,” Dana Bryce, a writer and cultural commentator in Toronto, said on Sunday afternoon.

“The AO3 outage has shown how deeply participatory storytelling is now embedded in everyday cultural life,” Ms. Bryce said. “And its absence made the importance of that relationship visible.”

The works hosted on the nonprofit, noncommercial archive are inspired by books, films and even celebrities, like the members of K-pop bands. It is run entirely by volunteers, and operated by the Organization for Transformative Works.

She said that fan fiction democratizes storytelling, and that AO3 readers were not limited by long publishing cycles or a single author’s pen.

“It’s a shared experience,” she said. “Instead of reading in silos, audiences can read and write as a collective, reimagining, revising, reinterpreting and expanding fictional worlds, becoming active participants in storytelling.”

AO3 said on Sunday that it would not disclose what had happened to the site, referring to the outage as a “security incident.”

It said it would not share specifics of mitigating changes it was putting in place but did say it was consulting with a security firm.

It recommended that users change their passwords, while noting that “we have no reason to believe that the user database was compromised.”

There was so much site traffic on Sunday that some users faced delays loading it. “This is an intentional measure to help manage traffic,” AO3 said.

Ms. Bryce was not surprised by readers’ response on Sunday, calling AO3 “a collective cultural archive.”

“It’s a living record of evolving audience desires and attachments,” she said.
