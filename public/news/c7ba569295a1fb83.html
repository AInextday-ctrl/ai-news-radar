Can A.I. Take the Edge Off One of Hollywood’s Worst Jobs?

✍️ By Matt Stevens

Hollywood visual effects studios are deploying AI and machine learning tools to automate rotoscoping, a labor-intensive process that can cost up to $75,000 per minute. This shift aims to reduce operational costs and production bottlenecks while maintaining professional fidelity.

⚡ Executive Summary & Key Facts

  • [Core Event] Hollywood VFX artists and studios (Post-production pipeline): Automating frame-by-frame rotoscoping with AI
  • [Root Cause & Mechanism] Reducing labor costs and production time
  • [Key Metrics] Traditional production cost reaching $75,000 per minute.
  • [Key Position & Outlook] Focus on achieving industrial-grade precision with AI tools.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

New York Times Full Report

Tedious. Mind-numbing. Soul-destroying.

Ask visual-effects artists in Hollywood and most will agree: The task known as rotoscoping, while essential, is no fun.

Perhaps that is why an industry that has at times had its pitchforks out for everything artificial intelligence seems open to the idea that the technology can alleviate this particular burden.

Rotoscoping — even the name is insipid — involves methodically tracing around an object or a person, frame by frame and often for hours so that it can be isolated from the rest of the shot. A hologram of Princess Leia does not plead for help from Obi-Wan Kenobi in the original “Star Wars” (1977) without rotoscoping. Nor do the light sabers glow.

But can A.I. ease the drudgery? And can it do so with the same expert precision that human hands have provided for decades? What happens to those workers if their jobs get replaced?

More than a dozen people who work in visual effects in Hollywood acknowledged in interviews that some of the A.I.-powered rotoscoping tools they have begun to tinker with may be saving time and reducing tedium. But they stressed that the tools were far from perfect. Almost more important, they added, rotoscoping is a slice of moviemaking that offers valuable insight into how A.I. has begun to influence the industry.

“A.I. is not a simple conversation,” said Sang-Jin Bae, the director of the Martin Scorsese Virtual Production Center at New York University. But rotoscoping, he added, “is actually the perfect case study.”

Few viewers think about rotoscoping when watching a movie, but, as a foundational part of visual effects, it is indispensable.

Ever cut out a person from an image using Photoshop? “Roto,” as some call it, is like that but way more complicated and time-consuming. As characters and objects move through the frames of a film, each frame must be rotoed again to match the movements.

In many cases, rotoing requires breaking an object into sections and drawing shapes for each section. All those shapes must be repositioned to track the object’s progression across each frame.

“If you have an actor with long blond hair, and they’re flipping in the air fighting a robot, you need to roto every single hair,” said Jason Gottlieb, a longtime visual effects artist. “It’s the worst.”

Perhaps that is why rotoscoping often falls to entry-level employees. Marti Romances, a founder and the creative director of Territory Studio, recalled doing it 20 years ago.

“I wish back then, that instead of spending the first two years of my career doing these tedious things, I could have done other things that would teach me more about creativity,” he said.

Because all shots are different, experts hesitate to generalize about the costs and time involved in rotoscoping.

By some estimates, a person might spend a week rotoscoping a complicated five-second shot. Roto for an intricate one-minute scene could cost as much as $75,000.

Varying forms of machine learning have, in fact, been assisting with the most painstaking parts of moviemaking for several years, before so many advancements were grouped under the umbrella term of A.I.

In “Dune: Part Two” (2024), a machine-learning tool was used to locate all the eyeballs of the Fremen people and color them a radiant blue. When “Dune: Part One” was made a few years earlier, that work was accomplished only through countless hours of manual rotoscoping.

Just this year, companies including Adobe and Foundry have released tools that can, with just a click from a human being, find objects and trace around them. They can also ensure that an object and its shapes move in synchronicity across frames.

The results are seldom perfect. But with only a few human-guided tweaks, they often can be. Foundry, a creative software developer based in London, estimates that its SmartRoto tool can accelerate the roto process by a factor of four.

Any filmmaker with a smaller budget than the “Dune” franchise can turn to generative A.I. tools, which tend to be cheaper and more accessible. Need to roto distracting power lines out of a scene? Generative A.I. can do that. But the results often appear a little fuzzy, like your body against a video call’s virtual background. And a little fuzzy, visual artists say, looks a lot fuzzy on a big screen.

“Everyone asks for a magic button,” said Juan Salazar, Foundry’s creative director. “That button doesn’t exist.”

The other catch to employing A.I. to solve the riddle of rotoing, one that has been stoking fears in many industries beyond Hollywood, is what happens to the workers?

As it turns out, because rotoing has been expensive and labor-intensive for so long, an overwhelming majority of those jobs have already been moved to India, where costs are lower than in the United States.

Some artists who remain stateside hope that the increased efficiency brought on by A.I. won’t simply put them out of a job, but free them up to engage with more fulfilling filmmaking tasks.

Hope also exists in that the companies experimenting with A.I. still need trained artists to clean up the substandard content most models typically produce. Fuzzy outlines don’t win Oscars.

Still, many people who work in the industry are distressed, even enraged, by what seems to be A.I.’s ineluctable march toward control. Others are more ambivalent, noting that new technologies are often overhyped.

“A.I. is a tool, but that’s all it is,” said Maggie Kraisamutr, a longtime visual effects artist. “It’s not a replacement for human beings who are creating art.”

How long that remains true is the question everyone is asking.
