SemiAnalysis Full Report

Stargate Abilene has become the symbol of America’s AI buildout. It is a gigawatt-scale campus on the Texas plains built for OpenAI. China has campuses on a similar scale, though few people outside the country have had a look inside. In September, we visited one, and we found the speed, scale, ultra low cost, and deep vertical integration astonishing.

During the trip, we toured Inner Mongolia, China’s fastest-growing AI datacenter region. It's the “West Texas” of China, but while the Lone Star State faces its highest ever anti-datacenter sentiment, Inner Mongolia is accelerating and faces no such backlash.

Our main stop was Galaxy, a campus in Ulanqab planned for up to 800MW of IT capacity.

Galaxy is about the size of Abilene’s core campus. Its first 120MW phase was delivered within a year at a fifth of US costs. All four phases are built-to-suit for two internet giants.

Envision, SemiAnalysis China Datacenter Model When we visited, the rest of the campus was still a busy construction site, and the surrounding Chahar High-tech Zone had nearly 30,000 construction workers on site across 22 datacenter projects according to a local report. As we noted in our primer on the China datacenter market, with this much labor available, Chinese datacenters rarely take a fully modular approach, yet construction costs keep falling. We take a closer look at why in this report.

SemiAnalysis China Datacenter ModelThe datacenter is owned by Envision Group, a renewable systems integrator with more than 100GW of wind turbines installed worldwide. It came to datacenters from energy management, a path many others are taking today. Yet it is one of the most vertically integrated players we have ever seen.

Beyond running the datacenter, Envision builds its own wind farms and a dedicated power line to supply it. Its blade factory sits just across the road from the Galaxy datacenter campus, with finished blades stacked in the yard.

Inside the campus, it designed and integrated the low-voltage power modules and the 800V system, manufactured the battery storage that smooths the load, and co-designed the prefabricated substation.

In China, datacenters have been running on DC power for almost two decades, from China Telecom’s 240V DC pilots in 2007 to Alibaba’s Panama power system in 2018. Today, roughly a quarter of Chinese datacenters built in the past decade run on DC, while about 97% of US datacenters still use AC UPS. An SST is a natural next step on that path, and we cover the evolution and the pilot in detail later in this report.

Envision comes to datacenters as a renewable systems integrator. Beyond building wind turbines, it designs, integrates, and manages power systems across the full chain, from generation and storage to computing loads. Its affiliate, Envision AESC, develops and integrates batteries, energy storage systems, and the power electronics and software that link them.

Inner Mongolia has far more wind than its existing industry and population can use, so policy encourages bringing new demand to where the renewable resources are. In Chifeng, Envision built a 2GW off-grid wind, solar and storage system that feeds a green ammonia plant. The system runs entirely on power electronics, with no synchronous generator. A small pilot datacenter built with Tencent’s modular T-Block units runs on the same system. Galaxy is a larger application of the same idea.

This is a pattern we see across China right now. Industrial groups with an edge in energy, construction or electrical equipment are pivoting into AI infrastructure. Project returns are thin, with IRRs squeezed hard, so the reward often comes from elsewhere. For Envision, datacenters create new demand for the power and equipment it already produces. For many listed companies, the draw is the stock market.

Of these newcomers, Envision is one of the most ambitious. Its datacenter plans in Inner Mongolia add up to 3GW. Galaxy is the first. Its initial phase was delivered in August 2026, and the full campus is expected to be completed by the end of 2027.

In addition to scale, the two share a starting point. Both are developed by companies with roots in energy, and both were planned around their own power. Abilene draws on the grid and on gas turbines on site, while Galaxy relies largely on wind.

What sets Galaxy apart from Abilene, and what draws our attention, is its vertical integration.

Galaxy’s power comes through a model China calls source-grid-load-storage (”SGLS”). Under this model, a project builds its own wind and/or solar farm, battery storage and datacenter, and links them through a dedicated line instead of the public grid.

For hyperscalers, the appeal of an SGLS project is long-term power at a largely fixed cost. Power from wind and solar also counts toward green targets.

Inner Mongolia holds about 57% of China’s usable wind resources. A wind farm here runs on average about 2,800 full-load hours a year, a capacity factor of roughly 32%. Ulanqab is windier, with a capacity factor of 35–40%. That sets the starting point for how much of an SGLS project’s power can come from wind.

For Galaxy, Envision aims to supply over 60% of the first two phases’ power use with its own wind farms. Three factors make this possible:

Siting. Envision chose windy sites, even though they sit about two hours’ drive from the campus. The two wind farms are about 80km apart, which cuts the hours in a year when neither has wind.

Scale. The wind farms have a combined 420MW of installed capacity, about 1.75 times the 240MW datacenter load. With this oversizing, even light-wind hours can still cover much of the load.

Storage. A 4-hour battery storage system shifts power from windy hours into calm ones. Most SGLS projects add storage too, but Envision makes its own storage systems and runs them on its own software, which we detail in the next section.

The second role goes beyond Envision’s traditional business into the datacenter space. For Galaxy, Envision designed and integrated the low-voltage power modules and the 800V SST system.

800V DC is the architecture that Nvidia is pushing for its next generation of high-density racks, as Kyber racks at around 600kW cannot be served efficiently at today’s voltages. In the US, the industry is still early on this path. SSTs that convert medium-voltage AC straight into 800V DC are the final step. Galaxy’s SST pilot is only 5MW, but it already runs 800V DC all the way to the rack at a live hyperscaler site. We discuss this in detail shortly.

Lastly, Envision co-designed the prefabricated substation sitting inside the campus. The substation is built and tested as modules in the factory while civil work goes on at the site, then shipped and connected on site. This takes less than 3 months, versus about 6 months for a conventional indoor substation.

Today, vertical integration on its own does not lead to hyperscaler orders, but two trends suggest it could matter more over time.

First, access to power is becoming central to datacenter development. In the US, developers bridge the power gap with on-site gas turbines, fuel cells and batteries. In Southeast Asia, governments have been pushing developers to bring their own power this year. Malaysia now requires datacenters to source at least 30% of their power from renewables and encourages on-site generation. In China, approvals increasingly favor projects that pair compute with green power, under the national push for “Compute Power Coordination.” Across different markets, access to energy is becoming part of what a datacenter developer sells.

Second, the power architecture from the grid connection to the rack is being redesigned. In the US, the industry is moving toward higher-voltage DC power, while battery backup units (BBUs) and BESS are starting to take over the role of UPS. The technology roadmap is not settled yet, but the shift looks set to continue, giving integrated players room to compete differently from traditional developers.

These trends have drawn other players onto a similar path. The closest case is the tie-up between VNET, CATL and Zhongheng Electric.

VNET is a leading colocation operator, CATL is the world’s largest EV battery maker, and Zhongheng Electric is China’s largest supplier of HVDC power systems for datacenters, working with both Alibaba and ByteDance.

In 2026, CATL became VNET’s largest shareholder and announced a deal to take a 49% stake in Zhongheng Electric’s controlling shareholder. It has since agreed with VNET to jointly develop gigawatt-scale datacenter campuses, and separately with Zhongheng to work on datacenter power systems. Between them, VNET covers the datacenter side, CATL leads green power and storage, and Zhongheng brings power conversion inside the hall.

CATL is also one of DeepSeek’s largest outside investors, which extends its reach downstream to the model layer.

Where Envision builds most of the pieces in-house, CATL links them through partnerships and equity stakes.

China’s datacenter market is highly competitive. Rents have roughly halved over the past five years, to about $40/kW/month excluding power. Capex has fallen about 25% over the same period, to less than $3mm per MW. Power in Inner Mongolia costs about $0.055/kWh all-in, among the lowest in the world.

In a market like this, owning more of the value chain is one way to differentiate, a playbook familiar from China’s EV market, where players like BYD make much of what goes into their cars.

It is too early to say whether vertical integration will become a meaningful advantage in China’s datacenter market. For now, it gives integrated players more control over cost and schedule, but hyperscalers still choose vendors mainly on resources and track record. That could change as power becomes harder to secure and the power system inside the datacenter is redesigned.
