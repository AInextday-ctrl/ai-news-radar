📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Wired AI Full Report

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang told employees on Tuesday that the company’s code of conduct allows room for a “second chance,” that succession planning is a “flawed’ process,” and that he would work to rein in his “insanely intense” management style, according to remarks reviewed by WIRED.

During his first all-hands since returning as CEO, Huang acknowledged that he violated the company’s policies and made some “very serious personal mistakes” that damaged his relationships with his wife and two children and prompted him to resign. But he said Nvidia’s board of directors made the appropriate decision for the company in welcoming him back. He added that the board made that call, not “the highest levels of our government.”

Huang spoke for about an hour, offering deeply personal thoughts about his therapy sessions and drawing applause at times from employees at Nvidia’s Silicon Valley headquarters as he spoke about the company’s success and future. He didn’t take any questions. While some Nvidia employees celebrated Huang’s return, others remained concerned about his behavior and the board’s decision to reinstate him.

“This is going to be the hardest company meeting I’ve ever done, and the second hardest meeting I have ever done,” Huang said as he began to speak. “The hardest meeting I’ve ever done was with my wife.”

Nvidia’s board accepted Huang’s resignation earlier this month, citing concerns about his personal behavior and lack of candor following news reports about extramarital affairs, including one with a woman accused of having ties to Chinese intelligence services. The very same board reinstated him on Monday, just 11 days later, after receiving fresh assurances from Huang and learning from the Trump administration that he was not under federal criminal scrutiny and his return would not be a risk to national security.

Huang and Nvidia, though, continue to face other legal and regulatory challenges. The board is investigating whether he was the target of foreign intelligence. US lawmakers expect him to testify before Congress next week. Lawsuits and other probes are ongoing into Nvidia’s disclosures about Huang’s behavior and its compliance with US export controls on chips.

Nvidia did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Huang told staffers that he has been going to therapy “every day for an hour and a half” and that it’s “been helping me a lot.” He added that he “made some very serious personal mistakes. I hurt my family, I hurt my wife.”

While Huang said he had never knowingly made a decision contrary to the company’s interests, he conceded that “in this one area” he had lacked “the courage” to disclose information that he knew would hurt his family, seemingly referring to the extramarital affairs.

“The board and I spent the whole weekend together, and they, of course, they've been contemplating, and they've been collecting facts,” Huang said. “They came to the judgment that they would like to invite me back as the CEO, to give me a second chance.”

The CEO emphasized that Nvidia’s code of conduct, much of which he said he wrote himself, still governs how employees are expected to treat one another and is intended to “create an environment that’s safe for people.”

Huang told employees that violations of the code would not be tolerated. But that “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” he said. “Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” would factor in.

“It has to always be evaluated in the context of the human,” he said. “And this company has been more than gracious, more than thoughtful, more than fair in giving me a second chance.”

Huang also pushed back against the notion that he had received preferential treatment because of his status in Silicon Valley. “There are rumors on the internet that apparently I’m a tech bro, and therefore I’m back,” Huang said. He rejected the suggestion that he was above the law or Nvidia’s code of conduct.

Later in the meeting, Huang addressed criticism of his self-described “deeply intense, insanely intense” management style, acknowledging that his behavior had at times negatively affected employees. While Huang maintained that the intensity he brings to his job elevates those around him, he conceded that he sometimes takes things too far.

“It disempowers them, and that’s not right,” Huang said. The Nvidia CEO said he needed to figure out how to maintain his level of dedication to the company without hurting the people who work there.

“I need to hold myself to a higher standard at this time,” Huang said. He said that he would undergo a 360-degree performance review, soliciting feedback as often as monthly from people who work with him. The CEO added that he would be the first and only person at Nvidia to undergo such a review and that he did not want to institute the practice companywide.

Huang acknowledged during the meeting that Trump was a vocal supporter of Nvidia but insisted that politics played no role in how the company operated. “It is absolutely true that President Trump really, really loves our company,” Huang said. “He is my president. And I’ll do everything I can to help this president.”

Huang said he would take the same approach with future US presidents. “We are the most apolitical company,” he said. “I don’t love that we talk about politics here. I love that we talk about work here.”

Huang also sought to reassure women at Nvidia, describing himself as “one of our greatest advocates for creating an environment for women” and touting the company’s flexible work policies, which he said allow employees to simultaneously build their families and careers.

“If I did anything that put that in jeopardy, I cannot tell you how sad I am, and I’ll do everything I can to fix it,” Huang said.

After Huang’s resignation, he and the company’s board received criticism from Wall Street for not having an obvious internal successor in line. Huang addressed the issue head-on during the company meeting. “I don't believe in succession planning; I think it’s flawed,” he said, specifying that the concept may make sense but the implementation often causes issues.

Huang described each of his 60 reports as CEOs of their own units who could step up when he eventually leaves. “It creates resilience, and it creates the opportunity, which is ultimately the responsibility of the board, to give them the opportunity to decide at that time what the world needed, what the company needed, and be able to choose from a large collection of amazing people,” he said.

The CEO also argued that his brief absence had demonstrated Nvidia’s ability to operate without him. “I promise you, whether I’m here or not, this company is wired up to execute, execute, and execute,” Huang said.
