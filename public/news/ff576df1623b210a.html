AI data centre boom piles pressure on stressed US defence sector

✍️ By Christian Davies

Eric Fanning, CEO of the Aerospace Industries Association, stated that hyperscale data center construction is draining critical resources, including labor, energy, and manufacturing components. This resource competition has exacerbated supply chain strain, severely impacting the production capacity of defense contractors like Boeing amidst urgent military and commercial demand.

📰 Full In-Depth Coverage

Financial Times Full Report

The US aerospace and defence sector is struggling to secure scarce labour and manufacturing resources as America’s AI data centre boom heaps pressure on the nation’s creaking industrial base.

“Hyperscaling is sucking all the oxygen out of the room — whether it’s workforce, land, energy, and raising the costs on supplies,” said Eric Fanning, chief executive of the US Aerospace Industries Association, referring to the aggressive data centre investment. “It’s become a real competitive issue for our industry.”

Fanning, whose organisation represents leading defence and aerospace contractors and their suppliers, said tech companies had a “bottomless pit of money” that had intensified the strain on an industry already under pressure from surging air travel and military spending.

Boeing has a record backlog of more than 6,300 unfulfilled orders for commercial aircraft, while leading US defence groups are struggling to meet the Pentagon’s demands to increase munitions production amid conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East.

Boeing’s chief executive Kelly Ortberg admitted last month that the US aerospace giant’s ambitious recovery plan had fallen at least a year behind schedule, citing rising costs, problems with wing production and a series of supply chain issues.

Fanning said aerospace and defence companies had for decades relied on single suppliers for thousands of different components in order to reduce costs.

Now, however, those suppliers are trying to serve booming commercial and military demand while wrestling with soaring inflation and skills shortages exacerbated by the Covid-19 pandemic and, more recently, by the AI data centre build-out.

Andrew Liby, of engineering and construction firm BRPH, noted that even aerospace and defence companies investing in massive new plants had been squeezed out by data centre builders amid a fierce battle for contractors and suppliers of crucial factory infrastructure ranging from steel to electronic switches and climate control systems.

Despite revenues of almost $1tn in 2025, the US aerospace and defence sector’s low production volumes gave it little purchasing power, Fanning added, while “reshoring” production of critical inputs to the US would push costs up even more.

“We don’t buy in the quantities that consumer electronics do, or data centres do, or the automotive industry does,” he said. “We don’t make the same volume of things that can drive markets.”

He noted the example of memory chips, the price of which has soared in recent years due to AI-related demand, and how cutting-edge technology has siphoned production capacity from the legacy chips used in aerospace and defence.

Ron Epstein, an aerospace and defence analyst at Bank of America, said hyperscalers were “drawing on a lot of the same supply chain” as aerospace and defence companies.“It’s not just the obvious stuff like turbine blades and castings,” he said. “There’s the non-obvious stuff like printed circuit boards and electronic components as well.”

Epstein also argued, however, that Ortberg’s original plan to increase Boeing’s rate of production of 737 jets by five planes a month every six months had never been realistic. “That [schedule] was just insane.”

Asked for comment, Boeing referred the FT to Fanning’s comments. Scott Mikus, an analyst at Melius Research, said Boeing’s new, more conservative production schedule illustrated why its suppliers were reluctant to invest in extra capacity.

“If the manufacturers put out production targets that are too optimistic, some suppliers may just second guess them and say, ‘Boeing and Airbus have missed their past targets . . . therefore I’m not going to hire as many people to support that production ramp or order as much material,’” said Mikus.

Kevin Matthies, president of global hardware at Texas-based aerospace and defence component distributor Incora, said the sector had “underestimated the pressure” on smaller suppliers and the difficulty of investing “ahead of demand”.

Many industry observers argue, however, that while mounting backlogs are frustrating for the manufacturers’ customers — including global airlines desperate for more fuel-efficient planes — they are not necessarily a problem for the industry itself.

“You never get in trouble making too few airplanes,” said Epstein. “You just get in trouble making too many.”

🤖 AI Radar Technical Deep-Dive

The data center boom has shifted from a pure computing race to an extractive competition for core industrial resources, revealing a zero-sum game between AI infrastructure and national defense readiness. Future policy may shift toward resource allocation priority for industrial sectors, forcing defense firms to accelerate vertical integration and supply chain autonomy.
